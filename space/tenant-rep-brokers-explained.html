When you walk into a building's marketing suite, everyone you meet works for the landlord. The listing agent's job is to fill the space at the best terms for the owner, which is entirely legitimate, but it means nobody in the room is paid to find you a better deal down the street. A tenant representative, usually called a tenant rep or, in the UK and Ireland, an acquiring agent, is the person on your side of the table.

This guide explains what a good tenant rep does, how they are typically paid in different parts of the world, where conflicts of interest creep in, and how to interview candidates so you hire someone who earns their fee. It is general information rather than legal advice. A broker negotiates the commercial terms; your lawyer still needs to review the lease itself.

What a tenant rep actually does

A full search runs through a predictable sequence, and a competent rep will lead each stage rather than simply forwarding listings.

  1. Requirements. Turning your headcount, hybrid pattern, budget and growth plans into a space brief. Our guide to how much office space you need covers the arithmetic.
  2. Market survey. Identifying every realistic option, including off-market space, upcoming vacancies, subleases and flexible offices, not just what is advertised.
  3. Tours and shortlisting. Organising viewings, flagging building issues (lift capacity, after-hours air conditioning, loading, accessibility) and arranging test fits.
  4. Proposals. Issuing a request for proposals to several landlords at once so they compete, then comparing the responses on a like-for-like basis.
  5. Financial analysis. Modelling total occupancy cost and net effective rent over the whole term, including rent-free periods, fit-out contributions, service charges or operating costs, and reinstatement.
  6. Negotiation. Agreeing the heads of terms or letter of intent, then supporting your lawyer through the lease drafting.
  7. After signing. Many reps stay involved through the fit-out, track your key dates (break notices, renewal windows, rent reviews) and start the next negotiation well before the lease runs out.

The most valuable of these is often the one tenants skip: keeping your current landlord in the conversation. A credible alternative is what makes a renewal negotiation work, and a good rep will tell you honestly when staying put is the best deal on the table.

How tenant reps are paid

Payment conventions vary by country, by market and sometimes by deal. The table below is orientation only. Confirm the arrangement in writing before the first viewing.

"The landlord pays" does not mean the service is free. The commission is a cost of the deal, and landlords build it into their economics. More importantly, it creates incentives you should understand. A commission calculated on total lease value rewards bigger and longer deals. If one landlord is paying more than another, the broker has a reason to prefer that building. None of this means a commission-paid broker will give you bad advice, but it does mean you should ask.

Two practical safeguards help. First, ask for a clause stating that if any landlord pays more than the agreed commission, the excess is credited to you, which makes the broker fee-neutral between buildings. Second, on larger deals some tenants negotiate a rebate of part of the commission, paid to them or applied to the fit-out. Neither is universal, but both are reasonable requests.

Conflicts of interest and dual agency

Many large brokerages run two businesses under one roof: agency leasing, where they act for landlords to fill buildings, and tenant representation. When your broker's firm also holds the leasing mandate for a building on your shortlist, that is dual agency. The firm owes duties to both sides of the same negotiation.

Dual agency is common and, in many jurisdictions, legal with proper disclosure and your written consent. Rules differ by country, state and province, so do not assume the protections are the same everywhere. Whatever the local law says, protect yourself:

  • At the interview, ask which buildings in your target area the firm leases on the landlord's behalf. Get the list in writing.
  • If a conflicted building makes your shortlist, require written disclosure before any confidential information about your budget or alternatives is shared.
  • Ask how the firm separates the teams. Separate people, separate files and a named person accountable for keeping them apart are the minimum.
  • Consider whether you are comfortable at all. Some tenants exclude conflicted buildings from the broker's scope and handle them separately, or choose a broker that does not take landlord mandates.

Smaller conflicts matter too. A broker who has just closed a deal with a landlord, or hopes to win that landlord's next mandate, may soften their approach. You cannot eliminate every relationship, but you can ask about them.

The representation agreement

Most tenant reps will ask you to sign an agreement before they start. Read it as carefully as the lease. The key terms are:

  • Exclusivity. Most agreements make the broker your exclusive representative for the search, which is reasonable, because they will not invest time otherwise.
  • Term. A defined period, often six to twelve months, with the right to end it on notice if the service is poor.
  • Scope. Which cities, property types and transactions are covered. Does it include a renewal with your current landlord, subleases, coworking, or a purchase?
  • Fees. Who pays, how much, and what happens if the landlord refuses to pay a commission.
  • Tail period. Most agreements entitle the broker to a fee if you lease a property they introduced within a set period after the agreement ends. Keep that period reasonable and limited to properties actually introduced, preferably listed in writing.

How to interview a tenant rep

Interview at least two or three candidates. Look for local depth in the specific downtown you are targeting, whether that is Manhattan, the City of London or downtown Toronto, and for experience with requirements your size. A broker who mostly handles whole-floor deals may not give a 15-person team much attention.

Questions worth asking:

  • How will you be paid on this deal, by whom, and on each type of option we might choose?
  • Which buildings in our target area does your firm lease for landlords?
  • Who exactly will work on our search day to day, and how many other searches are they running?
  • Walk us through a recent deal of similar size: what did the landlord first offer, and what did you achieve? (Anonymised is fine.)
  • How will you compare options with different rent-free periods, fit-out contributions and terms?
  • Would you include our current landlord in the process, and how?
  • What would you tell us if the best option was a flexible office or staying put?
  • Can we speak to two or three recent clients?

Pay attention to how candidates answer the money questions. A good rep explains their incentives plainly and without discomfort. Vagueness here is a warning sign.

What a good tenant rep negotiates

Headline rent is only one line in the deal, and frequently not the most valuable. A strong rep will work the whole package:

  • Incentives: rent-free months, a fit-out or tenant improvement allowance, and a rent-free period for the fit-out itself.
  • Escalations: fixed annual increases, indexation or rent review terms that do not compound against you.
  • Flexibility: break options, expansion rights on adjacent space, renewal options with defined rent, and the right to assign or sublet.
  • Operating costs: caps on service charges or operating expenses, exclusions for capital works, and audit rights.
  • Security: a smaller deposit, a bank guarantee instead of cash, or a guarantee that falls away after a period of on-time payments.
  • End of lease: limits on reinstatement and dilapidations so you are not paying to strip out a fit-out the next tenant would have kept.
  • The practical details: signage, parking, after-hours heating and cooling rates, access for fit-out before the lease starts, and remeasurement rights.

When you might not need one

If you are taking a handful of coworking desks on a monthly membership, the terms are standardised and a broker adds less value, although some will still help you compare operators. For anything with a multi-year commitment, a fit-out, or a personal guarantee, independent representation usually pays for itself, whether through better terms, avoided mistakes or simply the time it saves.
