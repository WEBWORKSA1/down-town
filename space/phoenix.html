In 30 seconds

  • Who it suits: law, government-facing, healthcare, education and creative firms that want light rail, the courts and the airport close by
  • The main office cores: the Central Avenue towers, the Warehouse District and the Midtown corridor
  • The one lease quirk: Phoenix taxes commercial rent through its city transaction privilege tax, usually passed on to tenants; the state does not tax commercial leases

Downtown Phoenix's commercial geography is compact. Office towers, City Hall, Maricopa County's offices, the federal courthouse and the Phoenix Convention Center sit within a few blocks of Central Avenue and the light rail hub on Washington and Jefferson streets, with the downtown arena and Chase Field at the core's south-east corner. The Warehouse District's brick buildings fill the blocks to the south, Arizona State University's downtown campus and the Phoenix Biomedical Campus spread north towards Roosevelt Row, and the state government's Capitol Mall lies to the west. Central Avenue then carries a second line of high-rises north through Midtown.

The market's character comes from the fact that downtown is only one of several business centres in a sprawling, fast-growing metro. Tempe, Scottsdale, Chandler and the Camelback Corridor all compete for the same tenants, and many large corporate users have chosen suburban campuses with free parking. Downtown's pitch is different: proximity to government and the courts, the university and medical schools, a light rail line that reaches Tempe and the airport, and an increasingly lively residential neighbourhood around the offices. The tenant base leans towards law and accounting firms, banks, utilities, public agencies, nonprofits, healthcare and education, with technology and design firms in the converted warehouses. Building stock ranges from 1970s and 1980s towers to a smaller number of newer buildings and mixed-use blocks, and landlords compete with concessions for tenants willing to commit.

Retail and restaurant corridors

Downtown Phoenix retail is driven by events, students and residents rather than shoppers on a high street. The blocks around the arena, Chase Field and the Convention Center, along Jefferson and Washington streets and the numbered streets between them, live and die by the event calendar: game nights, concerts and conventions can fill bars and restaurants, while quiet weeknights in summer can be very slow. Roosevelt Row's independent galleries, coffee shops, bars and boutiques depend on the growing residential population, ASU students and the First Friday art walk; Grand Avenue suits makers, galleries and small venues that need affordable, characterful space. Along Central Avenue, light rail station areas in the core and Midtown support cafés, quick-service food and convenience retail aimed at office workers.

Footfall follows the seasons more than in most US downtowns. October to April is high season, with patio dining, festivals and convention business; from June to September, daytime foot traffic thins sharply and shifts into the evening. Tenants that thrive tend to be destinations, such as restaurants, bars, galleries, fitness studios and service businesses with loyal local customers, rather than browse-in retail. Shade, misting systems, patio heaters and robust air conditioning are part of the business plan here, not extras.

Coworking and flexible space

Flexible workspace downtown clusters in three places: upper floors of the Central Avenue towers, converted buildings in the Warehouse District and mixed-use blocks near the ASU campus, with further options in Midtown and many more in Tempe and Scottsdale. Typical offerings are monthly memberships for hot desks or dedicated desks, private offices for teams of one to a dozen or so, and meeting rooms by the hour; most include internet, furniture and utilities, while parking is often an extra charge. Terms are usually month to month or a few months at a time, with longer commitments earning discounts. Coworking suits startups, university spin-outs, satellite teams of out-of-state companies testing the Phoenix market and anyone who wants a downtown address without signing a multi-year lease or guaranteeing it personally.

How commercial leases work here

Phoenix follows US conventions. Rent is quoted per rentable square foot per year, and rentable area includes a share of the building's common areas (the load factor), so compare usable space carefully. Multi-tenant office buildings usually quote full-service gross rent, in which the landlord pays operating expenses and property taxes, but with a base year or expense stop: you pay your share of increases above the first year's costs. Retail and industrial space is normally triple net (NNN), with the tenant paying base rent plus a share of property taxes, insurance and common-area maintenance. Modified gross deals sit between the two. Leases usually include fixed annual escalations, and landlords offer tenant-improvement (TI) allowances and months of free rent in exchange for longer terms and stronger credit.

Two Arizona specifics matter. First, rental tax: the State of Arizona does not tax commercial leasing, but Phoenix and many other Arizona cities levy a transaction privilege tax (TPT) on income from commercial rentals. The landlord is legally responsible, but most leases pass it through to the tenant, and the city counts more than base rent as taxable, including reimbursements for property tax, insurance and common-area costs. Ask how it will be billed and confirm the treatment with an accountant. Second, personal guarantees: small and new businesses are commonly asked for one, and because Arizona is a community property state, landlords often ask the guarantor's spouse to sign or consent so the guarantee can reach shared marital assets. Take legal advice before either of you signs.

Getting your team there

Valley Metro's light rail is downtown's commuting advantage. The A Line links the core with Tempe, Arizona State University's main campus and Mesa, and the B Line runs north–south along Central Avenue through Midtown; the two meet at the Downtown Phoenix Hub, a short walk from the main towers. Sky Harbor International Airport is minutes away by car, or by light rail and the free PHX Sky Train, a real advantage for teams that travel. Buses fill in the grid. Most Valley commuters still drive, using I-10, I-17, Loop 202 and SR 51, so parking matters: towers typically have structured garages with monthly permits charged on top of rent, while the Warehouse District and Roosevelt Row rely more on surface lots and street parking. Covered or shaded spaces are valued in summer. Cycling and walking work from nearby neighbourhoods for much of the year, though staff will want showers and secure bike storage.

What to check before you sign

  • After-hours cooling. Confirm standard HVAC hours, the hourly charge for after-hours air conditioning and whether the system copes with July afternoons and dense seating.
  • Rental tax. See how the lease handles the city transaction privilege tax, whether on rent only or on expense reimbursements too, and what happens if the rate changes.
  • Guarantee and spousal consent. If a personal guarantee is required, negotiate a cap or a burn-off, and understand what a spouse's signature commits.
  • Parking. Number of spaces, covered versus open, monthly cost and visitor validation; event-night pricing near the arena and Chase Field affects staff and clients.
  • Monsoon readiness. Ask about roof condition, drainage and any history of water intrusion, and check FEMA flood maps for ground-floor units.
  • Event-day access. Street closures and crowds around games, concerts and conventions can block loading and deliveries.
  • Permits for food and retail. Restaurants need Maricopa County environmental health permits, grease interceptors and city licences; historic buildings may need preservation review for signs and alterations.

Hosting events downtown

The Phoenix Convention Center, with three halls spread across several downtown blocks, anchors the meetings market and is surrounded by large hotels with ballrooms and breakout rooms. For something with more character, the historic Orpheum Theatre, the Herberger Theater Center and Symphony Hall handle performances and keynote-style events, Heritage Square's courtyards and restored houses suit receptions, and Warehouse District buildings and Roosevelt Row galleries host product launches and parties. The arena and Chase Field offer suites and club spaces for corporate hospitality. Seasonality is everything: the busy season runs from roughly October to April, when rooftops and patios are at their best and demand is highest; summer brings softer rates, but outdoor events only after dark, if at all. Events in parks or on public streets need City of Phoenix special event permits, which take time to secure, so plan ahead.

Is downtown the right base?

Downtown Phoenix makes the most sense for firms that need to be near government, the courts, the university or the biomedical campus, that recruit young staff who want to live in an urban neighbourhood, or that value the light rail and a short hop to Sky Harbor. If your workforce lives in the south-east Valley, Tempe offers a lakefront office market and ASU's main campus on the same light rail line; Scottsdale and the Camelback Corridor carry more prestige and executive appeal; and Chandler suits technology firms near the semiconductor cluster. Midtown is the value alternative on Central Avenue. Many companies split the difference with a downtown or Tempe hub and flexible space elsewhere.
