In 30 seconds

  • Who it suits: healthcare companies, music and entertainment, finance, professional services and corporate operations teams
  • The main office cores: the CBD around Church and Commerce streets, SoBro, the Gulch and Midtown
  • The one lease quirk to know: tourism and big events shape daily life downtown, so check event-day access, loading and noise as carefully as the rent

Downtown Nashville's commercial geography follows its hill. The central business district's towers sit on the ridge between the State Capitol and Broadway, along Church and Commerce streets and the numbered avenues, with state government offices clustered around Capitol Hill. South of Broadway, SoBro has filled with new office, hotel and residential towers around the Music City Center, and the Gulch, a former rail yard to the south-west, has become a dense mixed-use district. Midtown and Music Row continue the office belt towards Vanderbilt University, and across the Cumberland River, the East Bank is being redeveloped around a new stadium.

Nashville's market has grown fast on the back of corporate relocations and expansions, a national cluster of healthcare-management companies, finance and insurance, and the music industry, whose labels, publishers and studios occupy converted houses and low-rise offices on Music Row. New towers in SoBro and the Gulch have set the top of the market, while some older CBD buildings have been renovated or converted to hotels and apartments. The tourism economy is unusually visible: hotels, bars and event venues compete with offices for sites and set the rhythm of the streets, which is an asset for recruiting and a challenge for logistics.

Retail and restaurant corridors

Lower Broadway is one of the most intense retail strips in the South, but it is a specialised one: multi-level honky-tonks with rooftop decks, boot and souvenir shops and bars that trade from late morning to the early hours, with weekends far busier than weekdays and huge spikes around concerts, football games and festivals. Second Avenue and Printers Alley share the same tourist and nightlife trade. The Gulch's boutiques and restaurants serve both residents and visitors, the Fifth Avenue galleries and the Arcade draw crowds for a monthly art crawl, and Germantown's restaurants rely on locals and food-focused visitors. Midtown's bars depend on students and young professionals.

Licensing has two layers: beer is permitted by the Metro Beer Permit Board, while liquor by the drink is licensed by the Tennessee Alcoholic Beverage Commission. On Broadway and Second Avenue, historic overlays mean facade changes, signage and rooftop additions need design approval. Coworking and flexible space

Flexible operators cluster in the Gulch, SoBro, the CBD's towers, Midtown and East Nashville. The Gulch and SoBro spaces lean corporate and polished, while East Nashville's are smaller and more community-driven, popular with musicians' managers, designers and freelancers. Typical options are hot desks and dedicated desks on monthly memberships, private offices on agreements of a few months to a year, meeting rooms by the hour and virtual-office addresses. Companies relocating to Nashville often start in flexible space while a permanent office is fitted out. Run the numbers with our coworking versus lease calculator.

How commercial leases work here

Tennessee follows standard American conventions. Office rent is quoted per rentable square foot per year, including a load factor for common areas. Multi-tenant towers usually quote full-service gross rent with a base-year expense stop, so you pay your share of increases in operating costs and property taxes above the first year; some landlords quote a net rent plus estimated expenses, and retail and industrial space is generally triple-net. In new buildings, landlords compete for tenants with tenant-improvement allowances and free-rent periods, both of which grow with term length and tenant strength.

Small and young companies should expect to offer a personal guarantee or a larger security deposit, and larger tenants a letter of credit. Tennessee has no personal income tax, which helps recruitment, but most businesses pay state franchise and excise taxes, and a business tax on gross receipts, so take tax advice before choosing an entity and a location. Getting your team there

Nashville is a driving city, and most staff commute by car from across Davidson County and from the fast-growing counties around it via I-40, I-65 and I-24, which converge around downtown and congest at rush hour. WeGo Public Transit's bus routes meet at WeGo Central, a short walk from the CBD and SoBro, and the WeGo Star commuter train brings weekday riders in from Lebanon to Riverfront Station. A voter-approved transit plan from 2024 is set to improve bus frequency, sidewalks and signals over the coming years. Monthly garage parking is widely available but costs more close to Broadway, and Nashville BCycle and the greenways serve cyclists. The CBD and SoBro are easiest for bus riders, and the Gulch and Midtown for drivers using I-40 and I-65.

What to check before you sign

  • Event-day access. Ask how CMA Fest, football games, New Year's Eve and parades affect street closures, deliveries, staff parking and client visits.
  • Noise. Visit at night and at the weekend; amplified music from Broadway, Second Avenue and rooftop bars carries, so check glazing and sound insulation.
  • Historic overlays and zoning. Broadway and Second Avenue fall under historic overlays, and the Downtown Code sets rules for uses, heights and signs; confirm what approvals your plans need.
  • The base year and pass-throughs. Make sure the base year is grossed up to full occupancy, ask how property reappraisals will affect your share, and secure audit rights.
  • Construction next door. Cranes are everywhere; ask what is planned on neighbouring sites, and how it could affect views, noise and access during your term.
  • Parking. Confirm the ratio, monthly rates and increases, and whether event pricing affects your staff or visitors.
  • Storm and flood resilience. Middle Tennessee gets severe storms and occasional floods near the Cumberland; ask about backup power, shelter areas and flood history.

Hosting events downtown

The Music City Center handles large conventions, and hotel ballrooms across SoBro, the Gulch and the CBD take most corporate meetings and dinners. Nashville's music venues are its best event spaces: the Ryman Auditorium, the Schermerhorn Symphony Center, the Country Music Hall of Fame's event rooms and the Frist Art Museum host private events, and honky-tonk floors and rooftop bars can be booked for receptions and after-parties. Outdoor events on the riverfront or in city parks need permits from Metro Nashville, and the Bicentennial Mall, a state park, has its own permitting. Spring and autumn are peak season, CMA Fest and football weekends fill hotels, summer events work best in the evening, and the holiday season is busy with corporate parties, so book early.

Is downtown the right base?

Downtown Nashville offers a showcase address, walkable hotels and nightlife that help with recruitment and client entertaining, and the best access to WeGo's network. Midtown is the better fit for healthcare companies tied to the hospitals and universities, Music Row for the music industry, and Brentwood and Cool Springs for headquarters whose staff live in Williamson County and want easy parking. If your business benefits from energy and visibility, and you can manage event-day logistics, downtown is the strongest choice; read the downtown Nashville guide for the wider picture.
