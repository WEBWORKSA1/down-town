Most first-time tenants negotiate one number: the rent. Landlords know this, and often prefer it. A building's value is closely tied to the headline rents recorded on its leases, so many owners will hold the rate firm while giving away considerably more value through rent-free months, fit-out money and flexible terms that appear nowhere in the headline. If you only push on the rate, you leave those other levers untouched.

This guide walks through the terms worth negotiating, how each works in different markets and how to run the process. How much you can win depends on local supply, your covenant strength (how financially secure you look as a tenant) and the size and length of your commitment. It is general information, not legal advice; have a lawyer review the heads of terms before you agree them and the lease before you sign.

Headline rent versus net effective rent

To compare offers fairly, convert every proposal into net effective rent: the average annual rent you actually pay after incentives. A simple illustration in round units rather than any currency:

  • Building A: headline rent of 100 a year, five-year lease, six months rent-free. You pay 4.5 years of rent, or 450, over five years. Net effective rent is 90 a year.
  • Building B: headline rent of 95 a year, five-year lease, no rent-free. You pay 475 over five years. Net effective rent is 95 a year.

Building A looks more expensive but is cheaper. Add fit-out contributions, service charges or operating costs, and any break penalty, and the gap can move either way. The coworking vs lease calculator helps you put total occupancy cost on one line, and a good tenant rep will model every offer on the same basis.

Incentives: rent-free and fit-out money

Rent-free periods

Rent-free (often called free rent or rent abatement in North America) is the most common incentive. Two points matter as much as the length. First, when it starts: ideally once you have access to fit out, so you are not paying while builders are on site. Second, what it covers: some leases waive base rent but still charge service charges or operating costs during the free period. Ask for both to be covered, or at least know which applies. In some markets the rent-free is spread across the term rather than taken at the start; that can help cash flow later, but you lose the benefit if you exercise an early break.

Fit-out or tenant improvement allowance

Landlords commonly contribute to fitting out the space, as a cash allowance, a capital contribution or work carried out by the landlord. North American leases call this a tenant improvement (TI) allowance, usually expressed per square foot. Negotiate the amount, whether unspent allowance can be used for furniture, cabling, moving costs or as rent credit, and when it is paid. Payment on completion against invoices is common; staged payments help your cash flow. In the UK, landlords increasingly offer space already fitted to a basic office standard (Category A) or fully fitted and furnished, with a different rent; compare those on total cost, not rent alone.

Escalations and rent reviews

How the rent changes during the lease can matter more than where it starts. Conventions differ widely.

  • US and Canada: fixed annual increases (a set amount or percentage each year) are common. Negotiate the size of the step and whether it applies to the full rent or only the base rent.
  • UK and Ireland: leases traditionally include rent reviews, often every five years, to market rent, sometimes on an upward-only basis. Indexed reviews linked to inflation, with a cap and collar, are also used. Push for caps, and check the assumptions the review uses.
  • Continental Europe: indexation to a national inflation or cost index is widespread. Ask which index, how often it is applied and whether a cap is available.
  • Australia: fixed annual increases or inflation-linked reviews are typical, with market reviews at option periods.
  • Hong Kong and Singapore: rent is commonly fixed for a relatively short term, often around three years, and renegotiated at renewal, so the renewal option terms carry more weight.

Flexibility: breaks, renewals and expansion

For a growing company, flexibility is often worth more than a small rent saving.

  • Break options let you end the lease early on notice. Landlords usually want something in exchange: a penalty, repayment of unamortised incentives and fees, or a smaller rent-free. Watch the conditions. In the UK and Ireland, breaks conditional on full compliance with lease obligations are risky; aim for conditions limited to rent paid up to date and giving up occupation.
  • Renewal options give you the right to extend. Define how the renewal rent is set (a fixed increase, or market rent with a cap) and diarise the exercise window. In England and Wales, statutory security of tenure under the Landlord and Tenant Act 1954 may also apply, unless the lease is contracted out.
  • Expansion rights, such as a right of first offer or first refusal on adjacent space, let you grow without moving.
  • Assignment and subletting rights, with consent not to be unreasonably withheld, give you an exit if the business changes. Our guide to subleasing office space explains how that works from the other side.

Service charges and operating costs

In most markets you pay a share of the building's running costs on top of rent: service charges in the UK, Ireland and much of Europe and Asia-Pacific; operating expenses, common area maintenance or additional rent in North America; outgoings in Australia. These can rise faster than rent and are easy to overlook.

  • Ask for the last few years of actual costs, not just the current budget.
  • Negotiate a cap on annual increases in the costs the landlord controls, such as management, cleaning and security. Taxes, insurance and utilities are usually excluded from caps.
  • Exclude capital improvements that mainly benefit the landlord, the costs of leasing other space, and landlord financing costs.
  • Cap the management fee, and secure audit rights over the year-end reconciliation.
  • In a full-service gross lease in the US, check the base year or expense stop: a base year with unusually low occupancy can inflate your later pass-throughs.
  • In the UK, ask whether the landlord follows the RICS professional statement on service charges in commercial property, which sets expectations for transparency.

Deposits, guarantees and the practical details

Deposits. Deposit size reflects how risky the landlord thinks you are. Ask for a smaller deposit, a reduction after a period of on-time payments, or a bank guarantee or letter of credit instead of cash. Norms vary: deposits in Japan are commonly six to twelve months' rent for offices, and security deposits in India are often substantial, so check local practice through our Tokyo and Mumbai pages. Personal guarantees from founders should be limited in amount and time.

Signage. Lobby directory, door, floor and, for larger tenants, exterior signage. Agree what is permitted and who pays. Exterior naming rights are usually only available to major tenants.

Parking. Number of spaces, reserved or unreserved, cost and whether it is fixed for the term, plus visitor parking, bike storage, showers and electric vehicle charging. Downtown parking is often scarce; secure it in the lease rather than by side letter.

After-hours services. Heating and cooling outside standard hours can be charged hourly. Agree the rate and the standard hours, which matter if your team works late or across time zones.

Measurement. Confirm the measurement standard and your right to remeasure. Our guide to how much office space you need explains usable and rentable area.

Reinstatement. Agree up front which alterations you will not have to remove at the end. It is one of the largest exit costs and one of the easiest to negotiate before you sign.

How to run the negotiation

  1. Start early. Twelve to eighteen months before your current lease ends gives you time for a real search and keeps your current landlord honest.
  2. Create competition. Shortlist at least three viable buildings, including your current one, and ask each for a written proposal on the same brief.
  3. Compare on one basis. Net effective rent and total occupancy cost over the term, not headline rent.
  4. Trade, do not just ask. A longer term, a stronger guarantee or a quick decision are worth something to the landlord. Use them to buy the levers you value most.
  5. Lock it into heads of terms. The letter of intent or heads of terms is usually not binding, but it frames the lease. Put every important point in it; reopening terms later is much harder.
