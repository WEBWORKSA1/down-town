In 30 seconds

  • Who it suits: European headquarters, tech, creative agencies and visitor-facing retail
  • The main office cores: Zuidas, the canal ring, Sloterdijk, the Amstel station area and the IJ banks in Noord
  • The one lease quirk to know: shops and restaurants get statutory protection that offices do not

Amsterdam's commercial geography splits into two worlds. The historic centre — the medieval core and the UNESCO-listed canal ring — is full of small offices in converted merchant houses, along with the city's busiest shopping streets. The big floors are elsewhere: Zuidas in the south has grown into the country's leading business district, with Sloterdijk to the west, the Amstel station area to the east and new mixed-use quarters along the IJ in Noord.

Demand comes from European and regional headquarters, financial and legal firms, tech companies and a large creative sector, supported by an international workforce and an airport with direct flights across the world. The canal ring offers character and prestige but small, vertical floors and heritage restrictions; newer districts offer efficient floor plates, high energy standards and direct rail links, which large tenants increasingly require.

Where the offices are

ZuidasThe main business district around Amsterdam Zuid station: banks, law and accounting firms, corporate headquarters, modern towers and fast trains to Schiphol. The first choice for large teams and international firms.
Canal Ring and Old CentreOffices in canal houses and converted warehouses for agencies, investment boutiques, consultancies and start-ups that want an address with character. Floors are small and stairs steep.
SloterdijkA western business district built around a major rail interchange, with larger floors at lower cost and ongoing conversion into a mixed neighbourhood.
Amstel station areaOffice buildings in the east near Amstel station, with metro and train links, popular with firms that need space and easy commuting from the south and east.
Noord and the IJ banksFormer shipyards and new waterfront buildings across the IJ, home to media, creative and tech companies, linked by free ferries and the north–south metro.

Retail and restaurant corridors

The Kalverstraat and Nieuwendijk carry some of the heaviest footfall in the country, drawing international chains and visitors from morning to evening, seven days a week. The Leidsestraat links the centre to the Leidseplein's restaurants and theatres. For independents, the Nine Streets and the Haarlemmerstraat and Haarlemmerdijk mix fashion, design, cafés and food shops, while the Utrechtsestraat leans to restaurants. Luxury brands gather on the P.C. Hooftstraat near the museums. Tourist traffic keeps the centre busy all week and on Sundays, when central shops can open; locals shop more on Saturdays and in neighbourhoods such as De Pijp and Oost. The city has curbed new shops aimed mainly at tourists in parts of the centre, so a concept that works elsewhere may not be permitted here.

Coworking and flexible space

Amsterdam has one of Europe's busiest flexible-office scenes. Operators fill canal houses and former warehouses in the centre, modern floors in Zuidas, creative buildings in Noord and refurbished blocks in Oost. Memberships typically run monthly, from hot desks and fixed desks to private offices for small teams, with meeting rooms booked by the hour. Flexible space suits foreign companies registering a first Dutch entity, start-ups and teams that expect to grow fast. Check whether the operator can provide a registered address for the Chamber of Commerce.

How commercial leases work here

Dutch commercial leases are usually drafted on standard ROZ model contracts, published by the property sector, with separate models for offices and for retail and hospitality. Offices and other business space fall under a lighter regime with wide freedom of contract: five-year terms with renewal periods are common, and tenants have limited protection when the landlord ends the lease. Shops, restaurants and other premises open to the public — known as '290 premises' after the relevant article of the Civil Code — get statutory protection: a default term of five years plus five, after which the lease continues indefinitely, termination by the landlord only on legal grounds, and the right to ask the court to review rent against comparable premises.

Rent is quoted per square metre per year, excluding VAT, with service costs paid as monthly advances and settled annually. Rents are usually indexed each year to the consumer price index. Rent is VAT-exempt by default, but landlord and tenant can opt for a VAT-taxed lease if the tenant uses the space mainly for VAT-able activities.

Getting your team there

Most staff arrive by train, metro, tram or bike. Amsterdam Zuid, Sloterdijk, Amstel and Central Station are all on the national rail network, and Zuid and Sloterdijk have quick direct trains to Schiphol. The north–south metro line links Noord, the old centre, De Pijp and Zuidas, while trams cover the canal ring. Secure bike parking is essential: ask how many spaces come with the lease and whether there are showers. Car parking is scarce and expensive in the centre, so visitors are best directed to public transport or Park and Ride sites on the edge of the city.

What to check before you sign

  • Confirm the permitted use under the municipal zoning plan, especially for shops, restaurants and any change from office use.
  • Check the energy label: with limited exceptions, offices without at least label C can no longer be used as offices.
  • For canal houses, check monument status, which limits changes to façades, interiors and installations.
  • Ask about foundations and quay walls in older buildings; some canals and bridges carry weight limits for delivery vehicles.
  • Check delivery windows and loading access in the centre, where heavy vehicles are restricted.
  • For restaurants and bars, factor in operating and alcohol permits, terrace permissions and noise rules.
  • Review which ROZ model applies and any deviations from it, plus indexation and the service-cost budget.

Hosting events downtown

The city's main convention centre sits beside Zuidas, and downtown has a deep stock of hotel ballrooms, historic churches and former warehouses, galleries, rooftop terraces and boats for canal receptions. Noord adds large industrial spaces for launches and festivals. Spring and summer are busiest, and hotels fill for big events such as King's Day, Pride in early August and Amsterdam Dance Event in October. Events in public space need a permit from the city, with rules on noise, crowds and waste, so apply well ahead.

Is downtown the right base?

For client-facing firms that want an address with character, a canal-house office is hard to beat, and the centre suits retail and hospitality that live on visitors. Large teams and corporate headquarters usually do better in Zuidas, with modern floors and direct trains to Schiphol, while Sloterdijk and the Amstel station area offer more space for the money. Creative and tech companies often choose Noord or Oost. Read our downtown Amsterdam guide to get a feel for the neighbourhoods before you shortlist buildings.
