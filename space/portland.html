In 30 seconds

  • Who it suits: creative agencies, design and apparel firms, professional services and life-sciences teams that want a walkable, transit-rich base
  • The main office cores: the CBD, the Pearl District, the Lloyd District, the Central Eastside and South Waterfront
  • The one lease quirk: no sales tax on rent, but a stack of city, county, regional and transit business taxes to budget for before you compare buildings

Downtown Portland's office core fills a compact grid of short blocks on the west bank of the Willamette, with the tallest towers along the transit mall on 5th and 6th avenues and around the Park Blocks, a few streets from City Hall, the county and federal courthouses and Pioneer Courthouse Square. North of Burnside Street, Old Town's historic brick and cast-iron buildings give way to the Pearl District's converted warehouses and newer mid-rise offices. Across the river, the Lloyd District offers larger floor plates near the Oregon Convention Center and the Rose Quarter, and the Central Eastside mixes warehouses, makers and creative offices. To the south, South Waterfront has grown up around Oregon Health & Science University.

Since 2020, downtown Portland has been a tenants' market. Hybrid work, corporate downsizing and concerns about street conditions have left far more empty and sublease space than before the pandemic, and older towers in particular are competing hard with concessions, upgrades and, in some cases, conversion plans. Demand is led by law and accounting firms, financial services, government agencies, healthcare, architecture and engineering practices, and the creative and apparel-design businesses the city is known for. The best-located, best-managed buildings, near MAX and with good amenities and committed owners, have held up far better than the rest, so quality varies widely from block to block.

Where the offices are

Central business districtClass A towers along the transit mall and around the Park Blocks, plus older mid-rise buildings. Law, finance, government and corporate regional offices, with every MAX line within a few blocks. Best for firms that want a classic address and the shortest commute from across the region.
Pearl DistrictBrick warehouse conversions and newer mid-rise offices with ground-floor shops, on the streetcar. Design, advertising, architecture and tech firms; good for recruiting staff who want restaurants and galleries nearby.
Old TownHistoric buildings with character and lower rents, but older systems and seismic questions. Suits creative firms and nonprofits willing to trade polish for price; visit the surrounding blocks at different times of day.
Lloyd DistrictLarger late-20th-century office buildings and newer mixed-use blocks east of the river, near the convention centre and on several MAX lines. Good value and easier parking for bigger teams.
Central EastsideA historic industrial district where warehouses house creative offices, food producers and makers. Zoning protects industrial uses, so check what is allowed; ideal for companies that make things as well as design them.
South WaterfrontNewer towers and labs around OHSU, linked by streetcar, the aerial tram and Tilikum Crossing. The natural home for health, biotech and research tenants.

Retail and restaurant corridors

Portland's strongest retail streets are not all downtown. Within the centre, the Pearl District's grid around NW 10th to 13th avenues mixes design stores, galleries, boutiques and restaurants with a resident population that keeps it busy at weekends, and NW 23rd Avenue, just beyond, is one of the city's most established neighbourhood shopping streets. The traditional retail core around Pioneer Courthouse Square has department stores and national chains but has lost tenants since 2020 and leans heavily on office workers and visitors. Old Town is a nightlife and food area, with the Saturday Market drawing weekend crowds to the riverfront. Across the river, the main streets of the east side, including Hawthorne, Division, Alberta, Mississippi and the Central Eastside, are where many of the city's most talked-about restaurants, bars and shops have opened.

Downtown footfall follows the office week, with quieter Fridays and weekends, and everywhere it follows the seasons: dry summers bring visitors, patios and festival crowds to the waterfront, while the long wet season favours cosy restaurants, takeaway and delivery. Destination food and drink, independent fashion and design, outdoor gear and service businesses do well; generic chain retail struggles outside the busiest blocks. Landlords with empty ground floors are often open to pop-ups and creative short-term deals.

Coworking and flexible space

Flexible space is plentiful. Operators cluster in CBD towers, Pearl District conversions, Central Eastside warehouses and the Lloyd District, ranging from polished business centres to creative studios and maker spaces with shared workshops. Typical terms are monthly memberships for hot desks or dedicated desks, private offices for small teams and meeting rooms by the hour, usually including internet, furniture and utilities; car parking is extra and limited, but bike storage is standard. Because many companies have shed space since 2020, subleases are another route: often furnished, available for a few years and priced below direct leases, though with less control over your relationship with the building. Flexible space suits startups, remote-first companies that need a hub a few days a week and out-of-state firms testing the market.

How commercial leases work here

Portland follows US norms. Rent is quoted per rentable square foot per year; rentable area includes a share of common areas, so ask for the load factor and compare usable space. Downtown office space is commonly quoted on a full-service basis, with the landlord paying operating expenses and property taxes but passing on increases above a base year. Retail and industrial space is usually triple net (NNN), with the tenant paying a share of taxes, insurance and common-area maintenance on top of base rent. Annual escalations are standard. In the current market, tenants with good credit can negotiate meaningful tenant-improvement allowances, months of free rent and flexibility such as expansion or early-termination options, especially on longer terms.

Small and new businesses are routinely asked for a personal guarantee; negotiate a cap, a declining amount or a release after a period of on-time payments. Oregon has no sales tax, so nothing is added to rent, but budget for the local tax stack: City of Portland and Multnomah County business income taxes, filed together through the city's Revenue Division; the regional Metro business income tax that funds homeless services, which applies to larger businesses; the TriMet transit payroll tax paid by employers; and, above a revenue threshold, Oregon's Corporate Activity Tax. Rules and thresholds change, so confirm your position with an accountant.

Getting your team there

Downtown is the hub of the region's transit network. All five MAX light rail lines run through the core, connecting the west-side suburbs, Gresham, Milwaukie, North Portland and the airport, and the transit mall on 5th and 6th avenues brings buses in from across the city. The Portland Streetcar links the Pearl District, downtown, Portland State and South Waterfront, and loops through the Lloyd District and Central Eastside. C-TRAN buses connect with Vancouver, Washington. Cycling is a mainstream commute, so expect staff to ask for secure bike storage, showers and lockers. Car parking downtown is limited and costly, with monthly permits in garages; the Lloyd District and Central Eastside are easier for drivers. Larger employers in the Portland area must also follow Oregon's Employee Commute Options rules, which require them to offer incentives that reduce drive-alone trips.

What to check before you sign

  • Seismic risk. The region faces the risk of a major Cascadia earthquake, and many older buildings, especially unreinforced masonry in Old Town, have not been fully retrofitted. Ask for a seismic assessment and who would pay for upgrades.
  • Cleaning and security. Ask what the landlord spends on cleaning, lighting and on-site security, how after-hours access works and what is passed through as operating expenses.
  • Historic districts. Skidmore/Old Town, New Chinatown/Japantown and the 13th Avenue district in the Pearl have design review for signs, storefronts and alterations.
  • East-side zoning. The Central Eastside's industrial protections limit some office and retail uses; confirm your use is allowed before committing.
  • Energy reporting. Portland requires larger commercial buildings to report their energy performance; ask for the results and any planned upgrades that could flow into expenses.
  • Cooling. Many older towers were designed for milder summers; check cooling capacity for heatwaves and the hourly charge for after-hours HVAC.
  • Licences. Restaurants need Multnomah County environmental health licences, a grease interceptor and, for alcohol, an Oregon Liquor and Cannabis Commission licence.

Hosting events downtown

The Oregon Convention Center in the Lloyd District, with its twin glass spires, handles large conventions and trade shows and sits on several MAX lines. Downtown hotels offer ballrooms and meeting rooms, while the publicly owned Portland'5 venues, including the Arlene Schnitzer Concert Hall and Keller Auditorium, host performances and large presentations. For character, look at converted warehouses and galleries in the Pearl District and Central Eastside, historic halls in Old Town, rooftop terraces with views of Mount Hood and gardens such as Lan Su, which can be booked for private events. Pioneer Courthouse Square and Tom McCall Waterfront Park host public festivals and large gatherings; both require permits, and the waterfront calendar fills early for summer weekends. Seasonality is pronounced: the dry months from July to September are ideal for outdoor events, while from October to May you should plan for rain and an indoor back-up.

Is downtown the right base?

Downtown Portland is the right base for firms that want the region's best transit access, proximity to the courts and government, and a central location for staff spread across Oregon and south-west Washington, and today it offers tenants unusually strong bargaining power. The Pearl District suits creative firms that want neighbourhood character; the Lloyd District offers value and parking; the Central Eastside fits makers and design studios; and South Waterfront is the life-sciences address. Many technology and apparel companies are in Beaverton and Hillsboro, close to the big employers of the west side, while Lake Oswego and the Kruse Way corridor attract professional firms whose staff live in the southern suburbs. Vancouver, Washington, across the Columbia, has no state income tax for residents, but Oregon still taxes income earned in Oregon, so weigh that carefully.
