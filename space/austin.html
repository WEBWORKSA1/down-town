In 30 seconds

  • Who it suits: tech and venture-backed companies, law and public-affairs firms near the Capitol, and music, events and hospitality businesses
  • The main office cores: Congress Avenue, the lakefront and Second Street, Seaholm and the eastern edge near Rainey Street
  • The one lease quirk to know: quoting conventions vary between full-service gross and base rent plus expenses, so always compare the all-in cost

Downtown Austin's commercial core is compact: a grid running from Lady Bird Lake north up Congress Avenue to the Texas State Capitol, bounded by Shoal Creek on the west and I-35 on the east. Congress Avenue's towers hold law firms, banks and public-affairs firms drawn by the Capitol; the lakefront and the Second Street District have the newest high-rise offices; Seaholm, on the west side, mixes offices with shops and residential towers around a converted power plant; and the eastern edge near Rainey Street and the Waterloo Greenway has added more new towers. North of the core, the University of Texas and its medical school anchor an innovation district.

Austin's market has been shaped by a long tech boom. A wave of new towers transformed the skyline in the 2010s and 2020s, so much of the downtown stock is modern, with efficient floorplates, outdoor terraces and lake views. When large tech employers pulled back, sublease space increased and landlords became more flexible. State government, the university, legal and lobbying work, and the music and events economy give the market a broader base than its tech reputation suggests, and Austin's growth means demand comes as much from companies relocating or expanding as from local startups.

Retail and restaurant corridors

Downtown retail divides by time of day. Congress Avenue and the Second Street District mix restaurants, cafés, boutiques and services that serve office workers by day and residents and visitors in the evening, and Seaholm adds grocery and everyday shopping on the west side. Sixth Street and Rainey Street are almost entirely bars, busiest late at night and at weekends, while Red River Street is the core of the live-music venue scene. Across the river, South Congress's boutiques, vintage shops and restaurants depend on visitors and weekend crowds, and East Sixth Street and East Austin mix bars, cafés and independent shops. Festival weeks such as SXSW bring enormous short-term demand for pop-ups and brand activations; see our guide to pop-up shops.

Bars and restaurants need alcohol permits from the Texas Alcoholic Beverage Commission and city health permits, and amplified outdoor music requires a city permit with sound limits, an important point as new residential towers rise next to established venues. Coworking and flexible space

Flexible offices are common in Austin, reflecting its startup culture and the number of remote and hybrid workers. Operators cluster in the CBD's towers, East Austin and North Austin, and some landlords offer move-in-ready spec suites and sublease space on short terms. Typical options are hot desks and dedicated desks on monthly memberships, private offices on agreements of a few months to a year, meeting rooms by the hour and virtual-office addresses. Flexible space suits early-stage companies, satellite teams of out-of-state firms and anyone who wants to test Austin before committing to a lease. How commercial leases work here

Office rent is quoted per rentable square foot per year, including a load factor for common areas. In Austin, quoting conventions vary: some landlords quote full-service gross rent with a base-year expense stop, while many quote a base rent plus estimated operating expenses and property taxes on a triple-net basis, and some gross quotes exclude electricity, which is then billed separately. Retail and industrial space is almost always triple-net. Ask every landlord for an all-in figure on the same basis, and check whether electricity is included.

Concessions have been generous in recent years, especially in buildings with vacant floors: tenant-improvement allowances per square foot, months of free rent and occasionally turnkey fit-outs, with more on offer for longer terms. Sublease space from tech companies can offer furnished, modern floors at a discount, but terms are short and need the landlord's consent; read our sublease guide. Small or young companies are often asked for a personal guarantee, a larger deposit or a letter of credit.

Getting your team there

Most Austin staff drive, using I-35, MoPac and the region's toll roads, and I-35 through central Austin is in the middle of a years-long rebuild, so expect delays on the east side of downtown. CapMetro's MetroRail Red Line brings commuters from the northern suburbs to its downtown station near the convention centre site, and MetroRapid buses run frequent services along the main corridors. The voter-approved Project Connect plan includes future light rail. Garages are plentiful, with monthly contracts, and many staff cycle or scoot along the lake trail and bike lanes, so ask about bike storage and showers. Seaholm is easiest for MoPac drivers, the eastern side of the CBD for MetroRail riders, and the Congress Avenue core for bus commuters.

What to check before you sign

  • The all-in cost. Convert every offer to the same basis, including operating expenses, taxes, electricity and parking, and ask for audit rights and expense caps.
  • Capitol View Corridors and height limits. If you plan rooftop signage, terraces or additions, confirm whether the building sits in a protected corridor.
  • Sublease terms. Check the remaining term, the landlord's consent, the condition of furniture and what happens if the sublandlord defaults.
  • After-hours cooling. Austin's long summers make after-hours HVAC charges a real cost for teams that work late or at weekends.
  • Event-week disruption. SXSW, ACL, race weekend and University of Texas home games bring street closures and parking spikes; plan staff access and deliveries.
  • Construction nearby. I-35 works, the convention centre rebuild and new towers can affect access, noise and dust; ask what is planned around your building.
  • Property-tax protests. Texas landlords routinely protest appraisals; ask whether refunds flow back to tenants who paid through operating expenses.

Hosting events downtown

The Austin Convention Center is closed for a full rebuild and is expected to reopen later this decade, so large conferences are spreading across hotel ballrooms, the city-owned Palmer Events Center across the lake and other venues. The big downtown hotels handle most corporate events, the Long Center for the Performing Arts and the historic Paramount Theatre suit presentations and receptions, and music venues, rooftop terraces and lakeside restaurants are popular for launches and parties. Events in Republic Square, Waterloo Park and other public spaces need permits from the city, as do street closures. Spring and autumn are peak season; SXSW, ACL and race weekend fill hotels and push up venue costs, summer events work best indoors or after sunset, and spring storms call for a wet-weather plan.

Is downtown the right base?

Downtown Austin offers the city's most prestigious addresses, modern towers, the Capitol and the lake trail on the doorstep, and the best access to restaurants and live music for recruiting and client entertaining. North Austin's tech cluster is closer to where many tech workers live and offers more parking, East Austin suits creative firms that want character and value, and the Loop 360 corridor appeals to firms that want hill-country campuses near the western suburbs. If visibility, amenities and walkability matter, downtown is the right base; read the downtown Austin guide to see the area beyond the office.
