In 30 seconds

  • Who it suits: tech, financial services, funds, law and professional firms that want an EU base with an English-speaking workforce
  • The main office cores: the Georgian Dublin 2 core, the north Docklands and Grand Canal Dock
  • The one lease quirk to know: statutory renewal rights after five years' occupation shape lease lengths, so watch for four-year-and-nine-month terms and renunciations

Dublin's commercial centre is compact and follows the river. South of the Liffey, the Georgian streets of Dublin 2 around St Stephen's Green, Merrion Square, Fitzwilliam Square and Baggot Street form the traditional office core, with retail on Grafton Street and hospitality around Temple Bar and George's Street. East toward the port, the Docklands have grown on both quays: the financial services centre on the north bank and Grand Canal Dock, the city's tech cluster, on the south. North of the river, the area around O'Connell Street and Henry Street is the main shopping district, and new offices have been built around the north quays, Heuston and the Liberties.

The market is driven by international firms that use Dublin as their European headquarters, especially in technology, financial services, funds, aircraft leasing and professional services, alongside a large domestic legal, accountancy and public sector. That gives the city two very different kinds of stock. Modern, energy-efficient Docklands and Dublin 2 buildings with large floors command a clear premium, while protected Georgian townhouses offer charm and prestige but small, awkward floorplates and weaker energy performance. Demand has concentrated on the best buildings as firms adjust to hybrid working, so the gap between prime and secondary space has widened.

Where the offices are

Dublin 2: the Georgian coreLaw firms, accountants, banks, government departments and embassies in Georgian terraces and modern blocks around St Stephen's Green, Merrion Square and Harcourt Street. On the Luas Green line and near Pearse DART. The prestige choice for professional and client-facing firms.
North DocklandsThe financial services centre and Spencer Dock: funds, banks, insurers and aircraft-leasing firms in purpose-built offices along the north quays, on the Luas Red line and near Connolly. Good for larger floors with modern specification.
Grand Canal DockThe "Silicon Docks" tech cluster around the canal basin and Sir John Rogerson's Quay, with big tech campuses, newer towers and plenty of cafés. Served by the Grand Canal Dock DART station.
BallsbridgeA leafy Dublin 4 district of embassies, professional firms and mid-sized office buildings just beyond the Grand Canal, near the RDS. Calmer than the core and on the DART.
The north inner cityOffices around O'Connell Street, Abbey Street and the north quays, with good Luas access and more competitive rents than the south side. Worth a look for growing teams.
Heuston and the LibertiesNewer schemes on the western edge of the centre, near Heuston station and the Luas Red line, suited to firms that want modern space at a lower cost than Dublin 2.

Retail and restaurant corridors

Grafton Street and Henry Street are Dublin's two prime shopping streets: Grafton Street for fashion and flagships on the south side, Henry Street and Mary Street for busier mass-market retail on the north side. Footfall peaks on Saturdays and through the Christmas season. South William Street, Drury Street and the lanes around George's Street suit independent fashion, cafés and restaurants, Temple Bar trades heavily on tourists and evenings, and Camden Street and Wexford Street are the late-night strip. Capel Street, pedestrianised in recent years, has become a corridor of independent and international restaurants. Changes of use generally need planning permission from Dublin City Council, which also has shopfront and signage guidance, and the protected status of many buildings limits what can be done to frontages. Alcohol licensing is restrictive: a full pub licence has traditionally had to be bought from an existing licence holder, which carries a significant premium.

Coworking and flexible space

Dublin has a deep flexible-office market, fed by overseas companies setting up an Irish entity before committing to a lease. Operators cluster around St Stephen's Green and Baggot Street, Grand Canal Dock, the north quays and Smithfield, from hot-desking floors to serviced suites in Georgian townhouses. Expect hot desks and dedicated desks on rolling monthly agreements and private offices on terms of a few months to a year, with commercial rates, service charge, utilities and internet bundled into one fee. Many centres also provide a registered office address and meeting rooms, useful while you recruit your first local staff. For teams with uncertain headcount, flexible space usually beats signing a long lease and funding a fit-out.

How commercial leases work here

Irish commercial leases are freely negotiated, but two statutory rules shape them. First, under the Landlord and Tenant (Amendment) Act 1980, as amended, a business tenant who has been in occupation for five years can acquire a right to a new lease, so landlords of smaller units often grant terms of just under five years, typically four years and nine months, or ask the tenant to sign a deed renouncing those rights after receiving independent legal advice. Second, upward-only rent review clauses are prohibited in leases entered into since early 2010, so reviews in newer leases can move rent down as well as up. Large office lettings commonly run for 10 to 15 years or more on full repairing and insuring terms, with rent reviews every five years and sometimes a tenant break option. Stamp duty is payable by the tenant on the grant of a new lease, calculated on the average annual rent and the term, and must be filed with Revenue.

Typical office lease10–15 years or more for large floors, with five-yearly rent reviews; under five years (often four years and nine months) for small units
Rent is quotedPer square foot per year for offices, sometimes per square metre; usually paid quarterly in advance
Building costsFull repairing and insuring terms are common; a service charge covers common areas and building services in multi-let buildings, plus building insurance
DepositVaries with covenant strength; a rent deposit of several months, a bank guarantee or a parent-company guarantee is common for new or overseas entities
Fit-outPrime buildings are usually let in Category A condition; tenants fund their own fit-out, often supported by a rent-free period on longer leases
TaxesThe occupier pays commercial rates to Dublin City Council; VAT at the standard rate (currently 23%) applies to rent where the landlord has opted to tax; the tenant pays stamp duty on a new lease

Conventions vary by building and landlord. Treat this as orientation, not legal advice, and have a local lawyer review any lease.

Getting your team there

The DART runs along the coast through Connolly, Tara Street, Pearse and Grand Canal Dock, putting the Docklands and Dublin 2 within reach of coastal suburbs north and south. The Luas Red line serves the north quays, the north Docklands, Connolly and Heuston, and the Green line runs through O'Connell Street, Trinity and St Stephen's Green to the southern suburbs. Commuter trains arrive at Heuston and Connolly, and the bus network, being redesigned under BusConnects, covers the rest. Protected cycle lanes are expanding, and Ireland's tax-saving Cycle to Work scheme makes cycling popular with staff, so bike storage and showers matter. Parking in the centre is scarce and expensive, so plan for staff to use public transport. The Docklands have the quickest road link to the airport via the Dublin Port Tunnel.

What to check before you sign

  • Renewal rights. Check whether the lease is under five years, whether you are being asked to sign a renunciation, and what that means for your long-term security.
  • Building Energy Rating. A BER certificate is required when commercial property is let; ask what the rating means for running costs and whether upgrades are planned.
  • Protected structures. Many Georgian buildings are on the Record of Protected Structures, so internal alterations, signage and services work may need planning permission and specialist contractors.
  • Fire and access certificates. Material alterations and changes of use can require a Fire Safety Certificate and a Disability Access Certificate before work starts; build that into your timeline.
  • Commercial rates. Check the property's valuation with Tailte Éireann and the council's annual rate so you can budget the full cost of occupation.
  • VAT and service charges. Confirm whether the landlord has opted to tax the letting, and ask for the service charge budget and past accounts.
  • Planning use. Make sure the unit's permitted use matches your business, especially for restaurants, takeaways and late-night venues.

Hosting events downtown

The purpose-built convention centre on the north quays handles large conferences, while the RDS in Ballsbridge hosts exhibitions and big gatherings. Dublin Castle's historic rooms, the Mansion House and the Royal Hospital Kilmainham suit receptions with a sense of occasion, and Georgian townhouses, galleries, hotel ballrooms and converted warehouses in the Docklands cover smaller launches and dinners. Rooftop and outdoor events are possible in summer, but always need a wet-weather plan. Demand peaks during the Christmas party season and around major rugby internationals and concerts, when hotel rooms are scarce. Outdoor events on public space need permission from Dublin City Council, and large events have their own licensing process, so start early.

Is downtown the right base?

For international firms, client-facing professionals and anyone hiring young, highly mobile talent, a city-centre address in Dublin 2 or the Docklands is the default, and the transport links and amenities justify the premium. The alternatives are suburban business parks such as Sandyford, Cherrywood, Citywest and Park West, which offer more parking and lower costs, though only some are on the Luas and most rely heavily on the car. Many firms keep a central hub for collaboration and client meetings and place larger operational teams further out. For neighbourhoods, transport and daily life, read the downtown Dublin guide.
