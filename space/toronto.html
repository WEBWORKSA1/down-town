In 30 seconds

  • Who it suits: finance, law, tech and professional-services firms that draw staff from the whole region via Union Station
  • The main office cores: the Financial District and South Core, with King West, Liberty Village and the Downtown East for character space
  • The one lease quirk to know: net rent is only part of the bill — additional rent (TMI) and 13% HST sit on top

Toronto's commercial heart is the Financial District at King and Bay, where the major banks, the stock exchange and the country's largest law and accounting firms fill a dense cluster of towers. From there the office market spills south across the rail corridor into South Core and the waterfront, west into the converted warehouses of King West and Liberty Village, east towards St. Lawrence and the Downtown East, and north along University Avenue's hospitals and campuses. Union Station is the anchor — subway, GO trains, the airport train and VIA Rail meet there — and the PATH network ties dozens of towers to it underground.

The tenant base is broad: banks, insurers and pension funds, legal and consulting firms, government agencies, and a large technology, media and life-sciences sector. Building stock ranges from mid-century modernist towers to glass towers completed in the last decade and heritage brick-and-beam lofts. Since the shift to hybrid work, demand has concentrated on the newest, best-connected and best-equipped buildings, while older towers carry more vacancy and more room to negotiate — a split that rewards tenants who shop carefully.

Where the offices are

Financial DistrictBank headquarters, law firms and trophy towers around King and Bay, connected by the PATH to Union, King, St. Andrew and Queen stations. Top floors command a premium; older towers offer value. Best for firms that need prestige and client access.
South Core and the WaterfrontNewer towers between Union Station and Queens Quay, with large, efficient floorplates and modern building systems, linked to Union and the PATH. Popular with large tenants consolidating several offices into one.
King West and Liberty VillageConverted warehouses and newer mid-rise offices west of Spadina, home to tech, media and agencies. Character and amenities are strong; transit depends on the King streetcar and Exhibition GO station.
Downtown East and St. LawrenceHeritage buildings and mid-rise offices east of Yonge, near King and Queen stations, with new Ontario Line stations planned nearby. Suits professional services and creative firms wanting character at a discount to Bay Street.
Discovery District and University AvenueHospitals, research institutes and the University of Toronto, with research-adjacent and lab-capable space along subway Line 1. The natural base for life sciences and health technology.
Bloor-YorkvilleMid-sized towers at the junction of subway Lines 1 and 2, on the northern edge of downtown. A good fit for wealth managers, consultancies and firms that want a quieter address with excellent transit.

Retail and restaurant corridors

Yonge Street carries the heaviest pedestrian traffic, especially around Sankofa Square and the large shopping centre between Queen and Dundas stations, and suits flagships, quick-service food and high-volume retail. Queen West is the fashion and independent-brand street, strongest on weekends. King West and the Entertainment District are dinner, theatre and nightlife territory, peaking in the evenings and around film festival and game nights. Bloor-Yorkville is the luxury corridor, drawing tourists and high-income shoppers. The PATH is its own market: food courts, services and convenience retail that do most of their trade between 11 and 2 on weekdays and are largely closed at weekends. St. Lawrence and the Distillery District lean on tourists, weekend visitors and seasonal markets, while Kensington Market and Chinatown favour small independent food and vintage shops. In summer, the City's CaféTO programme lets restaurants add sidewalk and curb-lane patios with a permit.

Coworking and flexible space

Flexible workspace is plentiful downtown. Operators cluster in Financial District towers and around Union Station, where they serve satellite teams and visiting staff, and in the converted buildings of King West and Liberty Village, where they serve startups and creative firms. Typical options are hot desks and dedicated desks on monthly memberships, and private offices for two to twenty people on terms of a few months to a year, with internet, furniture, meeting rooms and reception included. Many landlords now offer furnished, move-in-ready suites on short leases, which can bridge the gap between coworking and a conventional lease. For teams expecting to grow, compare the per-person cost of flex space with a small direct lease over a two- or three-year horizon.

How commercial leases work here

Commercial leases in Ontario are largely a matter of contract, with the province's Commercial Tenancies Act in the background. Most office and retail leases are net: you pay base rent per square foot per year on the rentable area, which includes a share of the building's common areas measured under industry standards, plus additional rent for your proportionate share of operating costs and realty taxes. Landlords estimate additional rent each year, bill it monthly and reconcile it after year-end, so budgets need a buffer. Landlord remedies are strong: under Ontario law a landlord can re-enter and change the locks once rent is more than 15 days in arrears, without a court order, and small companies are routinely asked for personal guarantees.

Negotiation happens around inducements — free-rent periods, tenant-improvement allowances and landlord's work — and around renewal and expansion rights, assignment and subletting, and caps on controllable operating costs. In older buildings earmarked for redevelopment, watch for demolition or relocation clauses that let the landlord end or move your lease early.

Typical office lease5–10 years; 3–5 for small suites; 10–15 or more for large floors, often with renewal options
Rent is quotedNet, per square foot per year, on rentable area
Building costsAdditional rent (TMI): operating costs, realty taxes and insurance, often plus a management fee; estimated monthly and reconciled annually
DepositCommonly first and last month's rent; more, a letter of credit or a personal guarantee for newer companies
Fit-outUsually the tenant's work, part-funded by a landlord tenant-improvement allowance; model suites for small spaces
Taxes13% HST on base rent and additional rent (registered businesses can usually claim it back); realty taxes flow through as additional rent

Conventions vary by building and landlord. Treat this as orientation, not legal advice, and have a local lawyer review any lease.

Getting your team there

Union Station is the region's main transit hub: GO Transit trains and buses bring commuters from across the Greater Toronto and Hamilton Area, the UP Express runs to Pearson Airport, and VIA Rail connects to Ottawa and Montréal. The TTC's Line 1 forms a U through downtown, Line 2 runs along Bloor, and streetcars serve King, Queen, Spadina and the waterfront; integrated fares make a TTC ride free when combined with GO using PRESTO or contactless payment. The Ontario Line, under construction, will add new stations through the core. Bike Share Toronto stations are everywhere, and many towers offer secure bike rooms and showers. Driving is the hard option: monthly tower parking is costly, and the Gardiner Expressway and Don Valley Parkway are congested. The Financial District and South Core are the easiest to reach; Liberty Village and the eastern waterfront depend more on streetcars and buses. For short-haul clients, Billy Bishop airport is minutes from the core.

What to check before you sign

  • PATH access: whether the building connects, at which level, and whether the link is open in the evenings and at weekends.
  • After-hours HVAC and building hours: how much the landlord charges for heating or cooling outside standard hours, which matters for hybrid and late-working teams.
  • Additional rent history: ask for several years of operating-cost and tax figures, what counts as a capital cost, and whether controllable costs are capped.
  • Heritage status: designated buildings and heritage conservation districts, such as parts of St. Lawrence and Queen Street West, limit alterations, storefront changes and signage.
  • Signage: the City's sign by-law and the landlord's sign criteria both apply, and permits are needed for most exterior signs.
  • Use and permits for food and retail: confirm zoning, change-of-use and building permits, ventilation, grease interceptors and barrier-free access before committing.
  • Nearby construction: Ontario Line works, Gardiner reconstruction and new towers can disrupt access, noise and footfall for years.

Hosting events downtown

Toronto has a deep events market. The Metro Toronto Convention Centre, beside the CN Tower, handles large conferences and trade shows, and Exhibition Place in the west hosts consumer shows. Hotel ballrooms in the Financial District and around Union Station suit galas and corporate meetings, while the city's museums and galleries, the historic St. Lawrence Hall, the Distillery District's industrial buildings and Harbourfront Centre's waterfront venues offer more character. King West rooftops and restaurant private rooms suit smaller gatherings. September (with the film festival and conference season) and November–December (holiday parties) book up early; summer favours patios and outdoor venues. Events in public squares and parks need a City of Toronto special event permit, road closures and noise exemptions are applied for separately, and serving alcohol at a one-off event requires a special occasion permit from the AGCO.

Is downtown the right base?

For most firms that compete for talent, serve clients face to face or rely on GO Transit commuters, downtown is hard to beat: no other part of the region matches its transit, amenities and prestige. The alternatives each solve a specific problem. Midtown at Yonge and Eglinton and North York Centre offer subway access at lower rents, Vaughan Metropolitan Centre sits at the end of Line 1 with easier parking, and the office parks near Pearson Airport and in Mississauga and Markham suit car-commuting teams, logistics and firms with heavy travel schedules. If your people live across the region and your clients are on Bay Street, start your search downtown. For the wider picture of living and visiting, see the downtown Toronto guide.
