In 30 seconds

  • Who it suits: corporate and regional headquarters, finance, law, healthcare and creative firms
  • The main office cores: the skyway-linked towers around Nicollet Mall and Marquette Avenue, Downtown East and the North Loop
  • The one lease quirk: rent is commonly quoted net, with operating expenses and real estate taxes on top, so add them before you compare

Downtown Minneapolis's commercial core is a grid of towers on the west bank of the Mississippi, centred on Nicollet Mall and the parallel Marquette Avenue and 2nd Avenue South, and stitched together by a skyway network linking around 80 blocks at second-floor level. City Hall and the Hennepin County Government Center stand on the core's eastern side, the Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis faces the river, and the Minneapolis Convention Center anchors the southern end. Downtown East has grown up around U.S. Bank Stadium and the Commons park, the North Loop's warehouses and new timber-frame buildings sit north-west of the core by Target Field, and the Mill District lines the riverfront by St. Anthony Falls.

The market's backbone is corporate. The Twin Cities have an unusually deep bench of large headquarters for a metro of their size, and several downtown towers are anchored by big employers in retail, banking, energy and financial services, alongside law and accounting firms, consultancies, advertising agencies and nonprofits. Since 2020, hybrid work and corporate consolidation have put more space back on the market, much of it as sublease, and the gap has widened between well-equipped, skyway-connected towers and older buildings that need investment. The North Loop's brick-and-timber offices remain popular with creative and tech firms, and landlords across the core are offering concessions to tenants willing to commit.

Retail and restaurant corridors

Downtown Minneapolis has two retail worlds. The skyway level is a weekday economy of food counters, coffee bars, convenience stores, dry cleaners and services that serve office workers at lunchtime and close by early evening, with little weekend trade. At street level, Nicollet Mall is the traditional shopping spine, with national retailers, Orchestra Hall, restaurants and the route of the city's parades. The North Loop is the strongest street-level district, with independent fashion, design and homeware shops along Washington Avenue and 1st Avenue North and some of the city's best-known restaurants. Hennepin Avenue's theatre district carries evening trade, and the blocks around Target Field, Target Center and U.S. Bank Stadium surge on game and concert days.

Footfall depends on the office week and the weather. Weekday lunchtimes are busiest in the core, while evenings and weekends belong to the North Loop, Hennepin Avenue and event crowds. Summer brings patio season, festivals and riverfront visitors; winter pushes customers into the skyways and makes delivery and takeaway more important. Restaurants, bars, fitness studios, salons and destination retail do best, and businesses serving residents are growing with the downtown population. Restaurants and bars should budget for the additional local sales taxes on food and liquor that apply in the downtown taxing area.

Coworking and flexible space

Flexible operators cluster in the skyway-connected towers of the financial core, in North Loop warehouses and across the river in Northeast, where studios and creative workspaces fill former industrial buildings. Expect monthly memberships for hot desks or dedicated desks, private offices for small teams and meeting rooms by the hour, typically including internet, furniture, utilities and shared kitchens; parking in a nearby ramp is usually extra. In the core, skyway access is a selling point in winter, letting members walk to client meetings, lunch and their cars indoors. Flexible space suits startups, consultants, satellite teams of national companies and firms bridging a move, and commitments of six to twelve months usually unlock discounts.

How commercial leases work here

Minneapolis follows US conventions, with one local habit worth knowing. Rent is quoted per rentable square foot per year, but listings and landlords' proposals commonly show a net rent with estimated operating expenses (often called CAM) and real estate taxes listed separately, rather than a single full-service figure. Add them together for the true cost, and remember that Minnesota's commercial property taxes are a substantial part of that total: ask for several years of expense and tax history and how the annual reconciliation works. Retail and industrial space is usually triple net (NNN). Leases include annual escalations, and landlords offer tenant-improvement allowances and free rent in exchange for longer terms and stronger credit.

Small and young businesses should expect to provide a personal guarantee, and sometimes a larger deposit or a letter of credit; negotiate a cap or a burn-off after a period of good payment. Minnesota does not charge sales tax on commercial rent, so the main tax costs are the property taxes passed through by the landlord, plus the local sales taxes you collect from customers, including the extra taxes on restaurant food and liquor in the downtown taxing area. Assessments for the Downtown Improvement District, which funds cleaning, safety ambassadors and greening, may also appear in operating expenses. Confirm your obligations with a local accountant and lawyer.

Getting your team there

Downtown is the hub of the region's transit network. Metro Transit's Blue Line light rail runs from Target Field Station through the core to the airport and Bloomington, and the Green Line runs to the University of Minnesota and downtown St. Paul; both serve Downtown East. Rapid bus lines and frequent local buses use dedicated lanes on Marquette Avenue and 2nd Avenue South, and express buses bring commuters in from suburban park-and-ride lots. Most office towers connect to parking ramps through the skyways, so drivers can arrive in January without stepping outside; monthly contracts are typically billed separately from rent. Cycling is a serious commuting option in the warmer months, with protected lanes and off-street trails from the west and south, so ask about bike rooms and showers. The North Loop and Downtown East are best for rail commuters; the financial core is easiest for bus riders and drivers using the ramps.

What to check before you sign

  • Skyway connections and hours. Confirm which buildings and ramps your space connects to and the building's skyway hours, especially if you run a shop or expect evening visitors.
  • Operating expenses and taxes. Get several years of history for CAM and real estate taxes, check what is excluded and ask for a cap on controllable expenses.
  • Winter operations. Ask about snow removal, heated loading docks, entrance vestibules and heated or underground parking.
  • Energy benchmarking. Minneapolis requires larger commercial buildings to report their energy use; ask for the results and any planned upgrades that could flow into expenses.
  • Event-day access. Games and concerts at Target Field, Target Center and U.S. Bank Stadium affect traffic, parking prices and deliveries.
  • Historic review. Much of the North Loop lies in the Minneapolis Warehouse Historic District, where exterior changes and signs need heritage review.
  • Food and drink. Restaurants need city health and liquor licences, must collect the downtown food and liquor taxes and should check grease interceptor and ventilation capacity.

Hosting events downtown

The Minneapolis Convention Center, at the southern end of Nicollet Mall, is the anchor for conventions and large meetings, surrounded by skyway-connected hotels with ballrooms. For something more memorable, the historic theatres on Hennepin Avenue, the Guthrie Theater and Orchestra Hall can host performances and presentations; Mill City Museum and other riverfront buildings suit receptions with a view; and North Loop warehouses, galleries and rooftops handle launches and parties. Suites and clubs at Target Field and U.S. Bank Stadium serve corporate hospitality. Summer is the season for rooftops, riverboats and outdoor events; parks along the river and the lakes are run by the Minneapolis Park and Recreation Board, which issues permits separately from the city. In winter, skyway-connected venues are a strong selling point for out-of-town guests.

Is downtown the right base?

Downtown Minneapolis is the strongest choice for firms that want a central address, transit links from across the metro, proximity to big corporate clients, banks and government, and a winter-proof daily routine through the skyways. Creative and tech firms often prefer the North Loop or Northeast for character and a younger talent pool. Downtown St. Paul, a light rail ride away, offers state government, the Capitol and generally lower costs. Many large employers sit in the suburbs along the I-494 and I-394 corridors, in Bloomington, Edina, Eden Prairie, Minnetonka and Golden Valley, where parking is free and campuses are large, and medical-technology firms cluster there too. If your team lives in those suburbs, a downtown hub plus flexible space closer to home may be the right balance. Read the downtown Minneapolis guide to see what your team's day would look like.
