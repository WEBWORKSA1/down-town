In 30 seconds

  • Who it suits: law, finance, government-facing and creative firms that want a rail-connected, central address in a sprawling region
  • The main office cores: Bunker Hill and the Financial District for towers; the Historic Core and Arts District for character space
  • The one lease quirk to know: office rent is usually quoted per square foot per month, full-service gross — not per year

Downtown Los Angeles divides into distinct commercial belts. The high-rise office core runs along Figueroa, Flower, Hope and Grand through the Financial District and up Bunker Hill. East of it, the Historic Core's early-20th-century buildings along Broadway, Spring and Main mix upper-floor offices with street-level shops and restaurants. The Civic Center to the north is the seat of city and county government and a cluster of courthouses. The Arts District on the eastern edge offers converted warehouses and newer creative offices, South Park in the south-west is the convention and hospitality quarter, and the Fashion District to the south-east is a dense grid of wholesale showrooms.

Downtown is one office market among many in the region, competing with Century City, Santa Monica and the Westside, Pasadena, Burbank and Glendale, and the South Bay. That competition, together with hybrid working, has kept vacancy well above pre-2020 levels and given tenants real negotiating power, particularly in older towers. Newer and recently renovated buildings with strong amenities lease more easily than tired ones, and some older offices are being converted to housing, slowly shrinking supply. Demand is anchored by legal and financial services tied to the courts and government, by creative and media businesses, and by downtown's position at the centre of the rail network.

Where the offices are

Financial DistrictThe tallest towers, largest floorplates and most institutional tenants: law firms, banks, accountants and insurers. Seventh Street/Metro Center station serves four rail lines. Best for firms that want a corporate address and easy client access.
Bunker HillTowers and plazas along Grand Avenue and Hope Street, sharing the hill with apartment buildings and the big cultural institutions. A good fit for professional firms that want an amenity-rich setting a short walk from the courts.
Historic CoreFormer banks, department stores and office buildings with smaller floorplates, tall ceilings and big windows, now popular with creative agencies, tech start-ups and flexible operators. Pershing Square and Historic Broadway stations are close by.
Civic Center and Little TokyoCity, county, state and federal offices and courts, surrounded by law firms, nonprofits and consultancies. Two rail stations and Grand Park make this the obvious base for government-facing work.
Arts DistrictWarehouse conversions and new creative-office buildings with open floors and outdoor space, favoured by media, design, fashion and consumer brands. Transit is thinner, so check parking.
South Park and Fashion DistrictOffices tied to the convention centre, hotels and entertainment venues, plus wholesale showrooms and apparel businesses. Pico station on the A and E lines serves the area.

Retail and restaurant corridors

Broadway is the headline retail street, where flagship and concept stores have moved into restored theatres and department-store buildings; it draws shoppers and visitors at weekends. Spring and Main Streets hold restaurants and bars that serve the Historic Core's residents, and Grand Central Market anchors a busy food scene at the foot of Bunker Hill. Seventh Street is the Financial District's lunch and after-work strip, strongest from Tuesday to Thursday when office attendance peaks. The Arts District is a destination for dining, design and lifestyle brands, busiest on evenings and weekends. Little Tokyo and Chinatown support long-running independent shops and restaurants, the Fashion District trades in wholesale and discount retail, and South Park lives and dies by the convention and event calendar. Food and drink, experiential retail and services for the growing residential population — groceries, fitness, pet care — tend to do best; businesses that depend on five-day office crowds have found it harder.

Coworking and flexible space

Flexible workspace clusters in three places: the Historic Core, where operators fill characterful older buildings; the Arts District, which suits creative and consumer-brand teams; and the Financial District towers, where landlords increasingly run their own flexible floors alongside conventional leases. Terms are typically monthly or for a few months at a time, with a choice of hot desk, dedicated desk or private office, plus meeting-room credits and a business mailing address. Many tower landlords also offer spec suites — pre-built, sometimes furnished offices on shorter leases — which bridge the gap between coworking and a full build-out. For teams of a handful of people, compare the all-in monthly cost with a small sublease from a tenant that has downsized.

How commercial leases work here

Los Angeles follows Southern California conventions. Office rent is quoted per rentable square foot per month, usually on a full-service gross basis: base rent includes operating costs and property taxes up to a base year, after which you pay your share of any increases. Retail and industrial space is normally triple net (NNN), with property taxes, insurance and common-area maintenance billed on top of rent. Rentable area includes a share of lobbies and corridors (the load factor), so the space you can actually use is smaller than the quoted figure. Parking is usually charged separately at a set ratio of spaces per thousand square feet.

Two California specifics matter. Under Proposition 13, a property's assessed value rises only modestly each year until it changes hands; a sale triggers reassessment to market value, and because most leases pass property taxes through, a building sale can raise your costs mid-lease. Tenants often negotiate a clause limiting those pass-throughs. California also requires commercial landlords to state whether the premises have been inspected by a Certified Access Specialist (CASp), and since 2025 it has given very small businesses, restaurants and nonprofits extra protections on rent-increase notice and how operating costs are billed.

Getting your team there

Downtown is the hub of the region's rail network. Seventh Street/Metro Center connects the A, B, D and E lines, reaching Hollywood, Koreatown, Pasadena, Long Beach, Santa Monica and East Los Angeles. Union Station adds Metrolink commuter trains from Ventura, the San Fernando and Santa Clarita valleys, the Inland Empire and Orange County, as well as Amtrak and the J Line busway. DASH buses circulate downtown, and Metro Bike Share has docks throughout the core. The 110, 101, 10 and 5 freeways meet around downtown, which makes it accessible by car but congested at peak times; tower garages are plentiful but parking is a real cost, so check the parking ratio and monthly rates. The Financial District and Civic Center are the easiest to reach by transit; the Arts District is the hardest.

What to check before you sign

  • Seismic status. Los Angeles requires retrofits of non-ductile concrete and soft-story wood-frame buildings, and many Historic Core buildings are older masonry. Ask for the retrofit history and timeline, and whether compliance costs can be passed through as operating expenses.
  • Property-tax protection. Ask how a sale and Proposition 13 reassessment would affect your pass-throughs, and negotiate a cap or exclusion if you can.
  • Accessibility. Review the CASp disclosure and agree in writing who pays for accessibility upgrades; disability-access claims are common in California.
  • Historic designation. Buildings on Broadway and elsewhere may be designated monuments or sit in a historic district, so exterior changes and signage need review by the city's historic preservation staff.
  • Permitted use. Downtown zoning has been rewritten under a new community plan, and the Arts District has its own industrial and live/work rules. Confirm your use, and any change-of-use permits, with the Department of Building and Safety.
  • Food and drink. Restaurants need county health plan approval, grease interceptors and kitchen ventilation; full liquor licences are capped by county population and often bought from existing holders, while beer-and-wine licences are easier to obtain.
  • Building operations. Check after-hours air-conditioning charges, freight lift and loading access in older buildings, and the parking ratio for staff and visitors.

Hosting events downtown

The Los Angeles Convention Center in South Park handles the largest trade shows, with hotel ballrooms nearby. For something with more character, Broadway's restored theatres host screenings, galas and product launches, Union Station's halls and patios are popular for receptions, and the museums and concert venues of Grand Avenue rent spaces for private events. The Arts District's warehouses, galleries and courtyards suit brand activations, and downtown's rooftops make the most of the skyline at sunset. The calendar is busiest in the awards season from winter into early spring and through the holidays, and outdoor events are reliable for much of the year thanks to the dry climate. Events in public spaces such as Grand Park or Pershing Square, or on streets, need permits from the agency that manages them, and filming requires a film permit.

Is downtown the right base?

Downtown makes the strongest case for firms that need the courts and government, want a transit-connected office their staff can reach from across the region, or are looking for big floorplates and generous concessions. Entertainment and media companies often prefer Century City, Burbank or Culver City; tech firms gravitate to Santa Monica and Playa Vista; and Pasadena suits finance and engineering firms that want a smaller-city feel. If your team, clients or customers are spread across the basin, downtown's rail connections are hard to beat, and its mix of towers, lofts and warehouses offers more choice than any other district.
