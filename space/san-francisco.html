In 30 seconds

  • Who it suits: tech and AI companies, investors and professional firms that want to be close to the Bay Area's talent and capital
  • The main office cores: the Financial District and the Transbay towers, with SoMa, Jackson Square and Mission Bay for character and lab space
  • The one lease quirk to know: many leases pass a city tax on commercial rents through to tenants, on top of base rent

Market Street is the spine of commercial San Francisco. North of it, the Financial District's towers rise around Montgomery, California, Sansome and Battery Streets, shading into historic Jackson Square's low-rise brick blocks. South of Market, the Transbay district around the transit centre holds the city's newest and tallest towers, and SoMa beyond it is a grid of converted warehouses and mid-rise offices stretching toward Mission Bay's labs and medical campus. Union Square is the retail core to the west, Mid-Market and Civic Center continue down Market Street, and the Port of San Francisco leases offices and event space in the historic piers along the Embarcadero.

San Francisco's office market moves in sharp cycles, driven by technology. Since 2020, hybrid working and tech layoffs have pushed vacancy and sublease availability far above pre-pandemic levels, giving tenants unusual leverage. The recovery has been uneven: newer towers and buildings with views and outdoor space lease far better than tired older stock, and artificial intelligence companies have been among the most active tenants. Some older buildings have sold at steep discounts or are candidates for conversion to housing. That turnover matters to tenants, because how a building is valued and taxed feeds straight into the operating costs passed through under most leases.

Retail and restaurant corridors

Union Square remains the city's main shopping district, though it has lost several big flagships since 2020; vacant storefronts have opened the door to pop-ups, experiential retail and food concepts on more flexible terms. Market Street carries heavy foot traffic between the transit stations, and the Ferry Building is a destination food hall that draws locals and visitors every day of the week, especially for the Saturday farmers' market. In the Financial District, restaurants live on weekday office lunches and after-work drinks, busiest from Tuesday to Thursday and quiet at weekends. Chinatown's Grant Avenue and Stockton Street support independent grocers, bakeries and restaurants, North Beach's Columbus Avenue is a café and dining strip, and SoMa's food and drink scene follows conventions at the Moscone Center and residents in the new towers. Concepts that do not depend on five-day office attendance tend to fare best.

Coworking and flexible space

San Francisco has one of the deepest flexible-space markets in the country. Operators cluster in SoMa, the Financial District, Jackson Square and Mid-Market, with options from hot desks to multi-room private suites on monthly or short-term agreements, usually including meeting rooms, events and a business address. Start-ups also make heavy use of subleases: companies that have downsized often offer furnished, network-ready floors for the remainder of their term, which can be cheaper and faster than coworking for teams of a dozen or more. Landlords in the larger towers increasingly build spec suites — finished, move-in-ready offices on shorter leases — to compete for the same tenants.

How commercial leases work here

Unlike much of Southern California, San Francisco office rent is usually quoted per rentable square foot per year, on a full-service gross basis: operating costs and property taxes are included up to a base year, and you pay your share of later increases. Retail, lab and some smaller spaces are more often triple net (NNN) and may be quoted monthly, so confirm the basis of any figure. Rentable area includes a share of the building's common areas, and annual rent increases are fixed in the lease. In the current market, generous tenant improvement allowances and months of free rent are common for direct deals, especially on longer terms.

Several local and state rules shape the real cost. The city's commercial rents tax applies to landlords' receipts from commercial leases, and many leases pass it through to tenants as additional rent. Businesses register with the Treasurer and Tax Collector and may owe the city's gross receipts tax. Under California's Proposition 13, a building is reassessed when it changes hands, so a sale can push the property taxes you pay up — or, when a building sells below its previous value, down. California landlords must also disclose whether the premises have been inspected by a Certified Access Specialist (CASp).

Getting your team there

Downtown is the meeting point of the region's transit. BART runs under Market Street, with Embarcadero, Montgomery, Powell and Civic Center stations bringing commuters from the East Bay, the Peninsula and SFO. Caltrain's electric trains reach Fourth and King Streets from Silicon Valley and the Peninsula, and ferries from Marin, Oakland, Alameda, Vallejo and other bay towns arrive at the Ferry Building. The transit centre handles regional buses from the East Bay and Marin, and Muni Metro, the Central Subway and buses cover the city. Bike lanes and bike share are widespread. Parking is scarce and expensive, and many towers have little or none, so most employees arrive by transit. The Financial District and Transbay are the easiest to reach; Mission Bay depends on Caltrain and the T Third line.

What to check before you sign

  • Seismic and ground conditions. Much of the Financial District east of Montgomery Street, and parts of SoMa and Mission Bay, sits on fill over the old bay shoreline, in zones prone to liquefaction. Ask for the building's seismic evaluation and retrofit history, especially for older concrete buildings.
  • Tax pass-throughs. Confirm how the commercial rents tax, property-tax changes after a sale and any other city levies are passed through, and cap them where you can.
  • Sublease protections. For a sublease, get the master landlord's written consent and recognition so your occupancy survives if the sublandlord defaults, and check the remaining term.
  • Permits and planning. Change-of-use and building permits can take months. Chain retailers face extra review in many neighbourhood districts, and amplified music needs an Entertainment Commission permit.
  • Historic rules. Jackson Square and several downtown conservation districts restrict exterior changes and signage.
  • Accessibility. Review the CASp disclosure and agree who pays for any access upgrades required for your use.
  • Building operations and security. Ask about after-hours air-conditioning charges, weekend access, lobby security and how the building manages the street outside.

Hosting events downtown

The Moscone Center in SoMa hosts the city's largest conferences, and big conference weeks fill hotels and push up prices across downtown, so book early. Grand hotel ballrooms on Nob Hill and around Union Square suit galas and dinners, while the Ferry Building, the Embarcadero piers and the Exploratorium offer waterfront settings with bay views. City Hall's rotunda, the museums of Yerba Buena and Civic Center, rooftop terraces and SoMa's warehouse spaces round out the options. Plan outdoor events around the weather: summer can be foggy and windy, and autumn is usually the warmest season. Street closures go through the SFMTA, park events need Recreation and Park Department permits, and events with amplified sound may need an Entertainment Commission permit.

Is downtown the right base?

For a company recruiting across the whole Bay Area, downtown San Francisco is still the best-connected address, served by BART, Caltrain, ferries and regional buses, with today's market offering better terms than at any time in years. Firms that need lab space are better served in Mission Bay or South San Francisco, Silicon Valley remains the home of the big tech campuses, and downtown Oakland across the bay offers lower costs with direct BART access. If you want dense talent, client meetings on foot and a deep pool of furnished subleases, downtown wins. For the visitor's view of the neighbourhood, see the downtown San Francisco guide.
