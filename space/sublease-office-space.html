Every office market has companies holding more space than they need. They hired for growth that did not come, moved to hybrid working, merged with another business or relocated. Rather than pay for empty floors until their lease ends, many offer the surplus as a sublease: you rent the space from the existing tenant, not the building owner. For the right occupier that can mean a fitted, furnished office at a meaningful discount, on a term shorter than any landlord would offer.

It can also mean inheriting someone else's lease problems. This guide explains where the savings come from, what you take on, the risks if your sublandlord gets into trouble, and how to protect yourself. Subleasing law and terminology vary by country (in the UK and Ireland a sublease is usually called an underlease, and the head landlord's consent a licence to underlet), so treat this as orientation and have a lawyer review the documents.

Why subleases can be cheaper

  • A motivated seller. The sublandlord is paying for space it does not use. Any income reduces the loss, so it may accept less than its own rent, sometimes much less in a soft market.
  • Existing fit-out. Partitions, meeting rooms, kitchens and cabling are already built. Furniture and AV are often included. You avoid most of the upfront cost of a new fit-out.
  • Short, defined terms. The remaining term of the head lease may be one to five years, which suits companies that cannot commit to a long direct lease and have outgrown flexible space.
  • Speed. A ready-to-use floor can be occupied quickly once consent is in place.

Sublease supply tends to be highest in downtowns where large employers have reduced their footprint, and often includes floors in good-quality buildings that would be unaffordable on a direct lease. Our city pages, such as San Francisco, New York and London, describe the local office districts.

What you inherit

A sublease sits inside the head lease. You cannot have more rights than your sublandlord has, and the head landlord can still enforce the head lease rules on the building.

The term

Your sublease must end before, or at the latest when, the head lease ends. There is usually no renewal right you can use. If you want to stay longer, you will need a direct lease with the head landlord, which you should raise early.

The fit-out, as it is

You generally take the space in its current condition. Check that the layout genuinely works for you, that furniture is included by a written inventory, and who owns it. Changing the layout needs consent from both the sublandlord and the head landlord, and may add to your reinstatement obligations.

The head lease rules

Permitted use, hours, building rules, signage restrictions, alterations, insurance and service charges or operating costs all flow down from the head lease. The sublease normally requires you to comply with the head lease terms as they apply to your space, so you need to see them.

Landlord consent and its conditions

Most head leases require the landlord's written consent to a sublease. In England and Wales, where a lease says consent will not be unreasonably withheld, the landlord has a statutory duty to respond within a reasonable time. Elsewhere the position depends on the lease and local law. Some head leases also give the landlord the right to take back the space instead of consenting (a recapture right) or to share in any profit the sublandlord makes. Expect the consent to impose conditions, and to take time.

The main risk: if your sublandlord defaults

Your right to occupy depends on the head lease surviving. If the sublandlord stops paying its rent, breaches the head lease or becomes insolvent, the head landlord may be able to end the head lease, and in most legal systems your sublease ends with it. You could lose the space, your fit-out and the money you spent moving in, at short notice.

The degree of protection varies. In England and Wales, a subtenant can apply to the court for relief when a head lease is forfeited, but that is a legal process, not a guarantee. In the US and Canada, a sublease generally terminates with the master lease unless the head landlord has agreed otherwise. Insolvency adds further complexity everywhere. The practical conclusion is the same: check your sublandlord's financial health and lease compliance, and get as much direct protection from the head landlord as you can.

Other things can go wrong more quietly. The sublandlord may fail to pass on services it is responsible for, such as air conditioning outside normal hours, or may be slow to deal with repairs, because it is no longer focused on space it does not use. The sublease should spell out who provides what, and what remedies you have if they do not.

How to protect yourself

  • Read the head lease. Ask for a full copy. The sublandlord may redact the rent, but you need every other clause.
  • Get consent signed first. Do not move in, start works or pay more than a holding deposit until the head landlord's written consent is signed.
  • Ask for recognition or non-disturbance. A recognition or non-disturbance agreement is the head landlord's promise that, if the head lease ends early, it will let you stay on the sublease terms or on agreed direct terms. Landlords often resist, especially for small subleases, but it is more achievable for a large floor or a long remaining term, and it is the most valuable protection available.
  • Get an estoppel or status letter. A written confirmation from the head landlord that the head lease is in force, that rent is paid up and that there are no known defaults.
  • Arrange a direct-payment right. Some subtenants negotiate the right to pay rent directly to the head landlord if the sublandlord defaults, with a credit against the sublease rent.
  • Protect your deposit. Keep any deposit modest, held in a way that survives the sublandlord's insolvency, such as a separate account or a bank guarantee you control the terms of.
  • Clarify end-of-term obligations. Agree in writing that you will hand the space back as you found it, and that the sublandlord, not you, remains responsible for reinstating its own fit-out under the head lease.
  • Check local formalities. In England and Wales, for example, subleases are often contracted out of the security-of-tenure provisions of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1954, which needs a specific procedure before signing.

Questions to ask before you sign

QuestionWhy it matters
When does the head lease end, and are there any break dates?A head-lease break exercised by either party could end your sublease early.
Why is the space being sublet?A business downsizing is normal; one in financial difficulty is a risk to you.
Is the sublandlord up to date with rent and other obligations?Arrears or breaches give the head landlord grounds to end the head lease.
Will the head landlord sign recognition or non-disturbance terms?It is your best protection if the sublandlord fails.
What exactly is included: furniture, AV, IT, kitchen equipment?Without a written inventory, items can be removed before you arrive.
Who provides services, repairs and after-hours heating or cooling?The sublandlord can only pass on what the head lease gives it.
What are the service charges or operating costs, and how are they reconciled?Year-end adjustments can arrive after the sublandlord has lost interest.
What signage can you have?Head lease and building rules may limit it, which matters for client-facing firms.
What must you remove or restore at the end?You should not inherit the sublandlord's reinstatement obligations.
Would the head landlord grant a direct lease when the head lease ends?An early conversation can secure continuity if you want to stay.

When a sublease is, and is not, the right move

A sublease suits a company that needs a fitted office quickly, values the saving and the shorter commitment, and can live with less control: a growing team that has outgrown coworking, a business between funding rounds, or a firm that wants to test a new city before committing. It suits less well if you need to customise heavily, want your name on the building, need certainty beyond the head-lease term, or cannot absorb the cost of moving again at short notice.

As with any lease, price the whole deal rather than the rent alone.
