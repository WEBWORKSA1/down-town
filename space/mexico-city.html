In 30 seconds

  • Who it suits: multinationals, nearshoring and regional teams, and hospitality and retail businesses that want access to one of the largest urban markets in the Americas
  • The main office cores: Paseo de la Reforma, Polanco and Santa Fe, with Insurgentes Sur and the Centro for value and character
  • The one lease quirk to know: Class A rent is often quoted in US dollars, IVA is added on top, and landlords expect a guarantor (fiador), surety bond or substantial deposit

Mexico City's business geography does not centre on its historic downtown. The Centro Histórico is the seat of government, the city's oldest commercial quarter and its busiest retail district on foot, but corporate Mexico works further west. Paseo de la Reforma, the boulevard that runs from near the Alameda past the Angel of Independence to Chapultepec Park, is lined with the city's tallest towers. Polanco, north of the park, mixes headquarters and embassies with luxury shopping, and Santa Fe, on the western edge, is a district of corporate campuses built around the car. Insurgentes Sur, Roma, Condesa and Juárez fill in with mid-rise offices, converted houses and creative studios.

Tenants include banks, insurers, consumer-goods and pharmaceutical companies, telecoms and media firms, law and consulting practices, and a growing number of technology and shared-services teams, including companies moving operations to Mexico to be closer to North American customers. The newest towers along Reforma and in Polanco were designed to modern seismic codes and international sustainability certifications, and command a clear premium over older Class B buildings. Earthquakes, most recently in 2017, have made structural quality a first-order question rather than a footnote, and water supply, parking ratios and security are part of every serious search.

Where the offices are

Paseo de la ReformaThe city's prime corridor of Class A towers, home to banks, insurers, multinationals and embassies, served by Metrobús Line 7 and Metro stations at either end. Suits companies that want the most recognised business address in the country.
Polanco and Nuevo PolancoCorporate offices, embassies and luxury retail in Polanco, with newer mixed-use towers and larger floors in redeveloped Nuevo Polanco to the north-west. Suits headquarters, consumer brands and professional firms.
Santa FeA planned corporate district on the western edge with large modern floors, campus-style complexes and ample parking, but heavy car dependence and long commutes from the east of the city. Suits large headquarters with staff in the western suburbs.
Insurgentes SurA long avenue of mid-rise office buildings running south through Del Valle and Nápoles, served by Metrobús Line 1. Often good value for mid-size tenants.
Roma, Condesa and JuárezConverted houses, small buildings and coworking floors popular with startups, agencies and design firms, a short ride from Reforma.
Centro HistóricoGovernment offices, professional firms and specialist trades in historic buildings, with the strongest pedestrian footfall in the city. Suits retail, hospitality and businesses that deal with government; check heritage rules before any works.

Retail and restaurant corridors

The Centro is the city's great pedestrian market. Calle Madero, Avenida 5 de Mayo and the streets around the Zócalo draw huge crowds of local shoppers and visitors, and side streets still specialise by trade — lighting, books, jewellery, wedding wear, stationery — which makes them destinations in their own right. Footfall is heavy every day and peaks at weekends, through the December holidays and around national celebrations. Polanco's Avenida Presidente Masaryk is the luxury strip, while Roma and Condesa are the city's restaurant and café districts, driven by evening and weekend trade and a large international crowd. Reforma's ground floors and the malls of Polanco and Santa Fe serve office workers and higher-income shoppers.

Before signing, confirm that the land-use certificate (certificado de uso de suelo) allows your type of business, and file the operating notice or permit through the city's system; venues that serve alcohol face a stricter permitting regime. In the Centro, alterations to protected buildings need approval from the national heritage institutes, INAH and INBAL, which can add months to a fit-out.

Coworking and flexible space

Flexible workspace has expanded quickly. Operators occupy floors in Reforma towers, Polanco and Santa Fe for corporate clients, and converted houses and smaller buildings in Roma, Condesa and Juárez for startups, agencies and remote workers. Typical options are hot desks and dedicated desks on monthly memberships, and private offices on agreements from a few months to a couple of years, usually priced in pesos with IVA added. Many centres provide a business address, which helps while a company is being registered. Flexible space is especially useful for foreign companies, because it avoids the guarantor, deposit and fit-out demands of a conventional lease while the local entity is set up.

How commercial leases work here

Commercial leases (contratos de arrendamiento) for Class A offices are commonly quoted in US dollars per square metre per month, while Class B buildings, smaller offices and shops usually quote in pesos. Obligations in dollars can generally be paid in pesos at the prevailing exchange rate, so check how the lease handles currency. IVA, Mexico's value-added tax, is charged on commercial rent and on the maintenance fee (cuota de mantenimiento), which covers common areas, security and building services and is quoted separately per square metre. Office leases commonly run three to five years, with annual increases linked to inflation — typically US inflation for dollar leases and the Mexican consumer price index for peso leases. Space is often delivered as a grey shell (obra gris), so the tenant pays for a full fit-out, sometimes with a landlord contribution or rent-free months.

Landlords protect themselves with a mix of deposit and guarantee. A deposit of one to three months' rent is common, and many landlords also ask for a fiador — a guarantor who owns property in the city — or, for companies, a surety bond (fianza) from a bonding company or a bank letter of credit. Parking spaces are usually allocated in proportion to the area leased, and extra spaces cost more. Your landlord must issue electronic tax invoices (CFDI) for rent, which you need in order to deduct it, and if your company rents from an individual rather than a company, it may have to withhold part of the income tax and IVA. Property tax (predial) is normally the owner's responsibility.

Getting your team there

Commute times shape office choices here more than almost anything else. Reforma is the best-connected corridor, with Metrobús Line 7 along the boulevard and Metro stations near both ends, and Insurgentes Sur has Metrobús Line 1. Polanco has Metro Line 7 and buses but relies heavily on cars and ride-hailing, and Santa Fe is the hardest district to reach without a car. The Centro has the densest Metro coverage in the city. Traffic is heavy at peak times and the Hoy No Circula scheme keeps some cars off the road on certain days, so many employers offer flexible hours. Parking ratios matter in Class A buildings because many staff and visitors still drive, and Ecobici bike share is practical in Reforma, Polanco, Roma, Condesa and the Centro. Test the commute from where your staff actually live, at rush hour, before you shortlist buildings.

What to check before you sign

  • Structural safety. Ask when the building was designed and built, whether a structural engineer has reviewed it since 2017, and for its structural safety documentation; walk away from visible settlement or unrepaired damage.
  • Land use. Confirm the land-use certificate permits your activity at that address before you commit to a fit-out.
  • Currency and indexation. Check whether rent is in dollars or pesos, which inflation index applies, and whether increases are capped.
  • Guarantee requirements. Agree early whether the landlord will accept a surety bond, letter of credit or larger deposit instead of a personal fiador.
  • Maintenance fee and parking. Get the maintenance fee and what it covers in writing, and confirm how many parking spaces come with the lease.
  • Water and power resilience. Ask about cistern capacity, the building's water-supply history and backup generators; supply interruptions affect parts of the city.
  • Heritage approvals. In the Centro, alterations to listed buildings require heritage-institute approval; build the time into your schedule.

Hosting events downtown

Mexico City is a major business-events destination, with large convention and exhibition centres in the west of the city and on Insurgentes Sur, and hotel ballrooms along Reforma and in Polanco for conferences and dinners. The Centro offers venues few cities can match: colonial palaces, former convents, museums and courtyards that can be hired for receptions, and rooftop terraces overlooking the Zócalo and the cathedral. Roma and Condesa have galleries, private dining rooms and event spaces suited to launches, and haciendas on the outskirts host weddings and retreats. The dry season, roughly October to April, is the best time for outdoor events; in the rainy season, plan for afternoon downpours. Day of the Dead, the Formula 1 Grand Prix weekend in the autumn and the December holidays push up hotel demand. Events in streets and plazas need permits from the borough (alcaldía) — Cuauhtémoc covers the Centro, Juárez, Roma and Condesa, and Miguel Hidalgo covers Polanco — and from city authorities for larger gatherings.

Is downtown the right base?

If "downtown" means the Centro Histórico, it is the right base for retail, hospitality, cultural businesses and firms that work closely with government — but not for most corporate headquarters. For those, Reforma is the city's true business downtown, Polanco suits brands and professional firms that want a polished address, and Santa Fe works for large teams who drive. Insurgentes Sur and the Roma, Condesa and Juárez neighbourhoods offer value and character for mid-size and creative firms. Whichever you pick, weigh the commute as heavily as the rent.
