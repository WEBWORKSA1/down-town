In 30 seconds

  • Suits law firms, associations, advocacy and policy groups, consultancies, government contractors and media that need to be near federal decision-makers
  • The main cores are the Central Business District around K Street and the East End around Penn Quarter, with NoMa and Navy Yard offering newer stock
  • The quirk to know: you need a "Clean Hands" certification — proof you owe the District nothing — before you can get a business licence

Washington's commercial core sits north of the National Mall between the White House and the Capitol. The Central Business District runs along K Street and around Farragut, McPherson and Franklin squares; the East End stretches from Metro Center through Penn Quarter to Judiciary Square and the courts. North of Union Station, NoMa has grown into a district of newer office and apartment towers, and to the south-east and south-west, Capitol Riverfront at Navy Yard and the Southwest Waterfront around the Wharf and L'Enfant Plaza house federal agencies and newer mixed-use buildings.

Because of the federal height limit, the market is made of box-shaped mid-rise buildings with large floors rather than skyscrapers. Tenants are dominated by law firms, trade and professional associations, lobbying and advocacy groups, consultancies, government contractors, think tanks, media and international organisations, all orbiting the federal government. Demand has shifted towards newer, amenity-rich buildings, while many older ones face high vacancy, and the city has offered incentives to convert them to housing. Shifts in federal staffing and leasing in recent years have added uncertainty for buildings that rely on government tenants.

Retail and restaurant corridors

Penn Quarter and Chinatown, around Gallery Place and the arena, have the busiest mix of restaurants, bars and shops, drawing office workers, visitors and event crowds. The Wharf has built a strong waterfront dining and entertainment trade, and Navy Yard's restaurants feed off residents and ballgame crowds. Union Station's concourses serve commuters and travellers. Just north of downtown, the 14th Street corridor is one of the city's leading dining streets. In the K Street core, ground-floor trade is largely weekday breakfast and lunch, and hybrid working has made Mondays and Fridays quieter. Spring tourist season, school groups and congressional sessions bring peaks, while August is often slow.

Coworking and flexible space

Flexible operators cluster in the Central Business District, Penn Quarter, NoMa and Navy Yard, and across the river in Arlington. Typical options are hot desks and dedicated desks on monthly memberships, private offices for small teams on terms from a month to a couple of years, and meeting and event rooms for briefings. They suit start-ups, advocacy campaigns, consultants and out-of-town companies that need a Washington address, and many offer mail handling and a registered business address. A few specialist providers in the region offer accredited secure space for cleared government contractors.

How commercial leases work here

D.C. follows US conventions. Office rent is quoted per rentable square foot per year, and most downtown office space is leased on a full-service basis: base rent covers operating costs and real estate taxes for a base year, and the tenant pays its share of increases after that. Annual escalations are built into rent. Measurement methods for rentable area can differ between buildings, so compare the usable area you will occupy. Retail is typically triple net (NNN), with the tenant paying a share of taxes, insurance and common-area costs. Large law firms and associations often sign long leases of ten to fifteen years or more, and in return negotiate substantial tenant improvement allowances and free-rent periods.

Smaller tenants usually sign three to five years, often in pre-built suites, and young companies are commonly asked for a personal guarantee, a larger deposit or a letter of credit. The District does not charge sales tax on commercial rent, but it levies its own business franchise tax. Before you open, you will need a certificate of occupancy for your use and a Basic Business License, which requires a "Clean Hands" certification showing you owe nothing to the District government.

Getting your team there

Metrorail is the backbone of downtown commuting. The Red, Orange, Blue and Silver lines meet at Metro Center, the Green and Yellow lines cross at Gallery Place and L'Enfant Plaza, and Farragut North and Farragut West serve the heart of the K Street core. Union Station brings MARC trains from Maryland, VRE trains from Virginia and Amtrak from the Northeast Corridor; VRE also stops at L'Enfant Plaza. Metrobus fills the gaps, and Capital Bikeshare and protected bike lanes make cycling popular. Many staff commute from Maryland and Virginia; some drive, and from Northern Virginia, informal carpooling on HOV lanes is a local institution. Garage parking is available but expensive, so buildings close to a Metro station have a strong advantage.

What to check before you sign

  • Licensing: secure your Clean Hands certification, Basic Business License and certificate of occupancy early, especially for food, medical or assembly uses.
  • Historic and design review: downtown historic districts, Pennsylvania Avenue and buildings facing federal landmarks can face extra review of façades and signage.
  • Security and access: protests, motorcades and federal security perimeters can close streets and entrances, so ask how the building manages deliveries and access on those days.
  • Building energy performance standards, and whether compliance upgrades could be passed through as operating costs.
  • Ceiling heights and floor-to-floor clearances in older buildings, which can limit fit-out options.
  • The building's tenant mix: high exposure to federal tenants can mean vacancy risk and changes to shared amenities.
  • For contractors, whether the space can meet the physical security standards your work requires.

Hosting events downtown

The Walter E. Washington Convention Center at Mount Vernon Square handles large conferences, and downtown hotel ballrooms host association meetings, dinners and policy events throughout the year. Distinctive options include the vast Great Hall of the National Building Museum, a traditional venue for inaugural balls, museum spaces for receptions, embassies that host cultural events, rooftop terraces with views of the monuments, the Wharf's piers and music venues, and the grand halls of Union Station. Spring is the busiest season, with cherry-blossom crowds and school groups, and autumn brings the conference season; summer is hot and humid, and the city is especially busy in January of inauguration years. Events on the National Mall and other federal parkland need National Park Service permits, and those on District streets go through the city's special events process.

Is downtown the right base?

For law, lobbying, associations and organisations that meet officials in person, downtown D.C. is hard to beat: the Central Business District and East End put Congress, the agencies and the courts a short Metro ride away. NoMa, Navy Yard and the Southwest Waterfront offer newer buildings and lively neighbourhoods at varying discounts. Across the river, the Rosslyn–Ballston corridor, Tysons and National Landing in Northern Virginia, and Bethesda in Maryland, appeal to tech firms and contractors, with different tax regimes, more parking and closer links to the region's tech workforce.
