In 30 seconds

  • Who it suits: tech companies, nearshore service centres, startups, tourism and hospitality businesses, and European teams wanting a lower cost base
  • The main office cores: Avenida da Liberdade, the Avenidas Novas and Parque das Nações, plus the historic centre for smaller firms
  • The one lease quirk to know: commercial rent is VAT-exempt by default, and the landlord can only add VAT by formally waiving the exemption

Central Lisbon's commercial geography runs north from the river. The historic Baixa, Chiado and Cais do Sodré are dominated by shops, hospitality and converted buildings that suit small offices and creative firms. The Avenida da Liberdade climbs from Restauradores to Marquês de Pombal, lined with luxury flagships at street level and prestige offices above. North of the roundabout, the Avenidas Novas, around Saldanha, Picoas, Avenida da República and Entrecampos, form the city's main business district, with larger and more modern office buildings. Parque das Nações, the former Expo 98 site on the eastern riverside, is a planned district of contemporary offices close to the airport.

Demand has been driven by technology firms, nearshore service and engineering centres, startups and international companies attracted by talent, quality of life and costs that are still competitive by western European standards, alongside the traditional banking, legal, consulting and public sectors. New, energy-efficient buildings with large floors are in short supply in the centre, so the best stock is keenly contested while older buildings need refurbishment to compete. In the historic core, tourism has reshaped street retail, with souvenir, food and hospitality uses crowding out traditional shops.

Where the offices are

Avenida da Liberdade and Marquês de PombalLaw firms, private banks, embassies and luxury brands in nineteenth- and early twentieth-century buildings with modest floorplates. On the Blue Metro line. The prestige address for client-facing firms.
Avenidas NovasSaldanha, Picoas, Avenida da República and Entrecampos hold most of the larger office buildings used by banks, insurers, consultancies and corporate headquarters. Served by the Yellow, Blue and Red Metro lines and rail at Entrecampos. The core for mid-sized and larger teams.
Baixa, Chiado and Cais do SodréStartups, agencies and small firms in restored historic buildings, with superb amenities but small floors, heritage constraints and limited parking. Good for teams that value character and an easy walk to everything.
Parque das NaçõesModern offices on the eastern riverside, popular with technology, telecoms and shared-service operations. Close to the airport and the Oriente rail and Metro interchange, with more parking than the historic centre.
Alcântara and the western riversideConverted warehouses and industrial buildings near the 25 de Abril Bridge, home to creative studios, media and tech firms, with trains along the Cascais line.
Marvila and BeatoThe eastern riverside's old warehouses and factories are being converted into creative and startup space, including a municipal creative hub. Still in transition, but a growing option for younger companies.

Retail and restaurant corridors

Avenida da Liberdade is the luxury corridor, with international fashion and jewellery flagships. Chiado, along Rua Garrett and Rua do Carmo, mixes fashion, bookshops, cafés and cosmetics, while the Baixa's Rua Augusta, Rua do Ouro and Rua da Prata carry heavy tourist footfall all week and at their peak from spring to autumn. Príncipe Real has concept stores and design shops, Bairro Alto and Cais do Sodré are the nightlife districts, and Campo de Ourique is a strong neighbourhood retail area for residents. Lisbon's Lojas com História programme recognises historic shops, and some of their leases enjoy extra legal protection, which can affect units you might otherwise expect to come to market. Before you sign, confirm that the building's use licence (licença de utilização) permits your activity, and check whether ventilation and extraction can be installed in older buildings.

Coworking and flexible space

Lisbon's coworking market is one of the most active in southern Europe, serving startups, remote workers, digital nomads and international firms testing the city before committing to a lease. Spaces cluster in the Baixa, Chiado and Cais do Sodré, around the Avenidas Novas, in Parque das Nações and in converted warehouses along the eastern and western riverside. Typical options are day passes, hot desks and dedicated desks on monthly agreements, and private offices on terms of a few months to a year, with utilities and internet included. Many centres also offer a registered business address. Check whether quoted prices include VAT, and whether the agreement is a service contract rather than a lease, which affects your rights if things go wrong.

How commercial leases work here

Non-residential leases in Portugal are governed by the Civil Code and the urban lease regime, and the parties have wide freedom to agree terms. Office leases commonly run for around five years, often renewing automatically for further periods unless either party gives notice, and the law gives tenants some protection against non-renewal in the early years, so the stated term is not the whole story. Rent is usually updated annually, by a government coefficient tied to inflation unless the lease sets another mechanism. Leasing commercial space is exempt from VAT by default, but where both parties are VAT-registered businesses using the space for taxable activities, the landlord can waive the exemption under strict conditions and charge VAT. The lease must be in writing and reported to the tax authority, stamp duty on the lease is generally borne by the landlord, and if your company keeps organised accounts it may have to withhold tax on the rent it pays, so speak to your certified accountant before the first payment.

DepositA deposit of a few months' rent is common, often with rent paid in advance; larger tenants may provide a bank guarantee instead
Fit-outUsually the tenant's responsibility, sometimes with a landlord contribution or rent-free period on longer leases; works may need municipal approval
TaxesRent is VAT-exempt unless the landlord waives the exemption, in which case VAT applies at the standard rate (currently 23% in mainland Portugal); stamp duty on the lease and rent withholding rules may apply

Conventions vary by building and landlord. Treat this as orientation, not legal advice, and have a local lawyer review any lease.

Getting your team there

The Metro's four lines link the main office districts: the Blue line runs under Avenida da Liberdade to Marquês de Pombal and São Sebastião, the Yellow line serves Saldanha, Picoas and Entrecampos, the Green line reaches the Baixa and Cais do Sodré, and the Red line connects Saldanha and Alameda with Oriente and the airport. Suburban trains arrive at Cais do Sodré, Rossio, Entrecampos, Sete Rios and Oriente, trains cross the 25 de Abril Bridge from the south bank, and ferries link the Baixa and Cais do Sodré with towns across the Tagus. Many staff use the monthly navegante pass, which covers public transport across the metropolitan area. The Gira bike share helps for short hops. Parking is scarce and expensive in the historic centre; buildings in the Avenidas Novas and Parque das Nações are more likely to have garages, which matters for visitors and senior staff. Parque das Nações and the Avenidas Novas are the closest districts to the airport.

What to check before you sign

  • Use licence. Confirm that the licença de utilização covers your intended use; changing it can take months.
  • Energy certificate. An energy certificate is required when property is leased; check the rating and the state of air conditioning, which matters in Lisbon's hot summers.
  • VAT and withholding. Ask whether the landlord has waived the VAT exemption and whether your company must withhold tax on rent payments.
  • Heritage rules. Much of the Baixa, Chiado and Alfama is protected, so façade, shopfront and signage changes and internal works need municipal approval.
  • Structural condition. Lisbon sits in a seismic zone; for older buildings, ask what structural reinforcement has been done and who is responsible for future works.
  • Flood risk. Low-lying parts of the Baixa and Alcântara can flood in intense rain; check the street's history and any basement space.
  • Condominium charges and rules. Ask for the condominium budget and regulations, including any limits on hours, deliveries or signage.

Hosting events downtown

For large conferences and trade shows, the exhibition centre and arena at Parque das Nações and the congress centre on the western riverside at Junqueira are the main options. Palaces, former convents and museums hire out historic rooms for dinners and receptions, while hotel ballrooms along Avenida da Liberdade and rooftop terraces across the centre suit smaller gatherings with views. Converted riverside warehouses work well for launches and parties. Spring and early autumn are the most comfortable seasons; summer heat favours evening events, and June's festivals and November's Web Summit squeeze hotel availability. Events in public space need permission from the Câmara Municipal de Lisboa, so apply early.

Is downtown the right base?

For client-facing firms, startups and teams that recruit internationally, central Lisbon offers a rare combination of good transport, character and quality of life. Larger teams that need big modern floors usually look to the Avenidas Novas or Parque das Nações, while business parks in the western corridor around Oeiras offer campus-style space, parking and lower costs, at the price of a car-dependent commute. Many firms combine a small central office with larger premises elsewhere. For neighbourhoods, transport and daily life, read the downtown Lisbon guide.
