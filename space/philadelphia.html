In 30 seconds

  • Suits professional services, health and life-science firms, universities' spin-outs and creative studios that want a Northeast Corridor address at a discount to New York, Boston or Washington
  • The main cores are Market Street West, University City and the loft blocks of Old City and Market East
  • The quirk to know: Philadelphia levies its own business taxes, including a Use and Occupancy tax that landlords collect from tenants

Center City's commercial geography follows William Penn's grid. The high-rise office core sits west of City Hall in Market Street West, a district built largely from the 1950s onward after the Pennsylvania Railroad's elevated viaduct into the city centre came down. East of City Hall, Market East runs past the convention centre and Jefferson Station towards Old City, where brick warehouses hold creative offices and showrooms. Across the Schuylkill, University City around 30th Street Station has become a second centre, built on universities, hospitals and research labs.

The market's character is steady rather than flashy. Anchors include law firms, insurers, banks, consultancies, healthcare systems, universities and a growing life-science cluster, with cell and gene therapy a particular strength. The tower stock is mostly from the 1970s to the 1990s, with a handful of newer trophy buildings that attract firms upgrading their space. Older, lower-grade offices face pressure, and some are being converted to apartments. Location on the Northeast Corridor, a large graduate talent pool and costs below the bigger East Coast cities are the main demand drivers.

Where the offices are

Market Street WestThe central business district: towers along Market Street and JFK Boulevard housing law, finance, insurance and corporate headquarters. Direct access to Suburban Station and the subway makes it the easiest place for regional commuters, and it is the default for firms wanting a classic downtown address.
Rittenhouse and Walnut StreetSmaller office floors above shops and in converted townhouses, popular with wealth managers, boutique law firms, medical and aesthetic practices and agencies that want a polished, walkable address.
Market East and Midtown VillageMixed older office buildings, government and healthcare tenants around Jefferson, and space tied to the convention trade. Conversions and new housing are changing the area, and rents tend to sit below Market West.
Old City and Northern LibertiesBrick-and-beam lofts with high ceilings and big windows, suited to design studios, tech start-ups, architects and showrooms. Historic-district rules shape façades and signage.
University CityPurpose-built lab and office buildings around the universities and 30th Street Station, aimed at life sciences, research and university spin-outs. Amtrak and Regional Rail on the doorstep help with national and regional recruiting.

Retail and restaurant corridors

Walnut Street between Broad Street and Rittenhouse Square is the city's prime retail strip, with flagship and luxury tenants and the strongest weekend footfall. Chestnut Street runs parallel with more everyday retail, services and casual dining. Market East is anchored by a large enclosed shopping centre, the convention centre and Reading Terminal Market, and its traffic rises and falls with the convention calendar. Midtown Village, around 13th Street, is a dense dining and nightlife quarter, while South Street and Old City suit independent fashion, galleries and bars, with Old City's First Friday gallery nights drawing evening crowds. Market West's ground floors live on weekday office lunches and go quiet at weekends. Many small restaurants open as BYOBs because Pennsylvania liquor licences are capped by county and usually bought from existing holders, which adds a large upfront cost.

Coworking and flexible space

Flexible operators cluster in Market Street West towers, around Rittenhouse, in Old City lofts and in University City, where shared wet labs and incubators serve early-stage life-science firms. Typical options are hot desks and dedicated desks on monthly memberships, and private offices for small teams on terms from a month to a year or two, usually with a deposit of about a month's fee. Many also offer virtual office addresses and meeting-room credits. Flexible space suits start-ups, satellite teams for New York or Washington firms and companies waiting for a long-term lease to come together.

How commercial leases work here

Philadelphia follows US conventions. Office rent is quoted per rentable square foot per year, which includes a share of common areas, so compare usable space as well as headline rates. Most Center City office space is offered on a full-service gross basis: the rent covers operating costs and real estate taxes for a base year, and you pay your share of increases after that. Some buildings use modified gross terms, where you pay electricity or janitorial separately. Retail is usually triple net (NNN), with the tenant paying a share of property taxes, insurance and common-area maintenance on top of base rent. Annual rent escalations are standard.

Landlords commonly offer a tenant improvement allowance and a few months of free rent, both scaled to the length of the lease and the tenant's credit. Small and new businesses are often asked for a personal guarantee, a larger deposit or a letter of credit. Pennsylvania does not charge sales tax on commercial rent, but Philadelphia has its own taxes to plan for: the Use and Occupancy tax collected by landlords, the Business Income and Receipts Tax and the city wage tax on employees.

Getting your team there

Center City is one of the best-connected downtowns in the country for regional commuters. Every SEPTA Regional Rail line runs through the tunnel stopping at Jefferson, Suburban and 30th Street stations, which gives Market Street West and University City the widest reach. The Market–Frankford Line (the L) and Broad Street Line (the B) cross beneath City Hall, trolleys tunnel to University City, and the PATCO Speedline brings South Jersey commuters to stations along Locust and Market Streets. Amtrak and New Jersey Transit serve 30th Street Station. Monthly garage parking is available but costly, and street parking is tightly enforced, so few downtown firms rely on it. Bike lanes and Indego docks make cycling a real option for staff living in nearby neighbourhoods.

What to check before you sign

  • Who pays the Use and Occupancy tax, how it is billed, and whether it is included in operating-cost statements.
  • Zoning and licensing: confirm your use is permitted, and that you can obtain the necessary zoning permit and Commercial Activity License before you commit, especially for food, medical or assembly uses.
  • Historic-district rules in Old City, Society Hill and parts of Rittenhouse, which can restrict signage, windows, awnings and façade changes.
  • After-hours heating and cooling charges and building access, particularly in older towers with fixed operating hours.
  • Loading and deliveries: narrow streets and strict curb rules make a loading dock or alley access valuable for retail and restaurants.
  • For restaurants, the availability and cost of a liquor licence, ventilation routes and grease interceptors in older buildings.
  • For riverside sites, flood risk on the Schuylkill and Delaware frontages and where building systems are located.

Hosting events downtown

The Pennsylvania Convention Center in Market East handles large conferences and trade shows, surrounded by hotel ballrooms that suit everything from board dinners to multi-day meetings. The museums of the Benjamin Franklin Parkway and Old City offer galleries and terraces for evening receptions, historic halls and churches suit more formal gatherings, and converted industrial buildings in Old City and Northern Liberties work for product launches and parties. On the Delaware, piers and seasonal waterfront parks give summer events a river backdrop. Spring and autumn are peak seasons; large city events such as Welcome America and the marathon tie up hotels and streets. Events on streets, squares or park land require city permits, so build in lead time.

Is downtown the right base?

For most firms that want to be seen, recruit graduates and reach clients by train, Center City is the natural choice, and Market Street West remains the safest bet. University City makes more sense for labs and research-heavy teams, and the Navy Yard offers a campus alternative within the city. Suburban markets such as King of Prussia, Conshohocken and the Main Line corridor attract firms weighing parking and city taxes, but they give up walkability and transit. Compare total occupancy costs, including the city's business taxes, before deciding. For the bigger picture of life and leisure in the centre, see the downtown Philadelphia guide.
