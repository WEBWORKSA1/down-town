In 30 seconds

  • Who it suits: start-ups, public-affairs and policy teams, media and creative firms, and visitor-facing retail
  • The main office cores: Friedrichstraße, Potsdamer Platz, the main-station quarter and Alexanderplatz, plus the Spree banks to the east
  • The one lease quirk to know: long fixed terms only hold if the whole lease meets the statutory form requirement

Berlin never had a single business district, and reunification left it with several. Mitte, the historic centre of the old East, holds the federal government, embassies, associations and law firms around Friedrichstraße and Unter den Linden. Potsdamer Platz was rebuilt from wasteland on the line of the Wall into a cluster of corporate towers, and the quarter north of the main station is the newest office district. The old western centre around Kurfürstendamm remains a separate market of its own.

The market's character comes from its tenants. Government and the organisations that lobby it want to be near the Reichstag; technology, music and media firms prefer converted industrial buildings and new campuses along the Spree; and start-ups move from coworking floors to their first lease as they grow. Building stock runs from Prussian-era commercial buildings and courtyard workshops to GDR-era blocks and recent glass towers with high energy standards, which large tenants increasingly demand.

Where the offices are

Friedrichstraße and GendarmenmarktLaw firms, consultancies, associations and embassies in stone-faced buildings from the 1990s rebuild. Excellent U-Bahn and S-Bahn links; the address of choice for public-affairs teams.
Potsdamer Platz and Leipziger PlatzCorporate towers and campus-style blocks with film and entertainment tenants, regional headquarters and professional services. Served by U-Bahn, S-Bahn and regional trains.
Main station quarterA new district of modern office buildings north of Berlin's central station, suited to firms that want long-distance rail on the doorstep and contemporary floor plates.
AlexanderplatzA mix of GDR-era blocks and new high-rises around the city's busiest eastern hub. Good for back-office teams and firms that value transit over prestige.
Spree banks in Friedrichshain and KreuzbergFormer warehouses and new campuses along the river, home to tech, music and media companies. Best for creative and digital teams who want space and character.
Hackescher Markt and Spandauer VorstadtCourtyard buildings and loft floors for agencies, galleries and small studios, surrounded by cafés and boutiques.

Retail and restaurant corridors

Friedrichstraße is the classic shopping street of the centre, though it has struggled with vacancies and a contested car-free trial, and it now leans on offices and visitors more than local shoppers. The streets around Hackescher Markt and Alte and Neue Schönhauser Straße are the place for independent fashion, concept stores and design shops. Torstraße and Rosenthaler Platz carry restaurants and bars that trade late into the night. Alexanderplatz draws volume shoppers to department stores and chains, while Potsdamer Platz relies on office workers, cinema-goers and tourists. Footfall in Mitte is strongest from Thursday to Saturday; on weekdays, lunch trade follows the office workers, and most shops must close on Sundays, which shifts weekend spending to cafés, restaurants and museums.

Coworking and flexible space

Berlin has a deep flexible-office market, and many start-ups spend their first years in one. Operators cluster around Rosenthaler Platz and Hackescher Markt, along the Spree in Friedrichshain and Kreuzberg, and near the main station. Expect monthly memberships for hot desks and fixed desks, with private offices for teams on terms from a few months to a year. Flexible space suits foreign companies testing the market, project teams and firms that want to avoid a long fixed-term commitment while they hire. Check whether a membership gives you a business address you can use for registration and post.

How commercial leases work here

German commercial leases (Gewerbemietverträge) are governed by the Civil Code but leave far more to negotiation than residential tenancies: there is no rent brake and limited tenant protection. Terms are typically fixed for five to ten years, often with one or more options to extend, and rent is commonly indexed to the consumer price index or stepped. Rent is quoted net per square metre per month, with service charges (Nebenkosten) — building operation, cleaning, insurance and property tax — paid as monthly advances and reconciled at year-end. Rent is exempt from VAT unless the landlord opts to charge it, which is common in office buildings and requires the tenant to use the space mainly for VAT-able business.

The form requirement is the quirk that catches newcomers. A lease fixed for more than a year must meet the statutory form: strict written form (Schriftform) for decades, and since 2025 text form. If the whole agreement — every annex, floor plan and side letter — is not documented properly, the fixed term can fall away and either party may terminate on statutory notice. Landlords usually provide shell or standard fit-out, with contributions or rent-free periods negotiated on larger deals.

Getting your team there

Berlin's public transport is the easiest sell to staff. The east–west S-Bahn line links Alexanderplatz, Hackescher Markt, Friedrichstraße and the main station, the Ring line circles the inner city, and U-Bahn lines U2, U5, U6 and U8 cross Mitte. The main station puts long-distance trains to Hamburg, Munich and Frankfurt within a few steps of the new office quarter, and the airport is reached by express and regional trains. Employers can offer the Deutschlandticket as a subsidised job ticket. Cycling is popular, so ask about secure bike parking and showers. Car parking is scarce and paid on most central streets, and the centre is a low-emission zone, so visitor parking needs planning.

What to check before you sign

  • Confirm the permitted use with the district building authority; a change of use needs a permit, and converting residential flats to offices or holiday lets is restricted in Berlin.
  • Check heritage protection, which covers many buildings in Mitte and limits changes to façades, windows and interiors.
  • Ask for the energy performance certificate and the last service-charge reconciliation, and check which costs can be passed through.
  • Make sure every annex, plan and side agreement is attached in the required form, so the fixed term holds.
  • Review indexation, extension options and any break rights; outside narrow grounds for termination for cause, a fixed term cannot be ended early.
  • For shops and restaurants, check permits for terraces, signs and kitchen extraction, and the building's rules on noise and night-time quiet.
  • Look at access for deliveries in courtyard buildings, lifts and step-free entry for staff and customers.

Hosting events downtown

Berlin hosts events at every scale. The exhibition grounds in the west handle major trade fairs, including ITB Berlin in spring and IFA in late summer, when hotels across the city fill up; the Berlinale does the same for Potsdamer Platz in February. Downtown, expect hotel ballrooms, historic halls and former industrial buildings, galleries, rooftop terraces with views of the TV tower, and boats on the Spree for summer receptions. Summer is the busiest season for outdoor events, and winter suits Christmas-season gatherings indoors. Events in public spaces need permission from the district office, and noise limits apply at night, so start the paperwork early.

Is downtown the right base?

For government relations, international visibility and transit, Mitte is hard to beat, and Potsdamer Platz and the main-station quarter offer the most modern space. Creative and tech firms often get more for their money and a better cultural fit along the Spree in Friedrichshain and Kreuzberg, while the western centre around Kurfürstendamm suits firms with clients in the west and a preference for traditional office buildings. Science and engineering companies may look further out, to the research campus at Adlershof. Read our downtown Berlin guide to get a feel for the neighbourhoods.
