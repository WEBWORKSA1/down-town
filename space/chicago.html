In 30 seconds

  • Who it suits: finance, law, consulting and corporate headquarters, plus tech and consumer brands in Fulton Market
  • The main office cores: the Central Loop and LaSalle Street, the West Loop riverfront, Fulton Market and River North
  • The one lease quirk to know: many listings show net rent with taxes and operating expenses quoted separately, and Cook County property taxes are a big part of the total

Chicago has one of the largest downtown office markets in North America, and its geography is easy to read. The Central Loop, inside the elevated tracks, holds the historic financial canyon of LaSalle Street and a dense mix of towers from every era. West of the river, the West Loop's riverfront towers along Wacker Drive and Canal Street sit beside Union Station and Ogilvie, the main commuter-rail terminals. Further west, Fulton Market has turned former meatpacking blocks into lofts and new boutique towers, while River North, Streeterville and the East Loop extend the office market north of the river and towards the lake.

The tenant base is deep and diverse: derivatives exchanges and trading firms, banks, insurers, law and consulting firms, corporate headquarters, healthcare and universities, and a fast-growing tech and consumer-brand scene. As elsewhere in North America, demand has concentrated in the newest and best-located buildings, especially the riverfront towers and Fulton Market, while older buildings, particularly on LaSalle Street, have been repositioned or converted to apartments under a city initiative. Access to Metra commuter rail is a defining factor in where companies choose to locate.

Where the offices are

Retail and restaurant corridors

State Street is the Loop's traditional retail spine, with department stores, flagships and everyday shops serving office workers and the many students from downtown's college campuses. The Magnificent Mile on North Michigan Avenue is the big-brand and luxury strip, driven by visitors and seasonal peaks, and has seen considerable turnover in recent years. Randolph Street and Fulton Market form the city's best-known restaurant row, busiest in the evenings and at weekends, while River North mixes restaurants, nightlife and galleries. Inside the Loop, lunch trade is strongest midweek, as hybrid work concentrates office attendance from Tuesday to Thursday, and the commuter concourses at Union Station and Ogilvie and the Pedway's corridors support convenience retail. The Riverwalk's seasonal concessions offer a summer-only trade.

Restaurants and bars need a city business licence and, for alcohol, a liquor licence; some areas are covered by liquor moratoria, so check the exact address before signing. Our restaurant space checklist covers ventilation, grease traps and the other fit-out questions.

Coworking and flexible space

Chicago has a large, mature flexible-office market. Operators fill floors throughout the Loop, especially in the West Loop near the commuter-rail stations, and in Fulton Market and River North. Many landlords also offer move-in-ready spec suites on shorter leases. Typical options are hot desks and dedicated desks on monthly memberships, private offices on agreements of a few months to a year, enterprise suites for larger teams, meeting rooms by the hour and virtual-office addresses. Flexible space suits satellite teams, companies testing Chicago and firms that want to cut space while hybrid work settles. Compare options with our coworking versus lease calculator.

How commercial leases work here

Office rent in Chicago is quoted per rentable square foot per year, including a load factor for common areas. Listing conventions vary more than in many US cities: many Loop landlords advertise a net rent and show estimated real-estate taxes and operating expenses separately, which the tenant pays on top, while others quote full-service gross with a base-year expense stop. Retail is generally net, with the tenant paying its share of taxes, insurance and common-area costs. Cook County reassesses property on a rolling three-year cycle and tax appeals are routine, so real-estate taxes can move noticeably during a lease.

Landlords compete with tenant-improvement allowances, free-rent periods and, for spec suites, finished space ready to move into, with the most generous packages reserved for long terms and strong tenants. Smaller and newer companies are often asked for a personal guarantee, a larger deposit or a letter of credit. A tenant-representation broker, usually paid by the landlord, is standard for anything beyond a small suite; see tenant rep brokers explained and negotiating office rent.

Getting your team there

Downtown Chicago is one of the easiest places in North America to commute to without a car. Every CTA 'L' line serves the Loop, and the Red and Blue lines run around the clock. Metra commuter trains terminate at Union Station and Ogilvie in the West Loop and at LaSalle Street and Millennium stations, and the South Shore Line brings riders from north-west Indiana. CTA buses, Divvy bikes and protected bike lanes fill the gaps. Driving is slow and garages are expensive, so parking is rarely part of the deal for most staff. The West Loop is easiest for suburban rail commuters, the Central and East Loop for 'L' riders from across the city, and Fulton Market works well for city residents but is a longer walk from Metra.

What to check before you sign

  • Real-estate tax pass-throughs. Ask how taxes are estimated, how reassessment increases are shared and whether refunds from appeals are credited back to tenants.
  • Operating-expense protections. Negotiate gross-up to full occupancy, audit rights and caps on controllable expenses.
  • Winter operations. Check snow and ice management, entrance vestibules, heating performance at the perimeter and after-hours HVAC charges for both heating and cooling.
  • Loading and deliveries. Many buildings rely on Lower Wacker Drive or alleys for freight; confirm dock access, freight-elevator scheduling and move-in rules.
  • Landmarks, zoning and signage. Parts of downtown are in landmark districts, and signage and storefront changes need city permits; confirm what is allowed before you design.
  • Energy rules. Large Chicago buildings must report energy use under city benchmarking rules; ask about planned efficiency upgrades and how their costs pass through.
  • Licensing for hospitality uses. Confirm the zoning, business licence and liquor licence path, including any moratorium on the block.

Hosting events downtown

McCormick Place, on the Near South Side, is one of the largest convention centres in North America, and Navy Pier, the big downtown hotels and their ballrooms handle most other conferences and corporate events. The Chicago Cultural Center, the major museums and the Art Institute offer grand settings for receptions, and the Riverwalk, architecture cruises and rooftop terraces are summer favourites. West Loop lofts and Fulton Market's event spaces suit launches and parties. Events in Millennium Park and Grant Park need permits from the city's cultural affairs and special events department and the Chicago Park District. Outdoor events work from May to October; winter is for indoor and holiday events, and big conventions can fill hotels across downtown, so check the calendar early.

Is downtown the right base?

For most companies with Chicago-area staff, downtown is the obvious choice: it has the region's transit hub, the deepest talent pool, the widest choice of buildings and unmatched amenities. The O'Hare corridor and suburban office markets such as Oak Brook, Schaumburg and Deerfield suit firms whose staff and clients drive, or that need airport access above all. Within downtown, the decision is often between the value and classic addresses of the Central Loop, the commuter convenience of the West Loop and the energy of Fulton Market.
