In 30 seconds

  • Who it suits: tech, film and visual effects, mining and resource head offices, and trade firms facing the Pacific
  • The main office cores: the Financial District and Coal Harbour, with Yaletown, Gastown and Railtown for character space and the Broadway corridor beyond
  • The one lease quirk to know: property tax, assessed on a site's highest and best use, can be a large part of your additional rent — and GST applies to rent, PST does not

Vancouver's office core fills the northern half of the downtown peninsula. The Financial District runs along Burrard Street, West Georgia and West Hastings down to Waterfront Station, and Coal Harbour extends it west along the water to the convention centre. To the south-east, Yaletown's brick warehouses and newer mid-rises house tech and creative firms; to the east, Gastown and Railtown offer heritage and light-industrial space on the edge of the Downtown Eastside. Across False Creek, the Broadway corridor and Mount Pleasant form a second office hub around the city's main hospital and City Hall.

Geography shapes everything. Water on three sides, protected view corridors to the mountains and a strong residential pull on the peninsula keep the supply of office land tight, so new towers are tall and built carefully. Demand comes from technology companies large and small, film and visual-effects studios, mining and resource firms, the port and the trade and logistics businesses around it, and professional services. Hybrid work has added sublease space and made tenants choosier, with the newest, greenest and best-located buildings in demand and older towers more willing to negotiate.

Retail and restaurant corridors

Robson Street is the main shopping street, lined with flagships and busy with tourists and locals every day, especially at weekends. The shopping centre at Granville and Georgia connects underground to SkyTrain and does strong trade in the rainy months, while Alberni Street is the luxury strip. Granville Street's entertainment district is about evenings and weekends: bars, clubs, theatres and late-night food. Gastown's Water Street draws tourists and suits design-led, gift and restaurant tenants; Yaletown's patios fill on summer evenings; and Davie and Denman streets serve the dense West End with groceries, cafés and services. Chinatown has a loyal following for bakeries, barbecue and heritage-minded retail. The cruise season, roughly spring to autumn, brings day visitors along the Canada Place–Gastown–Robson axis, and outdoor footfall drops in the wet winter months. Storefronts near the Downtown Eastside face higher security and cleaning costs, so budget for them.

Coworking and flexible space

Flexible workspace is easy to find. Operators cluster in Financial District towers for corporate satellite teams, and in Gastown, Yaletown and Mount Pleasant for start-ups, creative firms and remote workers. Expect hot desks and dedicated desks on monthly memberships, and private offices for two to twenty people on terms of a few months to a year, with internet, furniture, meeting rooms and kitchens included; many also offer bike storage and showers. Landlords increasingly offer furnished, move-in-ready suites on shorter leases, a useful middle ground for growing teams in a market where conventional space can be tight.

How commercial leases work here

Commercial leases in British Columbia are mostly a matter of contract, with the province's Commercial Tenancy Act in the background. Office and retail leases are usually net: base rent per square foot per year on rentable area, plus additional rent for your share of operating costs and property taxes, estimated monthly and reconciled annually. Property tax deserves attention. BC Assessment values property at market value, taking a site's highest and best use into account, so a low-rise building on land zoned for a tower can carry a heavy tax bill that tenants pay through additional rent; relief measures for small businesses have been introduced, so ask whether your property qualifies. Leases longer than three years are commonly registered at the Land Title Office so they bind a future owner.

Strata-titled commercial units are common in Metro Vancouver, so you may lease from an individual owner rather than an institutional landlord, and strata bylaws on use, signage, hours and ventilation will apply. Negotiation centres on free rent, tenant-improvement allowances, renewal options and caps on controllable costs.

Conventions vary by building and landlord. Treat this as orientation, not legal advice, and have a local lawyer review any lease.

Getting your team there

TransLink's SkyTrain is the backbone: the Expo and Millennium lines run through the core to Burnaby, New Westminster and Surrey, and the Canada Line links Waterfront, the city centre and Yaletown with Richmond and the airport. The SeaBus brings North Shore commuters to Waterfront Station, the West Coast Express commuter train runs on weekdays from the Fraser Valley, and frequent buses fill the gaps. Cycling is a real option, with protected lanes across downtown, the seawall and secure bike rooms in many buildings. Driving is hardest: no freeway enters downtown, the bridges back up at rush hour and parking is scarce and expensive. The Financial District, Coal Harbour and Yaletown are the easiest to reach by transit; Railtown and parts of the Flats rely more on buses and bikes.

What to check before you sign

  • Seismic performance: Vancouver is earthquake country, so ask about the building's age, structural upgrades and insurance — especially in older masonry buildings in Gastown and Chinatown.
  • Property tax exposure: check the assessed value and how much of your additional rent is tax, particularly in low-rise buildings on redevelopment sites.
  • Heritage rules: Gastown and Chinatown have heritage zoning and design guidelines that govern storefronts, signage and alterations.
  • Building envelope: in a rainy climate, ask about roof and window condition and any history of leaks.
  • Strata bylaws: if the unit is strata-titled, read the bylaws on permitted uses, signage, hours and restaurant ventilation.
  • Security and cleaning: near the Downtown Eastside, factor in shutters, lighting, insurance and the owner's obligation to remove graffiti promptly.
  • Loading and HVAC: many buildings are served from rear lanes, so check loading access, freight elevators and after-hours heating and cooling charges.

Hosting events downtown

The Vancouver Convention Centre's two waterfront buildings handle large conferences and trade shows, with harbour views that sell themselves, and hotel ballrooms across the Financial District and Coal Harbour cover galas and meetings. For something different, there are heritage spaces and galleries in Gastown, the Roundhouse community centre in a former railway building in Yaletown, the civic theatres, rooftop terraces, waterfront venues on Granville Island and pavilions in Stanley Park. Summer and the cruise season are the peak, with spring and autumn busy for conferences and the wet winter months the best value. Events on streets and plazas need City of Vancouver permits, parks have their own booking rules, and serving alcohol at a one-off event requires a special event permit from the provincial Liquor and Cannabis Regulation Branch.

Is downtown the right base?

Downtown is the right base for firms that need client access, the region's transit hub and a talent pool that increasingly lives within walking or cycling distance. The alternatives are well connected too. The Broadway corridor and Mount Pleasant offer a more neighbourhood feel near the hospitals and tech firms, Burnaby's Metrotown and Brentwood town centres sit on SkyTrain with lower occupancy costs, Richmond suits airport-linked and trade businesses, and Surrey City Centre serves the fast-growing south-east of the region. If your clients are in the towers and your team rides SkyTrain, start your search downtown. For the wider picture, see the downtown Vancouver guide.
