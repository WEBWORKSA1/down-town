In 30 seconds

  • Who it suits: energy, engineering, law and finance firms, and the businesses that serve them
  • The main office cores: the west side of the CBD around Louisiana and Smith streets, with Uptown, the Energy Corridor and Greenway Plaza competing outside downtown
  • The one lease quirk to know: towers usually quote full-service gross with a base-year expense stop, and parking is a separate monthly contract, so negotiate both

Downtown Houston's office market is concentrated in a few dozen towers on the west side of the central business district, where Louisiana, Smith and Travis streets form a canyon of energy-company and bank headquarters close to the Theater District and the I-45 freeway. Main Street, with its light-rail line, runs through older and smaller buildings, several now converted to flats and hotels, while the east side around Discovery Green and the ballpark has added newer mixed-use towers. Underneath, about six miles of pedestrian tunnels connect many of the buildings, which shapes where shops and food courts locate.

The market's character comes from energy. Demand rises and falls with the oil and gas cycle, and big moves by a handful of companies can swing availability across the core. Much of the stock dates from the 1970s and 1980s boom, and tenants have been moving into the newest or most heavily renovated towers, leaving older buildings to reposition, cut deals or consider conversion. Law firms, banks, engineering firms and a growing number of energy-transition businesses round out the tenant base, and Houston is unusual among large American cities in having many competing office districts rather than one dominant downtown.

Where the offices are

West CBD (Louisiana, Smith and Travis)The tallest towers and the most prestigious floors, home to energy majors, law firms and banks, all on the tunnel network. The choice for client-facing firms that want a downtown address and freeway access.
Main Street and the historic coreOlder mid-rise and early 20th-century buildings with smaller floorplates, some converted to residential. Better value and character for creative firms and small professional offices, right on the Red Line.
East side and the convention districtNewer mixed-use towers near Discovery Green, the ballpark and the arena, with amenity-rich floors that help with recruiting. Good for tech, coworking and companies that want residential and nightlife nearby.
Midtown and the innovation districtA short Red Line ride south, anchored by a Rice University innovation hub with flexible floors, event space and startup programmes. A fit for early-stage companies and corporate innovation teams.
Beyond downtownUptown and the Galleria, the Energy Corridor along I-10 west, Greenway Plaza, Westchase and the Woodlands all offer large office stocks. Compare them seriously if most of your staff live in the western or northern suburbs.

Retail and restaurant corridors

Downtown retail works differently from most American city centres because so much of the weekday trade happens underground. The tunnel's food courts, pharmacies, dry cleaners and service counters do heavy business at lunchtime from Monday to Friday and are largely closed in the evenings and at weekends. At street level, the bars and restaurants around Market Square and along Main Street draw an evening and weekend crowd, while the blocks near the convention centre, the ballpark, the arena and Discovery Green see big spikes on event days and conventions. EaDo's breweries and bars rely on soccer nights and the growing residential population, and Midtown, just south, has a more consistent nightlife trade.

Fashion and big-box retail have largely gone to the Galleria and suburban centres, so downtown suits food and drink, convenience retail and services for office workers and residents. Restaurants need city health and building permits and, for alcohol, the right permit from the Texas Alcoholic Beverage Commission. Coworking and flexible space

Flexible offices cluster in three places: amenity floors and flexible suites inside downtown towers, often run by the landlord; the newer mixed-use buildings on the east side; and Midtown's innovation district. There is more supply in the Galleria and Energy Corridor markets, where many consultancies and project teams are based. Typical options are hot desks and dedicated desks on monthly memberships, private offices on agreements of a few months to a year, meeting rooms by the hour and virtual-office addresses. Flexible space suits project teams working on energy deals, out-of-town firms testing the market and startups that expect to grow quickly. How commercial leases work here

Houston follows standard American office conventions. Rent is quoted per rentable square foot per year, which means the area you pay for includes a share of the building's common areas, the load factor, on top of the usable space you occupy. Most downtown towers quote full-service gross rent: the landlord pays operating expenses and property taxes up to the level of a base year, and you pay your share of any increases after that. Some landlords quote a net rent plus estimated operating expenses instead, and retail and industrial space is almost always triple-net, with the tenant paying its share of taxes, insurance and common-area maintenance on top of base rent.

Landlords compete with concessions rather than headline rent: a tenant-improvement allowance per square foot towards fit-out, a period of free rent at the start, and sometimes moving allowances, with more on offer for longer terms and stronger tenants. Small or young companies are often asked for a personal guarantee or a larger deposit; larger tenants may provide a letter of credit. Getting your team there

Houston commuting is built around the car and the express bus. METRO's large park-and-ride network brings commuters into downtown from the suburbs on express buses using managed freeway lanes, which makes the core easier to reach than its freeway congestion suggests. METRORail's Red Line runs along Main Street to Midtown, the Museum District and the Texas Medical Center, and the Green and Purple lines run east. Most towers have their own garages or connected parking, and the tunnel links many garages to offices, so staff rarely need to walk outside in summer. The Buffalo Bayou trails and BCycle bike share help the minority who cycle. The west CBD is easiest for park-and-ride and freeway commuters, Main Street for rail, and the east side for the Green and Purple lines.

What to check before you sign

  • Flood history and resilience. Ask how the building and its garage fared in past floods, check the Harris County Flood Control District's maps, and confirm where electrical rooms and backup generators sit.
  • Storm and casualty clauses. Make sure the lease covers rent abatement if the building is closed after a hurricane, the landlord's duty to restore and how long it has, and pair it with your own business-interruption insurance.
  • The base year and expense stop. Insist that the base year is grossed up to full occupancy, so a half-empty tower does not inflate your share later, and ask for audit rights and caps on controllable expenses.
  • After-hours cooling. In Houston's climate, after-hours HVAC charges per hour and per zone add up quickly; negotiate included hours if your team works late or at weekends.
  • Parking terms. Confirm the number of spaces, reserved versus unreserved, visitor validation and how monthly rates can rise during the term.
  • Deed restrictions and permits. No zoning does not mean no rules: confirm your use is permitted under any deed restrictions and city ordinances, and check signage limits before you design.
  • Sublease and older-tower risk. Energy-company subleases can be good value but tie you to someone else's lease; in older towers, ask about planned renovations, elevator upgrades and any conversion plans. See our sublease guide.

Hosting events downtown

The George R. Brown Convention Center and its connected hotels handle the largest conferences, and hotel ballrooms across downtown take everything from board dinners to product launches. The Theater District's halls and lobbies suit evening receptions, historic buildings such as the Julia Ideson Building offer character for smaller gatherings, and the sports venues have club and suite spaces for corporate hospitality. Discovery Green and the bayou-side parks host outdoor events, which need permits from the park operators and, for street closures, the city's special-events office; serving alcohol requires temporary permits under state rules. Spring and autumn are peak season. Summer outdoor events work only in the evening, hurricane season calls for a contingency plan, and the rodeo in late February and March squeezes hotel rooms across the city.

Is downtown the right base?

Downtown wins on prestige, transit, the tunnel's amenities and access to theatres, sport and residential towers that help with recruitment, and it remains the natural home for energy headquarters, banks and the law firms that serve them. Uptown and the Galleria offer shopping, hotels and shorter commutes for west-side staff, the Energy Corridor suits campus-style energy operations near Katy, the Texas Medical Center is the obvious choice for healthcare and life sciences, and the Woodlands appeals to firms whose people live north. If you want a walkable, connected address with culture on the doorstep, downtown is the strongest option; read the downtown Houston guide to see how the area feels after hours.
