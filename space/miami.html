In 30 seconds

  • Suits international banks, wealth managers, law firms, trade and logistics companies, and finance and tech firms relocating to a bilingual gateway to Latin America
  • The main cores are Brickell and the downtown core, with Edgewater, Wynwood and the Design District for newer and creative space
  • The quirk to know: insurance and storm-readiness are big cost lines — check how windstorm and flood premiums pass through to tenants

Downtown Miami's commercial geography is split by the Miami River. To the south, Brickell Avenue's towers form the financial district, mixing offices with dense residential high-rises, hotels and restaurants. North of the river, the older downtown core around Flagler Street and Government Center holds the county government, the courts and the law firms that serve them, with the bayfront parks, museums and arena along Biscayne Boulevard. Further north, the Arts & Entertainment District and Edgewater have new mixed-use towers, and Wynwood and the Design District have added creative offices and luxury retail.

The market's character is international and fast-changing. Brickell has long hosted banks with Latin American and private-banking businesses, and in recent years it has drawn finance and tech firms relocating from other US cities, helping to fuel construction of new trophy towers. Older downtown buildings are more mixed, and some are being repurposed. Demand drivers include Latin American trade and capital, the port and airport, Florida's lack of a state personal income tax, and a deep bilingual workforce; the main constraints are cost, traffic and the expense of insuring buildings against storms.

Where the offices are

BrickellThe financial district: the newest towers along Brickell Avenue and around the Metromover's Brickell loop, home to international banks, wealth managers, investment firms and law firms. Expect the area's highest office costs and strong restaurant and residential amenities.
Downtown core and Flagler StreetGovernment offices, courthouses and the legal, accounting and civic organisations around them, in older towers and historic buildings. Metrorail's Government Center hub makes it the easiest district to reach by train.
Arts & Entertainment District and EdgewaterOffice floors in new mixed-use towers along Biscayne Boulevard, near the performing arts centre, suited to media, professional services and firms that want bay views at a discount to Brickell.
WynwoodNewer mid-rise creative offices among the murals and galleries, popular with tech, design and consumer brands. Lively, but transit is limited, so check parking.
Design DistrictBoutique office space above luxury flagships, suited to fashion, design, architecture and small headquarters that value the address.

Retail and restaurant corridors

Brickell's restaurant rows along South Miami Avenue and Brickell Avenue, and the large open-air shopping centre at its heart, serve office workers by day and residents at night, with busy weekend brunch and nightlife trade. Flagler Street, the historic main street of the downtown core, mixes long-standing independent shops with a slow revitalisation. Biscayne Boulevard's bayfront draws visitors, concert-goers and cruise passengers, while Wynwood's streets fill with gallery visitors, diners and bar crowds, especially at weekends. The Design District is a luxury flagship destination, and Calle Ocho in Little Havana thrives on cafés and visitors. Footfall peaks in the winter high season and during event weeks such as Miami Art Week, then slows in the summer heat. Bilingual service is close to essential.

Coworking and flexible space

Flexible workspace is heavily concentrated in Brickell, with more in the downtown core, Edgewater and Wynwood. Options run from hot desks and dedicated desks on monthly memberships to private offices for small teams on terms from a month to a couple of years. Many companies relocating to Miami use flexible space as a landing pad while they hire and search for permanent offices, and Latin American firms use it to establish a US presence. Virtual-office plans provide a Florida business address and mail handling.

How commercial leases work here

Miami follows US conventions, with a few Florida twists. Office rent is quoted per rentable square foot per year, but listings vary: some towers quote full-service rent that includes operating costs and taxes for a base year, while others quote base rent plus an estimated operating-expense charge per square foot, so check which before comparing. Retail is usually triple net (NNN), with the tenant paying a share of property taxes, insurance and common-area maintenance. Insurance is a significant and volatile part of operating costs in South Florida, and windstorm and flood premiums are generally passed through to tenants.

Landlords commonly offer a tenant improvement allowance and free rent scaled to the term and tenant's credit. Small and new businesses are often asked for the first and last month's rent plus a security deposit, or a letter of credit, and a personal guarantee. Florida's state sales tax on commercial rent was repealed with effect from October 2025; leases signed before then may still reference it, so check older documents and confirm current rules with an accountant. Businesses also need a Certificate of Use from the City of Miami and local business tax receipts from both the city and Miami-Dade County.

Getting your team there

Metrorail serves Government Center and Brickell stations, connecting to the airport, the southern suburbs and Hialeah. The free Metromover links the downtown core with Brickell and the Omni area near Edgewater, so a tower near a Metromover station is a real advantage. Brightline and Tri-Rail trains from Broward and Palm Beach counties stop at the downtown rail station, and Metrobus and the City of Miami's free trolleys fill the gaps. Even so, most staff drive, often on congested routes such as I-95, and drawbridges over the Miami River add unpredictability. Parking in Brickell is expensive, so check what the building offers. In summer heat, covered parking and short, shaded walks matter more than they would elsewhere.

What to check before you sign

  • Flood risk: the flood zone, the building's elevation and its record during king tides and storms, especially for ground-floor retail.
  • Hurricane terms: how the lease handles casualty, rent abatement and closures, and whether the building has impact-resistant glass, protected roofing and enough backup power.
  • Insurance pass-throughs: how windstorm and flood premiums are allocated and whether there are any caps on yearly increases.
  • Recertification: the status of any periodic safety recertification for older buildings, and planned works or special assessments.
  • Zoning and permits: the Certificate of Use and business tax receipts for your use under the city's zoning code, especially for food, nightlife or medical.
  • Parking ratios, monthly costs, valet and rideshare drop-off arrangements.
  • Ownership: many offices are individually owned condo units, so check the owner's standing and association rules.

Hosting events downtown

Large conventions and fairs mostly go to the Miami Beach Convention Center across the bay, while downtown hotel ballrooms suit conferences, dinners and corporate meetings. The Adrienne Arsht Center, the bayfront museum terraces in Maurice A. Ferré Park and the city's rooftop venues offer dramatic settings for receptions, and Bayfront Park hosts large outdoor concerts and festivals. Wynwood's warehouses and galleries work for launches and parties, and the bay is a natural setting for boat-based events. Winter is peak season, and Miami Art Week in December books venues and hotels far in advance. Summer brings heat, daily thunderstorms and hurricane season, so outdoor events need indoor backup and a storm plan. Events in public parks and streets require permits from the city, and county facilities have their own process.

Is downtown the right base?

For finance, banking, law and firms that want the strongest international address, Brickell is the obvious choice, and the downtown core makes sense for anything that deals with government and the courts. Edgewater and Wynwood offer newer creative space with a different energy, though transit is thinner. Coral Gables, with its multinational regional offices and consulates, is the main alternative for Latin American headquarters, while Miami Beach and Fort Lauderdale appeal to firms closer to where their staff live or play.
