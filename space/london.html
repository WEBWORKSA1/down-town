In 30 seconds

  • Who it suits: financial, legal, professional, tech and creative firms that need a top address, deep talent pools and unbeatable transport
  • The main office cores: the City, Midtown, the West End, King's Cross, Shoreditch and London Bridge
  • The one lease quirk to know: the headline rent is only part of the cost — service charge, business rates and often VAT sit on top

Central London's commercial geography stretches from Mayfair in the west to Aldgate in the east. The City of London, the Square Mile, is the historic financial core and is governed by its own local authority, the City of London Corporation. West of it, Holborn and Farringdon form a Midtown belt of law firms and professional services, and the West End mixes prestige offices with the country's flagship shops, theatres and restaurants. North and east, King's Cross and Shoreditch hold technology and creative firms, while the towers around London Bridge extend the office market south of the river.

The market's defining trend is a flight to quality. Occupiers increasingly want new or comprehensively refurbished buildings with strong energy ratings, good daylight, terraces, end-of-trip cycling facilities and flexible floors, and they will pay a clear premium over older stock to get them. Hybrid working has pushed firms toward smaller but better spaces near the best-connected stations, a trend reinforced by the Elizabeth line, which transformed access to Farringdon, Liverpool Street, Tottenham Court Road and Bond Street. Older, less efficient buildings face tougher competition and, increasingly, energy-efficiency rules.

Where the offices are

The City of LondonBanks, the insurance cluster around Lime Street, law firms and global professional services, in everything from Victorian chambers to the towers of the eastern cluster. Served by Bank, Liverpool Street, Moorgate and Cannon Street. The natural choice for financial and legal firms.
Midtown: Holborn and FarringdonLaw firms near the Inns of Court and the Royal Courts of Justice, plus media, architecture and professional services. Farringdon's Elizabeth line and Thameslink interchange makes it one of the best-connected spots in the capital.
Mayfair and St James'sInvestment managers, private equity, family offices and luxury brands in period townhouses and small, high-specification buildings. Floorplates are small, addresses are prestigious and the premium is steep.
Soho, Fitzrovia and MaryleboneMedia, advertising, post-production, fashion and creative agencies in converted warehouses and mid-sized buildings, with restaurants and bars on the doorstep.
King's CrossA large regeneration district behind King's Cross and St Pancras, with tech and life-sciences campuses, new public squares and direct trains to Europe.
Shoreditch and Old StreetStartups, tech scale-ups and creative studios in warehouse conversions and new mid-rise blocks on the City fringe. Good for younger teams that want character and a slightly lower cost base than the core.

Retail and restaurant corridors

Oxford Street, Regent Street and Bond Street are the flagship shopping streets, with the heaviest footfall on weekends, in the run-up to Christmas and during the summer tourist season. Covent Garden and Carnaby Street suit fashion, beauty and experiential brands, and Soho is London's densest restaurant and bar district. The City behaves very differently: around Cheapside, Bank, Leadenhall and Liverpool Street, cafés, sandwich bars and pubs depend on weekday office workers, with trade peaking from Tuesday to Thursday and falling away sharply at weekends. Borough Market and London Bridge draw food lovers all week. Shops, cafés, restaurants, offices and gyms mostly sit in the flexible Class E planning use class, so many changes of use need no planning permission, but hot-food takeaways, pubs and bars need their own consent, and any business selling alcohol needs a premises licence from the council. Westminster applies a stricter licensing policy in the busiest parts of the West End.

Coworking and flexible space

Flexible workspace is a mature, highly competitive market in central London. Operators cluster around Liverpool Street, Old Street, Farringdon, King's Cross, Holborn, Soho and Mayfair, from hot-desking floors to discreet serviced suites aimed at investment firms. Typical options are a hot desk or dedicated desk on a rolling monthly agreement, or a private office on a fixed term of a few months to a year, with business rates, service charge, utilities, cleaning and internet bundled into one fee. A growing middle ground is the managed office: a floor fitted out to your brand on a one- to three-year agreement, with the operator handling the building. Flexible space usually costs more per desk than a conventional lease but saves on fit-out, deposits and long commitments.

How commercial leases work here

English commercial leases are negotiated contracts with strong conventions. Most are full repairing and insuring (FRI): the tenant bears the cost of repairs and insurance, directly for a whole building or through the service charge and an insurance rent in a multi-let one. Longer leases usually have upward-only rent reviews every five years, and many include a tenant break option, often conditional on paying all rent and giving vacant possession, so the conditions matter as much as the date. Under Part II of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1954, business tenants have a statutory right to renew when the lease ends unless the landlord proves a specified ground, but the parties can contract out of that protection before the lease is granted using a warning notice and a declaration by the tenant. Short and flexible leases are commonly contracted out. The RICS Code for Leasing Business Premises sets out what landlords' surveyors should cover in heads of terms.

Typical office lease5–10 years, often with a tenant break around the midpoint; 3–5 years for small suites; 10–15 years or more for large floors
Rent is quotedPer square foot per year, exclusive of service charge, business rates and VAT; usually paid quarterly in advance on the quarter days
Building costsFRI terms: a service charge covers common parts, building services and management, plus an insurance rent; the tenant pays its own utilities
DepositOften none for strong covenants; three to six months' rent or a parent-company guarantee is common for newer or smaller firms
Fit-outLandlords usually deliver Category A (raised floors, ceilings, lighting, air conditioning); tenants fund Category B (partitions, kitchens, IT), often offset by a rent-free period
TaxesThe occupier pays business rates to the local council; VAT (currently 20%) applies to rent and service charge where the landlord has opted to tax; longer leases can trigger Stamp Duty Land Tax

Conventions vary by building and landlord. Treat this as orientation, not legal advice, and have a local lawyer review any lease.

Getting your team there

Central London is served by almost every Underground line, the Elizabeth line, the DLR and a ring of mainline termini: Liverpool Street, Fenchurch Street, Cannon Street, London Bridge, Blackfriars and Moorgate for the City, and King's Cross St Pancras, Euston, Paddington, Victoria, Waterloo and Charing Cross around the West End. Thameslink runs north–south through Farringdon, City Thameslink and London Bridge. Farringdon, Liverpool Street and Tottenham Court Road are the best-connected office locations, thanks to the Elizabeth line. Cycleways and cycle hire make cycling a realistic commute, and good buildings now offer secure bike storage, showers and lockers as standard. Staff and visitor parking is minimal and expensive, the Congestion Charge applies across the centre and the Ultra Low Emission Zone covers all of Greater London, so assume your team arrives by public transport.

What to check before you sign

  • Energy rating. Under the Minimum Energy Efficiency Standards, most commercial property cannot continue to be let with an EPC rating below E, and the government has proposed tightening the threshold. Ask what upgrades are planned and who will pay.
  • Service charge. Request the budget, past reconciliations, any sinking fund and any cap, and check that the landlord follows the RICS professional statement on service charges.
  • Business rates. Look up the rateable value with the Valuation Office Agency before you agree terms. Rates are a major cost, larger London properties also pay a Crossrail business rate supplement, and some smaller businesses qualify for relief.
  • VAT position. If the landlord has opted to tax and your business is VAT-exempt, as many banks, insurers and some other financial firms are, you cannot recover the VAT on rent, which effectively adds 20% to your occupancy cost.
  • 1954 Act status and break conditions. Confirm whether the lease is inside or outside the Act, and make sure any break conditions are achievable.
  • Dilapidations. Agree a photographic schedule of condition at the start so you are not paying to put right problems that predate you when the lease ends.
  • Listed buildings and conservation areas. Much of the City and West End is in conservation areas, and alterations to listed buildings need listed building consent; new signage usually needs advertisement consent.

Hosting events downtown

Central London has venues for every scale. The Barbican Centre and the Queen Elizabeth II Centre in Westminster handle conferences, while the City's historic livery halls and the Guildhall suit dinners and receptions with ceremony. Museums, galleries and cultural centres along the South Bank hire out spaces after hours, hotel ballrooms cluster around Park Lane and the West End, and rooftop terraces and converted warehouses in Shoreditch and Southwark suit product launches and parties. The Christmas party season books out early, and summer terraces are in demand from May to September. Events on public land need permission from the relevant local authority or, in the royal parks, from The Royal Parks, and a one-off event serving alcohol without a premises licence needs a Temporary Event Notice filed with the council in advance.

Is downtown the right base?

For firms that live on client meetings, recruitment and reputation, central London is hard to beat: the talent pool, transport links and address carry real weight. Cost is the trade-off. Canary Wharf offers big, modern floors and the Elizabeth line at a discount to the City, Stratford and White City offer newer campuses at lower occupancy costs, and outer hubs such as Croydon, Hammersmith or regional cities can cut costs further for back-office teams. Many growing firms combine a smaller central office for collaboration with cheaper space elsewhere. For neighbourhoods, transport and daily life, read the downtown London guide.
