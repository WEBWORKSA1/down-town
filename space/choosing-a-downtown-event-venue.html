A downtown venue has advantages no out-of-town conference hotel can match: transit at the door, hotels and restaurants within a short walk, and an address people recognise. It also has constraints. Loading bays are tight, curfews are stricter where people live nearby, and the street outside may be closed for a parade on the one day you need it. Choosing well means reading past the photos to the capacity, the contract and the logistics.

This guide covers how to size a room for your layout, what you will actually pay, the contract terms that matter, and a checklist for the site visit. The space figures are planners' rules of thumb rather than standards, and local fire codes always take precedence. Our city space pages, such as London, New York and Dubai, describe the local venue types and seasons. When you have a shortlist, run the costs through the venue budget tool.

Capacity by layout

A venue's advertised capacity is usually the maximum permitted by its fire certificate or occupancy permit, typically for the densest possible arrangement. Your real capacity depends on the layout and on what else has to fit in the room.

LayoutRule-of-thumb area per guestGood for
Theatre (rows of chairs)About 0.6–1.0 m² (6–10 sq ft)Keynotes, launches, briefings
Classroom (rows of tables)About 1.7–1.9 m² (18–20 sq ft)Training, workshops, note-taking sessions
Banquet (rounds of 8–10)About 1.0–1.3 m² (10–14 sq ft)Dinners, awards, galas
Cabaret (rounds with one side open to the stage)About 1.4–1.9 m² (15–20 sq ft)Conferences that mix talks and group work
Boardroom or U-shapeAbout 2.3–3.7 m² (25–40 sq ft)Small meetings and discussion groups
Standing receptionAbout 0.5–0.9 m² (5–10 sq ft)Drinks, networking, parties

Before you apply these, subtract the space for the stage and lectern, screens and projection throw, the technical desk, bars and buffet stations, a dance floor, a cloakroom and any exhibition stands. For receptions, use the generous end of the range if there will be food stations or high tables, and plan seating for a share of guests: older or less mobile guests, and anyone at a long event, will want somewhere to sit. Ask the venue for scaled floor plans of your specific layout rather than relying on its capacity chart.

What you will actually pay

Hire fees

Room hire may be charged by the hour, by the session or by the day, and set-up and breakdown time may or may not be included. Exclusive use of a whole venue costs more but removes the risk of another event sharing your lobby or toilets. Check overtime rates if you overrun.

Food and beverage minimums

Hotels and many restaurants and event spaces charge little or nothing for the room if you commit to a minimum spend on food and drink. If you spend less, you pay the difference. Ask whether the minimum is measured before or after service charges and tax, and what counts toward it. In North America in particular, a service charge is often added to catering, and it is not necessarily a gratuity for staff; ask what it covers and whether tax is applied to it as well.

Packages and delegate rates

In the UK, Europe and parts of Asia-Pacific, daytime meetings are often sold as a day delegate rate: a per-person price covering the room, breaks, lunch and basic AV. A 24-hour rate adds accommodation and dinner. Packages simplify budgeting but come with minimum numbers, and "basic AV" may mean a projector and little else.

Numbers and attrition

Most venues ask for a final guaranteed number a few days before the event, and you pay for that number even if fewer people arrive. If you have also booked a block of hotel rooms, an attrition clause sets how many can be released without penalty and by when. Negotiate the release dates and the permitted shortfall before signing, not after registrations disappoint.

Holds, deposits and the contract

Venues usually let you place a provisional hold, sometimes called a first option, on a date. If another client wants the same date, you get a short period to confirm or release it. A second option means someone else is ahead of you. Holds expire, so diarise the deadline.

Once you confirm, expect a contract and a deposit, often a substantial share of the estimated total on signing, with further instalments as the date approaches. Read these clauses closely:

  • Cancellation. Most contracts use a sliding scale: cancel early and you lose the deposit; cancel late and you may owe most or all of the total. Check whether the venue must try to resell the date and credit you if it succeeds.
  • Force majeure. Which events excuse both sides: severe weather, government orders, transit strikes, a public-health emergency? Is the remedy a refund, a credit or a rescheduled date?
  • Changes. Can you move to a smaller room, change the date or reduce numbers, and at what cost?
  • Exclusive suppliers. Many venues require you to use their own caterers, AV company or furniture supplier, or charge a fee for outside vendors. Find out before you compare quotes.
  • Damage and cleaning. What counts as damage, how it is assessed, and whether a separate security deposit applies.
  • Construction and other bookings. Ask whether building works or another event are scheduled nearby on your date. You may not be able to stop them, but you can price the risk.

Audio-visual and technical requirements

AV is where budgets drift. Start with a written specification: number of presenters, microphones (handheld, lapel, lectern), screens and their size, confidence monitors for speakers, recording or livestreaming, interpretation and captioning. Then check the room can support it. Is there enough power, and on how many circuits? Is the ceiling high enough for a screen to be seen from the back row? Are there rigging points for lights or speakers? Can the room be blacked out, or will afternoon sun wash out the projection? For hybrid events, ask for a dedicated, wired internet connection with stated upload bandwidth rather than relying on the guest Wi-Fi. If you bring your own AV company, ask about any patch or connection fees for using the venue's systems.

Access: guests, kit and the street

Accessibility

Check step-free routes from the street to every space you will use, including the stage. Look for lifts large enough for a wheelchair and a companion, accessible toilets close to the event rooms, a hearing loop or assistive listening system, seating with space for wheelchair users throughout rather than only at the back, and somewhere quiet for guests who need a break. Accessibility law applies to venues in most countries, but standards vary and older downtown buildings may have partial access. Ask specifically rather than relying on a general statement.

Load-in and load-out

Ask where vehicles unload, the dimensions of the loading dock and the goods lift, how far it is from the dock to the room, and what hours access is allowed. Downtown streets often restrict loading times and kerbside stopping, and some venues must book delivery slots with building management. In some North American convention centres and theatres, rules may require specific labour for rigging or handling. Find out how much set-up and breakdown time is included in the hire.

Getting people there

Map the nearest rail, metro and bus stops and their late-evening frequency, a drop-off point for taxis and accessible vehicles, and parking for those who must drive. Check the city's calendar for marathons, parades, festivals and big matches that close roads or fill hotels; our events page and city guides such as Chicago, Barcelona and Singapore list the major recurring ones by season.

Noise, licensing and insurance

Downtown venues increasingly sit next to apartments. Municipal noise by-laws, the conditions on a venue's licence and the landlord's own rules can impose curfews on amplified music, earlier finishing times for terraces and rooftops, and limits on guests drinking outside. Ask for the exact times music must stop and guests must leave, and whether the venue measures sound levels. If you plan to serve alcohol, confirm the venue's licence covers your event, or whether you need a temporary permit.

Most venues require proof of public or general liability insurance, and in North America often ask to be named as an additional insured. Check whether your existing business policy covers events, whether suppliers carry their own cover, and whether liquor liability is included if you supply drinks. For a major event, event cancellation insurance can cover costs if you have to cancel for reasons outside your control. Read the exclusions carefully.

Venue-visit checklist

Visit at the same time of day as your event, and ideally on the same day of the week. Bring a tape measure and your floor plan.

  • Floor plans for your exact layout, with stage, screens, bars and buffets drawn in.
  • Fire-certified capacity for that layout, and how many exits and stewards are required.
  • All fees in writing: hire, minimum spend, service charges, tax, overtime, outside-vendor fees.
  • Hold expiry, deposit schedule, cancellation scale and force majeure wording.
  • Exclusive supplier rules for catering, AV and furniture.
  • Power, internet bandwidth, rigging, ceiling height, blackout and acoustics in the actual room.
  • Step-free access, lifts, accessible toilets and assistive listening.
  • Loading dock, goods lift dimensions, access hours and the route to the room.
  • Music curfew, final departure time and any terrace or rooftop restrictions.
  • Insurance certificates required, and by when.
  • Toilets, cloakroom and coat capacity for your numbers in winter.
  • Mobile signal inside the room, especially in basements.
  • Other events, building works or street closures scheduled on your date.

Once a shortlist passes, ask each venue for a written proposal on the same brief and compare them line by line. For ideas on tying your event to the wider downtown calendar, see using downtown events.
