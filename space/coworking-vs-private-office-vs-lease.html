Choosing between a coworking membership, a serviced private office and a conventional lease is not really a question about which is cheapest. It is a question about where you want to carry risk. Flexible space charges a premium every month in exchange for taking the upfront cost, the commitment and the management burden off your hands. A lease asks you to fund the fit-out and sign for years, and pays you back with a lower running cost and full control. Which trade suits you depends on your headcount, how confident you are about the next few years, and what the space needs to say about your business.

This guide compares the three models side by side, shows the break-even logic in plain numbers, and notes where conventions change from one region to another. The figures in the worked example are illustrative units, not market prices; plug your own quotes into the coworking vs lease calculator to see where your line falls.

The three models in plain terms

Coworking

You buy access to a desk in a shared space: a hot desk you take on arrival, or a dedicated desk that is yours. Memberships are typically monthly, and the price includes internet, cleaning, kitchen, some meeting-room credit and community events. It is the lowest commitment and the lowest privacy.

Serviced or private office

A lockable office or suite inside a flexible centre, priced per desk or per room per month on terms that commonly run from a few months to two years. Reception, meeting rooms, furniture, internet and building costs are usually bundled into one fee. You get a front door and a stable home for the team, while still sharing the lobby and meeting rooms.

Conventional lease

You rent space directly from the landlord on a multi-year term and are responsible for fitting it out, furnishing it, connecting it and running it. Rent is quoted per area rather than per desk, and you pay building costs, taxes and utilities on top in most markets. You get the most control and, over a long enough term, the lowest cost per person.

Two hybrids are worth knowing. A managed office is a dedicated space that a provider fits out and runs for you alone, on a multi-year all-inclusive fee. A fitted or plug-and-play lease is a conventional lease on space the landlord has already furnished and cabled, which cuts your upfront cost but usually carries a higher rent. A sublease of another company's fitted space can achieve something similar.

How the costs are built

The models are hard to compare because they bundle different things into the headline price.

  • Flexible pricing (coworking and serviced offices) is usually all-in per desk per month. Check what sits outside it: extra meeting-room hours, printing, phone lines, parking, out-of-hours access, mail handling, and tax on the fee.
  • Lease pricing starts with base rent per area, then adds some combination of service charges or operating costs, property taxes or business rates, insurance, utilities, cleaning, security and maintenance, depending on whether the lease is gross or net. Then there are the one-off costs: fit-out (net of any landlord contribution), furniture, IT and audio-visual equipment, legal and agent fees, a deposit or guarantee, moving costs, and the eventual cost of reinstating the space at the end of the term.

To compare fairly, convert both to the same unit: total cost per desk per month over the whole term, including one-off costs spread across the months you will use them.

The break-even logic

The key insight is that a lease converts a large one-off cost into a lower monthly cost. The longer you stay, the more months you have to spread that one-off cost across, and the cheaper the lease becomes per month. Flexible space has no such curve: you pay roughly the same every month whether you stay one year or five.

The break-even term is therefore:

Break-even months = one-off lease costs per desk ÷ (flexible cost per desk per month − lease running cost per desk per month)

Here is an illustration in round units rather than any currency. Suppose a flexible desk costs 100 units a month all-in. The equivalent lease costs 60 units per desk per month to run once you add rent, building costs, taxes, utilities, cleaning and IT. The one-off costs of the lease (fit-out net of the landlord's contribution, furniture, equipment, legal and agent fees, moving and a provision for reinstatement) come to 2,000 units per desk.

In this example the break-even is 2,000 ÷ (100 − 60), or 50 months. Now see how sensitive it is. If the landlord offers a larger fit-out contribution or a long rent-free period, the one-off cost falls and break-even comes sooner. If you negotiate the flexible price down to 85 units for a longer commitment, break-even stretches to 80 months. If your team is hybrid and you can share desks in a lease but must pay for every desk in a flexible centre, the lease looks better still. That is why the answer depends so heavily on your actual quotes, and why the same team can reach different conclusions in different cities.

Rules of thumb by headcount and term

  • Under about 10 people, or a horizon under two years: flexible space usually wins. The one-off costs of a lease are hard to justify, and few landlords offer meaningful incentives on very small suites.
  • About 10 to 50 people, or a horizon of two to five years: the grey zone. Run the break-even with real quotes, and look hard at managed offices, fitted leases and subleases, which can combine a lower monthly cost with little upfront spend.
  • Above about 50 people with five or more years of confidence: a conventional lease usually wins on cost and gives you the control and identity that a larger team tends to want.

Non-financial factors can override the maths. Regulated businesses may need their own network and access control. Client-facing firms may want a reception that is unmistakably theirs. A company about to raise money, or facing an uncertain year, may value the exit more than the saving.

How conventions differ by region

Lease structures change the comparison, so read local terms carefully.

  • US: office rent is quoted per square foot per year, either full-service gross (building costs included, with increases over a base year usually passed through) or net, with costs passed through from day one. Flexible fees are quoted per desk or office per month.
  • Canada: office leases are commonly net, with additional rent covering taxes, maintenance and insurance on top of base rent, plus GST, HST or QST depending on the province.
  • UK and Ireland: leases typically run five to ten years, often with break clauses, and you pay a service charge and business rates (commercial rates in Ireland) on top of rent. Flexible fees often bundle these, which makes headline comparisons misleading.
  • Continental Europe: conventions vary. France's standard commercial lease runs nine years, with the tenant generally able to leave at each three-year point; German leases often run five to ten years with indexation. Long, inflexible terms make flexible space relatively more attractive for small teams.
  • Japan: office deposits are large, often six to twelve months' rent, and rents are quoted per tsubo. Tying up that much cash pushes many small teams toward serviced offices in districts such as those covered in our Tokyo space guide.
  • Hong Kong and Singapore: conventional office terms are relatively short, often around three years, so the flexibility argument for serviced space is weaker than in markets with longer leases. See our Hong Kong and Singapore pages.
  • India: leave-and-licence agreements and sizeable security deposits are common, and flexible and managed operators are well established in business districts such as Bengaluru and Mumbai.

Everywhere, check whether VAT, GST or another sales tax applies to the rent or the membership fee, and whether your business can recover it. It can change the comparison more than you expect.

Core and flex: you do not have to choose just one

Many growing companies combine models. A common pattern is a leased core sized for the steady-state team, plus flexible memberships for project teams, new hires ahead of an expansion, or staff in other cities. Another is to start in a serviced office while you learn how your team uses space, then lease once your headcount and hybrid pattern settle down.
