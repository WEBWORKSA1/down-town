In 30 seconds

  • Who it suits: regional headquarters, financial firms and growth companies that want an Asia-Pacific base with predictable rules
  • The main office cores: Raffles Place, Marina Bay, Shenton Way and Tanjong Pagar, with the Beach Road and Bugis fringe as the value play
  • The one lease quirk to know: reinstatement — you will usually have to strip your fit-out and return the space to its original state at the end

Singapore's commercial centre is compact and almost entirely rail-served. The Central Business District runs from Raffles Place at the mouth of the Singapore River south along Shenton Way to Tanjong Pagar, and east across reclaimed land to Marina Bay. North of the river, the Civic District is largely given over to museums and government, while Beach Road, Bugis and the City Hall area form a fringe of mid-size towers, malls and mixed-use developments. Chinatown, Telok Ayer and the quays hold the small-format space: shophouse offices above restaurants and bars.

The market is shaped by planning. The Urban Redevelopment Authority's Master Plan and government land sales control what gets built and where, and policies such as the CBD Incentive Scheme encourage owners to redevelop older office blocks into mixed-use towers with homes and hotels. Demand comes from banks, asset and wealth managers, family offices, commodity traders, insurers, law firms and the regional teams of technology companies. Tenants increasingly favour newer, greener buildings, and the Building and Construction Authority's Green Mark certification is a standard part of the conversation. Most Grade A towers are held by institutional landlords and real-estate investment trusts, while some older buildings are strata-titled, with floors or units owned separately.

Where the offices are

A prestige address, though some floor plates are dated. Suits established firms that want to be at the centre of things.

Retail and restaurant corridors

Downtown retail is organised around the MRT. Malls built over or beside Raffles Place, City Hall, Esplanade and Marina Bay stations capture commuter footfall, and the underground links between them work as shopping streets in their own right. Street retail lives in the conservation districts: Chinatown's Pagoda and Temple Streets for visitors; Telok Ayer, Amoy Street, Club Street and Keong Saik Road for restaurants and bars; and Boat Quay and Clarke Quay for riverside dining and nightlife. Orchard Road, outside the core, remains the main fashion and department-store belt.

Footfall in Raffles Place and Shenton Way is intensely weekday-driven — packed at lunch, quiet on Saturdays and Sundays — whereas Marina Bay, Chinatown and the quays trade well in the evenings and at weekends. Food operators that thrive in the CBD are fast, consistent and lunch-focused; destination restaurants and cocktail bars do better in the shophouse streets. Food shops need a licence from the Singapore Food Agency, alcohol needs a liquor licence, and any change of use for a shophouse unit needs approval from the Urban Redevelopment Authority.

Coworking and flexible space

Flexible workspace is well established. Operators cluster in Grade A towers around Raffles Place and Marina Bay, along the Tanjong Pagar and Shenton Way corridor, in shophouse conversions in Telok Ayer and Chinatown, and around Beach Road and Bugis. Options run from hot desks and dedicated desks on monthly terms to fully serviced private offices on agreements of several months to two years, with meeting rooms, reception and IT included. Many centres provide a registered business address and mail handling, which helps because every Singapore company needs a local registered office. Flexible space suits foreign companies setting up while incorporation and work passes are processed, project teams and firms that expect headcount to change; once a team is stable and growing, a conventional lease usually offers better value over a full term.

How commercial leases work here

Singapore office rents are quoted per square foot per month, usually on net lettable area. Leases of two to three years are the norm for small and mid-size offices, often with an option to renew for a further term at the prevailing market rent; large occupiers and anchor tenants negotiate longer terms. Quoted rents may or may not include the service charge for common areas and air-conditioning during building hours, so confirm whether a figure is gross or net of service charge. Property tax is generally the owner's liability. GST is charged on rent and service charges where the landlord is GST-registered, as most institutional landlords are, and the tenant normally pays the stamp duty on the lease.

Before fit-out, you need the right approvals. The unit must be authorised by the Urban Redevelopment Authority for your intended use, building works may need plan approval from the Building and Construction Authority, and fire-safety plans go to the Singapore Civil Defence Force — usually coordinated by a qualified professional engaged through your contractor. Landlords commonly grant a rent-free fitting-out period. At the end of the lease, the reinstatement clause usually requires you to return the space to its original condition at your own cost.

Getting your team there

Almost every downtown office is a few minutes' walk from an MRT station, and most staff commute by train or bus. Raffles Place is served by the North–South and East–West lines; Marina Bay by the North–South, Circle and Thomson–East Coast lines; Downtown and Telok Ayer by the Downtown Line; Tanjong Pagar by the East–West Line; and Shenton Way and Maxwell by the Thomson–East Coast Line. Bugis, City Hall and Esplanade cover the northern fringe. Covered walkways and underground links matter more here than in most cities, because a building connected to a station stays easy to reach in a monsoon downpour. Car parking is limited, season parking is costly, and Electronic Road Pricing charges apply on the busiest roads at peak times, so few employers offer staff parking. End-of-trip facilities — bike racks, showers and lockers — are increasingly standard in new towers, and Changi Airport is a straightforward MRT or taxi ride for visiting clients.

What to check before you sign

  • Approved use. Confirm with the Urban Redevelopment Authority that the unit is authorised for office, shop or restaurant use; conservation shophouses often restrict what upper floors can be used for.
  • Reinstatement terms. Agree the hand-back standard in writing, photograph the space at handover, and ask whether the landlord will accept your fit-out for the next tenant.
  • Service charge and after-hours air-conditioning. Ask what the service charge covers, how it is reviewed, and the hourly charge for air-conditioning outside building hours if your team works across time zones.
  • Redevelopment risk. Older CBD buildings are prime candidates for redevelopment or collective sale; look for redevelopment break clauses and check how many years remain on the building's land lease.
  • Green Mark rating. Certified buildings tend to run more efficiently and help with sustainability reporting; check the rating level and when it was last renewed.
  • Loading and exhausts for food. Restaurants need goods-lift and loading-bay access, kitchen exhaust routes and grease traps; confirm what the landlord provides before you design the kitchen.
  • GST status. Confirm whether the landlord is GST-registered so you can budget for GST on rent and service charges and, if you are registered yourself, claim it back.

Hosting events downtown

Downtown Singapore is built for business events. The two large convention and exhibition centres at Marina Bay and Marina Centre host trade shows and conferences, and the surrounding hotels offer ballrooms of every size. For more character, the Civic District's heritage venues — including the National Gallery, the Asian Civilisations Museum, the Victoria Theatre and Concert Hall, and The Arts House in the old Parliament building — hire out halls, courtyards and terraces. Fort Canning Park and Gardens by the Bay offer lawns and conservatory spaces, and rooftop bars and shophouse restaurants handle smaller receptions. Because rain can arrive any afternoon, outdoor events need a covered fallback. Demand peaks around major conferences and the Formula 1 night race weekend, when hotel rates climb and roads around Marina Bay close, and business slows over Chinese New Year. Ticketed or public entertainment may need a licence from the police, and events in parks need approval from the National Parks Board.

Is downtown the right base?

For client-facing finance, legal and advisory firms, and for any business that wants the strongest address in Southeast Asia, the CBD and Marina Bay are hard to beat. The trade-off is cost, which is why the government's decentralisation plans have found takers: one-north suits technology, media and biomedical research; Paya Lebar and Tampines suit back-office and regional operations closer to the eastern suburbs; and Changi Business Park sits beside the airport. The Beach Road and Bugis fringe is often the best compromise — a few minutes from Raffles Place by train at a meaningful discount.
