In 30 seconds

  • Suits asset managers, consultancies, law firms, biotech and health-tech companies and university spin-outs that need access to one of the world's deepest pools of graduates and researchers
  • The main cores are the Financial District, Back Bay and the Seaport, with Cambridge's Kendall Square across the river for labs
  • The quirk to know: office is usually gross, but lab space is triple net — compare the true total cost

Boston's commercial centre is compact. The Financial District's towers rise between the Rose Kennedy Greenway, Post Office Square and Downtown Crossing, on streets that still follow colonial lanes. Government Center and the area around North Station hold city, state and federal offices and newer mixed-use towers. Back Bay, a mile or so west, forms a second office core around Copley Square and Boylston Street. Across Fort Point Channel, the Seaport has grown from car parks into a district of glass towers, and across the Charles River, Kendall Square in Cambridge is one of the world's most concentrated clusters of biotech and research.

The market is built on finance — especially asset and investment management — alongside consulting, law, healthcare, higher education and life sciences. Building stock ranges from 19th-century masonry and Fort Point brick warehouses to 1970s and 1980s towers and new Seaport glass. After a construction boom in laboratory space, lab demand cooled, giving tenants more choice, while office tenants increasingly favour newer, amenity-rich buildings near the main rail stations. Universities and teaching hospitals keep a steady pipeline of talent and spin-out companies flowing into the market.

Where the offices are

Financial DistrictThe traditional core around Post Office Square, Federal Street and State Street: banks, asset managers, law firms and consultancies in a mix of top-tier towers and older buildings. Walkable from South Station and several subway stations.
Back BayTowers around Copley Square and Boylston Street plus offices above shops on Newbury Street, popular with finance, consulting and tech. Back Bay station brings Amtrak, commuter rail and the Orange Line to the door.
Seaport and Fort PointNew towers for tech, life sciences and corporate headquarters, plus brick-and-beam lofts in Fort Point for creative firms. Close to the airport and the convention centre, with Silver Line bus rapid transit.
Downtown Crossing and Government CenterOlder office buildings, government agencies and conversions to housing and hotels, with good subway access and generally lower costs than the Financial District's best towers.
North Station and the West EndNewer mixed-use towers above the station and arena and space near the teaching hospitals, suited to health, tech and firms with commuters from the north.

Retail and restaurant corridors

Downtown Crossing's pedestrian streets carry the heaviest everyday shopping traffic, from office workers, residents and students. Newbury Street in Back Bay is the city's boutique and flagship strip, and Boylston Street adds larger stores and restaurants. Faneuil Hall Marketplace and Quincy Market rely on visitors, and the North End's Hanover Street lives on dining crowds, especially on summer evenings. The Seaport has become a dense restaurant and bar district serving office workers by day and visitors and residents at night. Foot traffic swings with the academic year, the tourist season from spring to autumn and big events such as the marathon and graduation weekends. Liquor licences in Boston are capped by state law, so restaurants often buy an existing licence from a current holder; recent legislation added a batch of licences restricted to specific neighbourhoods.

Coworking and flexible space

Flexible operators are concentrated in the Financial District, Back Bay and the Seaport, offering hot desks and dedicated desks on monthly memberships and private offices on terms from a month to a couple of years. Shared laboratory and incubator space, with benches, equipment and support services on flexible terms, is a Boston speciality, found in the Seaport and especially in Cambridge. Flexible space suits start-ups, university spin-outs, satellite teams and companies waiting for a lab or office build-out to finish.

How commercial leases work here

Boston follows US conventions. Office rent is quoted per rentable square foot per year, which includes a share of common areas. Most office space is leased on a gross basis, with base rent covering real estate taxes and operating costs for a base year and the tenant paying its share of increases; electricity is often charged separately, so listings may be described as gross plus electric. Laboratory and most retail space is leased triple net (NNN), with the tenant paying a share of property taxes, insurance and operating costs on top of base rent. Annual escalations are standard.

Landlords commonly provide a tenant improvement allowance and free-rent months scaled to the term and the tenant's credit; lab fit-outs are far more expensive than office, so allowances and timelines matter. Security is usually a cash deposit or letter of credit, and small or young companies are often asked for a personal guarantee. Massachusetts does not charge sales tax on commercial rent. Boston taxes commercial property at a higher rate than residential property, and those taxes flow through to tenants in operating-cost statements.

Getting your team there

South Station, at the edge of the Financial District, is the main gateway: the Red Line, the Silver Line, commuter rail lines from the south and west and Amtrak all meet there. North Station serves commuter rail from the north, along with the Orange and Green lines, and Back Bay station links its office core to the Orange Line, commuter rail and Amtrak. Subway lines meet at Park Street, Downtown Crossing, State and Government Center. Commuter ferries from the South Shore dock at the downtown wharves, and the Seaport is reached by the Silver Line or a walk from South Station. Logan Airport is minutes away through the harbour tunnels. Parking is scarce and expensive, so most downtown firms rely on transit, Bluebikes and walking; ask about bike rooms and showers.

What to check before you sign

  • Flood risk in the Seaport, Fort Point and along the waterfront, where coastal flood resilience zoning applies; ask where electrical and mechanical systems sit.
  • Building emissions rules: Boston's emissions ordinance for large buildings can lead to upgrade costs that appear in operating expenses.
  • Historic districts and architectural commissions in Beacon Hill and Back Bay, which tightly control signage, windows and façades.
  • The certificate of occupancy and any zoning relief your use needs from the city's inspectional services and zoning board, especially for restaurants.
  • For labs: electrical capacity, ventilation and exhaust, floor loading, vibration, loading-dock access and hazardous-materials permits.
  • After-hours heating and cooling charges, and how snow removal and winter access are handled.
  • For retail and food, the availability and price of a liquor licence before you commit to the space.

Hosting events downtown

The Boston Convention and Exhibition Center in the Seaport hosts the largest conferences and trade shows, and hotel ballrooms in Back Bay, the Seaport and the Financial District handle mid-sized meetings and dinners. Museums such as the Institute of Contemporary Art and the New England Aquarium suit waterfront receptions, historic halls bring colonial atmosphere, and harbour cruises and the Boston Harbor Islands offer summer options. Late spring and autumn are peak seasons; graduation weekends in May and Marathon weekend in April fill hotels across the city, and winter storms are a real risk for January and February dates. Events on the Rose Kennedy Greenway, Boston Common or other public spaces need permits from the relevant park or city authority, so apply early.

Is downtown the right base?

For finance, law, consulting and firms that want the widest commuter reach, the Financial District and Back Bay remain the natural choices. The Seaport suits companies that want new buildings, waterfront amenities and quick airport access, while lab-based life-science companies usually look first at Kendall Square, the Seaport or other dedicated lab clusters. Suburban markets along Route 128 and in towns such as Waltham and Burlington offer parking and larger floor plates for those willing to trade walkability for space.
