In 30 seconds

  • Who it suits: energy, engineering, finance and tech firms, and any business that wants a head-office address for less than Toronto or Vancouver
  • The main office cores: the Downtown Core along Stephen Avenue and the +15, ringed by Eau Claire, the West End, the Beltline and East Village
  • The one lease quirk to know: abundant space gives tenants real leverage — and rent carries only 5% GST, with no provincial sales tax

Downtown Calgary's office market is concentrated in a tight grid between the Bow River and the main-line railway tracks. The Downtown Core of head-office towers runs west from Centre Street, with the Stephen Avenue pedestrian mall as its retail spine and the CTrain running along 7 Avenue. The +15 skywalk network threads through the towers about 15 feet above the street, so much of the core is connected indoors. Eau Claire and the Downtown West End stretch towards the river and the west; across the tracks, the Beltline mixes mid-rise offices, converted warehouses and apartment towers; and East Village and Stampede Park anchor the eastern edge with newer mixed-use buildings and convention space.

The market was built around the oil and gas industry: towers from the 1970s and 1980s booms sit beside newer, taller buildings from the 2000s and 2010s. When energy prices fell in the mid-2010s, and companies later consolidated and adopted hybrid work, a large amount of space came back to the market, particularly in older towers. Since then the newest buildings have kept their appeal while older ones compete hard on price, incentives and upgrades, and several have been converted to housing. Growth in technology, finance, agribusiness and logistics, and a fast-growing population, are slowly absorbing space — but for now this remains a market where tenants hold the stronger hand.

Retail and restaurant corridors

Stephen Avenue (8 Avenue SW) is downtown's main street: restaurants, cafés and shops in historic sandstone buildings, with strong weekday lunch trade, summer patios and a huge spike during Stampede. Above it, the +15 level holds food courts, services and convenience retail that depend on office workers and are largely closed in the evenings and at weekends, though they gain traffic in winter when people stay indoors. The Beltline's 17th Avenue SW is the restaurant and nightlife strip, busiest on evenings and weekends and famous for street celebrations during hockey playoff runs, while 4th Street SW in Mission offers a calmer dining scene. North of the river, Kensington is a village of independent shops and cafés, and Inglewood's 9 Avenue SE is known for vintage, design and live music. East Village's new towers are adding residents and ground-floor retail, and Chinatown remains a strong food destination. Alberta's lack of a provincial sales tax is a small but real advantage for retailers.

Coworking and flexible space

Flexible space is plentiful and competitively priced. Operators occupy floors in Downtown Core towers, often with +15 access, and in converted buildings in the Beltline, East Village and Inglewood. Many landlords now market furnished, move-in-ready suites on short terms, which compete directly with coworking. Typical terms are monthly memberships for hot desks and dedicated desks, and private offices for small teams on terms from a few months to a year, with internet, meeting rooms and kitchens included. Start-ups also have access to public and non-profit innovation programmes on the east side of downtown. Given the incentives on offer for conventional leases, compare flex costs with a short direct lease before you commit.

How commercial leases work here

Commercial leases in Alberta are governed mainly by the contract and the common law rather than a detailed commercial tenancy statute. Most office and retail leases are net: base rent per square foot per year on rentable area, plus additional rent for your share of operating costs and property taxes, estimated monthly and reconciled after year-end. Calgary folded its old business tax into the non-residential property tax, so that cost reaches tenants through additional rent. Alberta law allows a landlord to seize a defaulting tenant's goods through a civil enforcement agency, and personal guarantees are common for small companies. Longer leases are often protected by registering a caveat at Land Titles.

Because supply is ample, the negotiation is where the value lies: free-rent periods, generous tenant-improvement allowances, landlord-built turnkey suites, and renewal, expansion and early-termination rights. Compare offers on net effective rent — the average rent you actually pay over the term after incentives — rather than the headline figure.

Conventions vary by building and landlord. Treat this as orientation, not legal advice, and have a local lawyer review any lease.

Getting your team there

Calgary Transit's CTrain is the backbone: the Red and Blue lines bring commuters from the northwest, northeast, west and south suburbs straight into the 7 Avenue transit mall, and travel between the downtown stations has long been free, although the City has been reviewing that zone. MAX rapid bus routes and regular buses fill the gaps, and an express bus links downtown with the airport. The +15 means staff can move between towers, transit and lunch without going outside in winter. Calgary's river pathways and downtown protected bike lanes make cycling practical much of the year, and many towers have bike rooms. Parking is easier to find than in Toronto or Vancouver, in tower parkades and City-run lots, and the main roads — Deerfoot, Crowchild and Memorial Drive — make driving in from the suburbs realistic outside rush hour. The Downtown Core is easiest to reach; Eau Claire and the Beltline are a short walk from the CTrain.

What to check before you sign

  • +15 connection: whether the building links in, which floor the link reaches and the hours it is open.
  • Flood risk: the 2013 flood reached parts of downtown, Eau Claire, East Village and Inglewood, so check flood maps, mitigation, parkade and basement exposure, and insurance.
  • The building's future: in older towers, ask about planned upgrades, the landlord's plans for the property and any conversion, relocation or demolition clauses.
  • Operating costs and property tax history: ask for several years of figures and how capital upgrades are charged back.
  • Stampede and parade days: road closures and crowds can block access and loading, so check how they affect a storefront or office.
  • Signage and heritage: the City's sign rules apply, and Stephen Avenue's historic buildings carry extra limits on storefronts and signs.
  • After-hours HVAC and security: confirm charges for heating and cooling outside standard hours and how access works at night and at weekends.

Hosting events downtown

Calgary has two large convention venues: a convention centre on Stephen Avenue in the Downtown Core, and the expanded convention and exhibition halls at Stampede Park. Hotel ballrooms across downtown handle galas and corporate meetings, while Studio Bell, the Central Library, the Confluence Historic Site, the downtown performing-arts centre, heritage sandstone buildings and rooftop patios offer more character. Stampede week is in a league of its own: corporate Stampede parties book venues, caterers and bands months in advance, and hotel rates climb. Summer festivals and the holiday party season are also busy, while late winter and spring offer better availability. Events in parks, plazas and streets need a City of Calgary special event permit, and serving alcohol at a one-off event requires a special event licence from Alberta Gaming, Liquor and Cannabis.

Is downtown the right base?

For head offices, client-facing firms and businesses that want transit access and the deepest pool of amenities, downtown offers more for the money than at any time in recent memory, and the incentives make a move into better space realistic. Suburban alternatives suit specific needs: office parks in the southeast such as Quarry Park for firms whose staff drive in from the south, the research and medical precinct in the northwest near the university, and the industrial and logistics districts in the northeast near the airport for distribution and light manufacturing. If you want visibility, talent and a CTrain commute, start downtown. For the wider picture, see the downtown Calgary guide.
