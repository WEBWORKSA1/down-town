In 30 seconds

  • Who it suits: tech, AI, gaming, aerospace, finance and creative firms that want big-city talent at lower occupancy costs than Toronto
  • The main office cores: René-Lévesque and McGill College above the RÉSO, plus Old Montréal, Griffintown and the Cité du Multimédia
  • The one lease quirk to know: leases fall under Quebec's Civil Code and the Charter of the French Language — expect French drafts and GST plus QST on rent

Downtown Montréal's central business district fills the slope between Mount Royal and Old Montréal: office towers line René-Lévesque Boulevard, McGill College Avenue and Peel Street around Place Ville Marie and Central Station. Beneath them, the RÉSO — the underground city — links métro stations, office lobbies, shopping centres, universities and hotels, so a large share of downtown can be crossed without stepping outside. To the south, the Quartier international and the convention centre bridge to Old Montréal, whose stone warehouses now hold tech firms, studios and boutique offices. To the southwest, the former industrial districts of the Cité du Multimédia and Griffintown have become a cluster for video games, visual effects and software.

The market has two personalities. The towers house banks, insurers, engineering, law and consulting firms and the head offices of Quebec institutions; the lofts house gaming studios, AI start-ups, e-commerce and design firms drawn by the universities and by provincial tax credits for digital industries. Occupancy costs have generally run below Toronto's for comparable space. As elsewhere, hybrid work has pushed tenants towards the newest and best-connected buildings and left older towers with more space to fill, which gives tenants leverage on incentives and terms.

Where the offices are

Quartier internationalNewer towers and international organisations between downtown and Old Montréal, around the convention centre and Victoria Square, on the Orange Line. Suits firms with global clients and conference-heavy calendars.

Retail and restaurant corridors

Sainte-Catherine Street West is the main shopping street, with department stores, flagships and a rebuilt streetscape of wider sidewalks; it is busy every day, peaking on weekends and during holiday shopping. Below it, the RÉSO's malls and food courts trade most heavily at weekday lunchtimes and through the winter, when foot traffic moves underground. Crescent Street and de la Montagne are bar and restaurant streets, the Quartier des spectacles explodes with crowds during the summer festivals, and Saint-Laurent Boulevard and Saint-Denis Street suit independent shops, nightlife and student trade. Old Montréal's Saint-Paul and Notre-Dame streets depend on tourists and cruise passengers, so they are strongest from spring to autumn, while Chinatown, the Village and Griffintown's Notre-Dame Street West have loyal local followings. Major roadworks are a fact of life; the City offers financial support to merchants hit by long construction projects, and it is worth asking what is planned for your block. Whatever the corridor, signage and customer service must meet French-language requirements.

Coworking and flexible space

Flexible operators cluster in downtown office towers near the RÉSO, in Old Montréal's heritage buildings, in Griffintown and in Mile End, with university-linked incubators adding more options for start-ups. Typical offers are hot desks and dedicated desks on monthly memberships and private offices for small teams on terms of a few months to a year, with internet, meeting rooms and community events included. Most spaces work comfortably in both French and English. Because conventional leases in Montréal often run long, flex space is a useful way to test the market or house a satellite team before committing.

How commercial leases work here

Quebec is a civil-law province, so commercial leases are governed by the Civil Code of Québec rather than common law. The parties are largely free to negotiate terms, but some Code rules apply unless the lease excludes them — for example, if you stay in the premises more than ten days after the lease ends without objection, the lease can be renewed automatically for up to a year, so most commercial leases address renewal expressly. To protect your term if the building is sold, the lease (or a notice of it) should be published at the land register. Landlords commonly ask for a movable hypothec on your equipment or a personal guarantee as security.

Most office and retail leases are net, with additional rent covering operating costs and municipal and school property taxes — the same TMI idea as in Ontario, though Quebec leases more often talk of "frais d'exploitation" and taxes. Language matters: under the Charter of the French Language, standard-form contracts must first be provided in French, and many landlords draft in French even for negotiated deals, so have the final version reviewed in the language that governs.

Conventions vary by building and landlord. Treat this as orientation, not legal advice, and have a local lawyer review any lease.

Getting your team there

The STM métro's Green and Orange lines serve the downtown core and Old Montréal, the Yellow Line reaches Longueuil on the South Shore, and the Blue Line serves the north of the city. The REM light rail runs from the South Shore across the Samuel De Champlain Bridge to Central Station and is extending to the West Island, the North Shore and the airport as segments open. Exo commuter trains arrive at Central Station and Lucien-L'Allier, and express buses come in from the suburbs. BIXI bike share and the protected lanes of the Réseau express vélo make cycling practical from spring to autumn, and the RÉSO keeps winter commutes indoors. Driving is slow: the bridges and highway interchanges are congested, roadworks are constant and tower parking is costly. The blocks around McGill, Peel, Bonaventure and Central Station are the easiest to reach from everywhere.

What to check before you sign

  • French-language compliance: get the French version of the lease, check that your planned signage meets the rules on French predominance, and note that firms with 25 or more employees in Quebec must register with the Office québécois de la langue française.
  • Heritage controls: Old Montréal is a protected heritage site, so exterior work and signs need provincial authorisation on top of borough permits.
  • Renewal and publication: confirm how the lease handles renewal and tacit renewal, and publish it at the land register.
  • Operating costs and taxes: ask for several years of figures and how capital repairs and snow removal are charged.
  • RÉSO connection: whether the building links underground, and the hours of that link.
  • Permits: an occupancy certificate from the borough, food-service permits and, for alcohol, a permit from the Régie des alcools, des courses et des jeux.
  • Roadworks: check the City's planned works for your street before you commit to a storefront.

Hosting events downtown

Montréal has a deep conference and events market. The Palais des congrès handles large conferences and trade shows, connected to the métro and the RÉSO, and downtown hotel ballrooms cover galas and corporate meetings. For character, look at Old Montréal's heritage halls, including the rooms of Bonsecours Market, the Old Port's waterfront venues, museum spaces, converted churches and loft studios in Griffintown and the Cité du Multimédia, plus rooftop terraces in summer. Seasonality is pronounced: hotels fill for the Formula 1 Grand Prix weekend in late spring and the summer festival run, while spring and autumn are peak conference seasons and winter brings good-value dates. Events on streets, squares and parks need permits from the City and borough, and serving alcohol at a one-off event requires a permit from the Régie des alcools, des courses et des jeux.

Is downtown the right base?

For firms that need talent from the universities, clients in the towers and the best transit in Quebec, downtown is the clear first choice, and the RÉSO makes it workable year-round. The alternatives each serve a niche: Mile End and Mile-Ex for creative and AI teams that prefer a neighbourhood feel; Saint-Laurent and the area around the airport for aerospace, life sciences and firms with industrial needs; Laval and the South Shore, increasingly linked by the REM and the métro, for lower costs and easier parking. If your work depends on bilingual talent and face-to-face business, start downtown. For the wider picture, see the downtown Montréal guide.
