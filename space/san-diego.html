In 30 seconds

  • Who it suits: law, finance, defence-related and creative firms, plus hospitality operators who want the bay, the trolley and the airport on the doorstep
  • The main office cores: the Core and Columbia towers along Broadway and B Street, with East Village and Little Italy for creative space
  • The one lease quirk to know: office rent is quoted per square foot per month, full-service gross — and waterfront sites sit on Port-managed land

Downtown San Diego's office towers concentrate in the Core and Columbia districts, along Broadway, B Street and C Street and toward the waterfront by Santa Fe Depot, close to the federal and county courts and the city's civic offices. East Village, on the east side, mixes converted warehouses and newer creative offices with the ballpark, the Central Library and a downtown university campus. The Gaslamp Quarter has small offices above its restaurants and bars, Little Italy has boutique mid-rise buildings, and the Marina District and Embarcadero are given over to hotels, the convention centre and the visitor economy, much of it on public tideland leased from the Port of San Diego.

Downtown is one of several office hubs in the region, and many of San Diego's biggest employers sit elsewhere: life sciences around Torrey Pines and Sorrento Valley, tech and professional firms in University City, and defence and engineering in Kearny Mesa. Downtown's tenant base leans toward law, finance, accounting, government and government contractors, plus tech, design and creative firms drawn to East Village and Little Italy. Most towers date from the 1980s onward. As elsewhere, hybrid working has raised vacancy and made landlords more flexible, newer and renovated buildings lease best, and some older offices and the former Horton Plaza mall have been earmarked for housing or labs.

Retail and restaurant corridors

The Gaslamp Quarter's Fourth, Fifth and Sixth Avenues are the busiest food-and-drink strips downtown, with traffic that peaks on weekend nights and during conventions, when footfall can be extraordinary. Little Italy's India Street and Kettner Boulevard are a dining district with steadier, all-week trade and a big Saturday market that brings crowds to surrounding shops. East Village suits breweries, cafés, fitness studios and neighbourhood services for its growing residential population, and it surges on baseball game days from spring to early autumn. The Embarcadero and waterfront serve tourists and cruise passengers, and the Core's ground floors depend on the weekday office lunch trade, strongest mid-week. The shift from enclosed-mall shopping to street-level food and drink is clear across downtown, and operators that can ride the seasonal peaks — and survive the quieter weekdays — do best.

Coworking and flexible space

Flexible workspace is concentrated in the Core's towers, where landlords and operators run furnished floors with city and bay views, and in East Village and Little Italy, where converted buildings suit creative teams. Typical options include hot desks, dedicated desks and private offices on monthly or short-term agreements, with meeting rooms, mail handling and a business address. The market suits consultants working with the region's defence and life-sciences employers, start-ups, remote workers and satellite teams of companies based in Los Angeles or the Bay Area. For a handful of desks, compare flexible terms with the spec suites and small subleases now available in many towers.

How commercial leases work here

San Diego follows Southern California conventions. Office rent is quoted per rentable square foot per month, usually full-service gross: operating costs and property taxes are included up to a base year, after which you pay your share of any increases. Retail is generally triple net (NNN), with property taxes, insurance and common-area maintenance billed on top. Rentable area includes a share of common space, annual increases are fixed in the lease, and parking is usually charged separately per space. Landlords typically fund build-out through a tenant improvement (TI) allowance and may offer months of free rent, particularly on longer terms.

Under California's Proposition 13, a property's assessed value rises only modestly until it changes hands, when it is reassessed at market value; because property taxes pass through under most leases, a sale can change your costs mid-term, so consider negotiating a limit. California landlords must disclose whether premises have been inspected by a Certified Access Specialist (CASp), and since 2025 very small businesses, restaurants and nonprofits have extra protections on rent-increase notices and operating-cost billing. Waterfront tideland is leased from the Port of San Diego and restricted to public-trust uses, and businesses in the city need a City of San Diego business tax certificate.

Getting your team there

The MTS Trolley gives downtown direct rail links in three directions: the Blue Line north to UC San Diego and University City and south to Chula Vista and the border, the Green Line through Old Town and Mission Valley, and the Orange Line east toward El Cajon. The Coaster commuter train runs from North County coastal towns to Santa Fe Depot, Amtrak's coastal trains arrive from Orange County and Los Angeles, and MTS buses fill the gaps. Interstate 5 runs along downtown's eastern edge, with State Route 163 and Interstates 15 and 94 close by; garages are plentiful, but parking costs and event-day congestion are real. The airport is minutes away, a big advantage for teams that travel. The Core and Columbia districts are the easiest to reach by transit; parts of Little Italy and East Village are a walk from the nearest stop.

What to check before you sign

  • Seismic and fault zones. The Rose Canyon fault runs beneath parts of downtown and the bay, and some blocks lie in a state-designated earthquake fault zone. Ask about the building's seismic design and any retrofits, and for new builds, the geotechnical studies.
  • Port tideland leases. On the waterfront, your landlord may hold a ground lease from the Port. Check the remaining term, permitted uses and any Port approvals your fit-out or signage needs.
  • Event-day impacts. Ballgames, conventions and big festivals affect access, deliveries, parking and noise. Ask how the building handles garage access and visitor parking on those days.
  • Historic-district review. In the Gaslamp Quarter, façade changes and signage go through the city's historic-district design review, which adds time.
  • Property-tax and cost pass-throughs. Clarify how a Proposition 13 reassessment, capital repairs and building-wide upgrades are billed, and cap them where you can.
  • Food and drink. Restaurants need county environmental health plan approval, a grease interceptor and kitchen ventilation; full liquor licences are capped and often bought from existing holders, and outdoor dining needs a city permit.
  • Flood and sea-level exposure. Low-lying blocks near the bay can flood in heavy storms; ask about drainage, insurance and any history of water intrusion.

Hosting events downtown

The San Diego Convention Center on the waterfront hosts the city's largest gatherings, from trade shows to Comic-Con International in July, when hotel rooms and venues across downtown are booked far in advance. Waterfront hotel ballrooms and terraces suit conferences and weddings, the USS Midway Museum and the Maritime Museum's historic ships offer memorable settings for receptions, and Balboa Park's museums and gardens are a short ride away. Gaslamp and Little Italy rooftops, breweries in East Village and galleries round out the options. The mild climate makes outdoor events possible for much of the year, though May and June mornings are often grey. Events on streets or in public parks need permits from the city's special events office, and events on Port tideland need Port approval.

Is downtown the right base?

Downtown San Diego suits firms that serve the courts and government, want an urban address with the bay and the airport close by, or need to recruit staff who prefer a walkable neighbourhood and trolley access. Life-sciences companies usually look to Torrey Pines and Sorrento Valley, many tech and professional firms prefer University City near UC San Diego, and Kearny Mesa and Mission Valley offer central locations with easier parking. If client entertaining, visitor traffic or a lively setting matter to your business, downtown has no rival in the region.
