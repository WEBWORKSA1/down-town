In 30 seconds

  • Who it suits: tech, life-sciences, professional-services and trade firms whose teams commute by rail, bus and ferry
  • The main office cores: the Commercial Core towers, South Lake Union and the Denny Triangle, with Pioneer Square for character space
  • The one lease quirk to know: no state income tax, but Washington and Seattle tax businesses on gross receipts, whether or not you make a profit

Downtown Seattle's commercial geography runs north to south along the slope above Elliott Bay. The Commercial Core's towers fill the avenues between Pioneer Square and Westlake, with retail around Pine Street and the convention centre on the eastern edge. North of it, the Denny Triangle and South Lake Union became, within a couple of decades, one of the country's largest concentrations of tech offices and research labs. To the south, Pioneer Square — rebuilt in brick and stone after the Great Seattle Fire of 1889 — and the Chinatown–International District offer smaller historic buildings, with First Hill's hospitals and medical offices above them and the stadiums and industrial land of SoDo beyond. Belltown, Pike Place Market and the new waterfront provide the retail and food space.

The market is dominated by technology. Cloud computing and e-commerce companies drove a long office boom, then cut back, leaving vacancy and sublease availability well above pre-2020 levels. Downtown also competes directly with Bellevue on the Eastside, where several big tech employers have expanded. Tenants therefore have leverage, especially in older towers, while newer buildings in the Denny Triangle and well-located renovations lease best. Life sciences, the port and maritime trade, professional services and the region's hospitals and universities provide steadier demand, and the rebuilt waterfront is reshaping the western edge of downtown.

Retail and restaurant corridors

Westlake and the blocks around Pine Street form the main shopping district, with department stores and flagships drawing weekend and holiday crowds. Pike Place Market is a world of its own: hundreds of farmers, craftspeople and small businesses trading under a public development authority that manages the market. The rebuilt waterfront is adding new food and retail space aimed at visitors and residents. Pioneer Square's bars and restaurants surge on stadium event days, the Chinatown–International District supports long-established restaurants, bakeries and grocers, and Belltown is the late-night strip. South Lake Union's ground floors depend on the weekday tech crowd, busiest mid-week. Seasonality is pronounced: summer and the cruise season, which runs from spring into autumn, bring the biggest visitor numbers, and the dark, wet months are quieter.

Coworking and flexible space

Flexible operators cluster in the Commercial Core's towers, Pioneer Square's characterful brick buildings and South Lake Union near the tech campuses, offering hot desks, dedicated desks and private offices on monthly or short-term agreements with meeting rooms and a business address. They suit start-ups, consultants and satellite teams of companies based on the Eastside or in California. The large supply of sublease space left by downsizing tech companies is a real alternative for growing teams: many floors come furnished and wired, available for the remainder of the original tenant's term, often on attractive terms.

How commercial leases work here

Seattle office rent is quoted per rentable square foot per year. Most towers lease on a full-service basis, with operating costs and property taxes included up to a base year and later increases passed through; retail and lab space is usually triple net (NNN), with taxes, insurance and common-area maintenance billed on top. Rentable area includes a share of common space, and annual increases are fixed in the lease. Tenant improvement (TI) allowances and free-rent periods are common in the current market, especially on longer terms, and spec suites offer move-in-ready space.

Washington does things differently from California. There is no Proposition 13: property is reassessed regularly at market value, and taxes pass through under most leases regardless of a sale. There is no sales tax on commercial rent, but the state's business and occupation (B&O) tax applies to gross receipts, Seattle adds its own B&O tax, and larger employers pay a city payroll expense tax. Leases of publicly owned property, such as Port of Seattle piers, carry a leasehold excise tax in place of property tax. Washington's Clean Buildings law and Seattle's building emissions standard also require larger buildings to cut energy use, and some landlords pass compliance costs through.

Conventions vary by building and landlord. Treat this as orientation, not legal advice, and have a local lawyer review any lease.

Getting your team there

Sound Transit's Link light rail runs through downtown in a tunnel beneath Third Avenue, linking the airport and south Seattle with Capitol Hill, the University District and the northern suburbs, and the network has been expanding toward the Eastside. Sounder commuter trains from Tacoma and Everett arrive at King Street Station, Washington State Ferries bring commuters from Bainbridge Island and Bremerton to Colman Dock, and the King County Water Taxi crosses from West Seattle. King County Metro and Sound Transit express buses converge on the Third Avenue transit spine, and the streetcar serves South Lake Union and First Hill. Protected bike lanes cross downtown. Parking is costly, and larger employers must take part in the state's commute trip reduction programme, which favours transit passes over parking. The Commercial Core and Pioneer Square are the easiest to reach.

What to check before you sign

  • Seismic risk. Seattle sits above the Seattle Fault and near the Cascadia subduction zone, and Pioneer Square, the waterfront and SoDo stand partly on filled tideland. The city has identified hundreds of unreinforced masonry buildings, many in Pioneer Square and the International District; ask about retrofit status and how future work would be billed.
  • Historic-district approvals. The Pioneer Square Preservation Board and the International Special Review District review exterior changes, signage and some uses, and Pike Place Market has its own historical commission that approves businesses and uses in the market.
  • Energy-performance costs. Ask how the building is meeting state and city energy and emissions standards, and whether upgrade costs will be passed through as operating expenses.
  • Tax exposure. Model your B&O and payroll expense tax before you commit headcount to the city, and check for leasehold excise tax on public property.
  • Street conditions and security. Some blocks along Third Avenue and around Pioneer Square have visible street disorder; ask how the building manages entrances, security and after-hours access.
  • Food and drink. Restaurants need public health plan review from King County, a Washington State Liquor and Cannabis Board licence for alcohol, grease interceptors and kitchen ventilation.
  • Building operations. Check after-hours heating and cooling charges, loading access on steep streets and the impact of stadium days on deliveries and parking.

Hosting events downtown

The Seattle Convention Center, now spread across two buildings, hosts the largest conferences, with hotel ballrooms throughout the Commercial Core. For more character, consider the waterfront piers, galleries and historic halls in Pioneer Square's brick buildings, the art museum and other museums that rent space after hours, and rooftop terraces with views of the Sound and the mountains. Seattle Center, home of the Space Needle, is a short monorail ride away. Summer is peak season, when long, dry evenings make outdoor events reliable and the cruise season fills hotels; from autumn to spring, plan for rain and have an indoor backup. Events in public spaces, parks or on streets need permits from the city's special events office or the parks department.

Is downtown the right base?

Downtown Seattle offers the region's best transit access, from light rail and buses to commuter trains and ferries, along with the widest range of space, from glass towers to brick lofts, and tenant-friendly terms. Bellevue is the main alternative, with newer towers, a strong tech presence and an Eastside workforce, while Redmond centres on big tech campuses and SoDo offers cheaper flex and industrial space. Firms serving clients across Puget Sound, recruiting city-dwelling staff or wanting a historic setting in Pioneer Square will find downtown hard to beat.
