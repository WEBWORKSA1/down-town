In 30 seconds

  • Who it suits: professional-services, finance, education, health and tech firms that want a walkable grid with excellent tram access
  • The main office cores: Collins Street and the western core, Docklands, and Southbank and St Kilda Road, with Cremorne and Richmond for tech
  • The one lease quirk to know: net rent plus outgoings plus GST is the usual structure — and Victoria's retail law treats shops very differently from offices

Melbourne's commercial heart is the Hoddle Grid, the rectangle of streets bounded by Flinders, Spring, La Trobe and Spencer Streets. Collins Street runs through it as the financial spine, with the tallest and newest towers toward the west near Southern Cross Station and older, boutique buildings at the eastern Paris end near Parliament. West of the grid, Docklands has large modern office buildings on the former wharves; south of the river, Southbank and the St Kilda Road boulevard add towers and mid-rise offices. Further out, Cremorne and Richmond have become the hub for tech and creative firms, and Parkville to the north is a major medical and research precinct.

Melbourne's office market is broad rather than finance-dominated: banking and insurance sit alongside law, accounting, consulting, government, education, health and a growing technology sector, and several of Australia's largest companies and banks have their headquarters here. Building stock ranges from Victorian-era banking halls and arcades to new towers with strong energy ratings, and as elsewhere, demand has shifted toward newer, better-serviced buildings since the pandemic, leaving older stock to compete on price. Major projects — the Metro Tunnel, the Fishermans Bend renewal area and the Arden precinct — are steadily extending the city's commercial footprint.

Where the offices are

Western core and Collins StreetThe traditional business district between Southern Cross Station and Elizabeth Street, home to banks, law firms and consultancies in premium towers along Collins, Bourke and William Streets. Suits firms that value the address and Southern Cross's regional and airport links.
Eastern core and the Paris endThe leafy eastern end of Collins Street toward Parliament, with heritage buildings, boutique offices and medical suites alongside luxury retail. Suits professional firms, family offices and advisers who prefer character to scale.
DocklandsA waterfront district of large, efficient floor plates built over the past two decades, home to several bank and corporate headquarters. Suits big tenants consolidating teams, with easy tram and train access from Southern Cross.
Southbank and St Kilda RoadTowers along the river and a boulevard of mid-century and newer office buildings running south past the Shrine of Remembrance. Often good value, with trams along St Kilda Road and a Metro Tunnel station at Anzac.
Cremorne and RichmondConverted warehouses and new mid-rise buildings just south-east of the CBD, home to many of the city's tech companies. Suits startups and creative firms that want space with personality, close to trains and trams.

Retail and restaurant corridors

The CBD's retail core is Bourke Street Mall and the surrounding blocks of Swanston, Elizabeth and Collins Streets, anchored by department stores and stitched together by the Block Arcade, the Royal Arcade and a web of laneways. Luxury brands cluster at the eastern end of Collins Street, while Melbourne Central and the streets around it draw students from the nearby universities. Laneways such as Degraves Street, Centre Place, Hardware Lane and Flinders Lane support small cafés, bars and restaurants that could not afford main-street frontage, and Chinatown on Little Bourke Street and the Greek precinct on Lonsdale Street are established dining strips.

Footfall is unusually balanced for an Australian CBD: weekday office workers and a large student population, plus strong evening and weekend trade from theatres, sport and events. Big fixtures at Melbourne Park, the MCG and the Docklands stadium bring surges to the eastern and western edges of the grid. Food businesses must register with the City of Melbourne, footpath trading needs a council permit, and alcohol requires a licence from Liquor Control Victoria.

Coworking and flexible space

Flexible workspace is well developed. Operators occupy floors in Collins Street towers and around Southern Cross, in Southbank and Docklands, and in converted warehouses in Cremorne, Richmond, Collingwood and Fitzroy, where much of the city's startup community is concentrated. Typical options are hot desks and dedicated desks on monthly terms and private offices on agreements from a few months to a couple of years. Landlords also offer fitted, ready-to-move-in suites on shorter leases, a middle path between coworking and a conventional lease. Flexible space suits interstate and overseas companies opening a Melbourne presence, project teams and small firms, and the universities run innovation hubs for early-stage ventures.

How commercial leases work here

Commercial leases in Victoria follow the broad Australian pattern. Rent is quoted per square metre per year, either net — with the tenant paying a share of outgoings such as council rates, insurance, cleaning, building services and management — or gross, with outgoings included. GST is added to rent and outgoings. Office leases commonly run three to five years for small and mid-size tenants and longer for large floors, often with options to renew. Rent usually rises each year by a fixed percentage or in line with CPI, with market reviews at option dates. Landlords offer incentives, usually rent-free periods or fit-out contributions, so compare effective rather than face rents.

Security is usually a bank guarantee covering several months' gross rent including GST, and a make-good clause requires you to remove your fit-out at the end. Retail premises — shops, cafés, restaurants and many service businesses — are covered by the Retail Leases Act 2003, which requires the landlord to provide a disclosure statement at least seven days before the lease is signed, sets a five-year minimum term unless the tenant waives it with a lawyer's certificate, and bars landlords from recovering land tax from retail tenants. Disputes under the Act go first to the Victorian Small Business Commission for mediation.

Getting your team there

Few CBDs are easier to reach. The City Loop connects Flinders Street, Southern Cross, Flagstaff, Melbourne Central and Parliament stations, the Metro Tunnel adds State Library and Town Hall stations beneath Swanston Street, and Southern Cross is the terminus for regional trains and the express airport bus. The tram network, the largest in the world, crosses every street of the grid, and the Free Tram Zone means client meetings across the CBD and Docklands cost nothing to reach. The flat grid suits cycling, with protected lanes on several streets, and end-of-trip facilities are standard in better buildings. Driving in is expensive: CBD parking is limited, and a congestion levy applies to many off-street parking spaces in and around the centre. Buildings near Southern Cross and Flinders Street have the widest commuter catchment, while Docklands relies on trams and a short walk from Southern Cross.

What to check before you sign

  • Outgoings and land tax. Ask for the outgoings budget and recent actuals, and confirm whether land tax is recovered — usual on office leases, not permitted on retail.
  • Effective rent. Compare incentives across buildings and check whether they arrive as rent-free time or fit-out contributions.
  • NABERS rating. Check the building's energy rating and whether it meets your organisation's sustainability commitments.
  • Heritage overlays. Many CBD buildings sit under heritage controls that restrict alterations, signage and shopfront changes; check before planning a fit-out.
  • Laneway access and loading. For laneway sites, confirm delivery and waste-collection arrangements and any time limits on loading.
  • Cladding and building safety. Ask whether the building has been assessed or rectified for combustible cladding, and request the latest essential safety measures report.
  • Retail disclosure. For shops and restaurants, read the disclosure statement carefully and take legal advice before waiving the five-year minimum term.

Hosting events downtown

Melbourne sells itself as Australia's events capital, and the infrastructure backs it up. The Melbourne Convention and Exhibition Centre on the Yarra at South Wharf hosts major conferences and exhibitions, and hotel ballrooms across the CBD and Southbank handle dinners and meetings. For something distinctive, the State Library, the National Gallery of Victoria, Melbourne Town Hall and the World Heritage-listed Royal Exhibition Building all hire out spaces, and the MCG and Melbourne Park have function rooms with arena views. Laneway bars, rooftop venues and warehouse spaces in the inner suburbs suit launches and parties. The calendar is crowded — the Australian Open in January, the Formula 1 Grand Prix in autumn, the football finals in September and the spring racing season fill hotels and venues — so book early. Events in streets, squares and parks need permits from the City of Melbourne.

Is downtown the right base?

For professional, financial and government-facing firms, the CBD grid is the natural base: everything is walkable, trams are free across it, and the city's rail network converges on it. Docklands offers bigger floors for less, Southbank and St Kilda Road add value south of the river, and Cremorne and Richmond suit tech firms that recruit young talent. Suburban centres such as Box Hill and the Monash precinct serve companies with staff in the eastern suburbs. Read the downtown Melbourne guide to see what the CBD offers your team beyond the office.
