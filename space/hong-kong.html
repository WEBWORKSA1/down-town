In 30 seconds

  • Who it suits: financial, legal and advisory firms, and companies serving mainland China and the wider region
  • The main office cores: Central and Admiralty, ringed by Sheung Wan, Wan Chai and Causeway Bay, with Quarry Bay and Kowloon East as decentralised options
  • The one lease quirk to know: rent is charged on gross floor area, so building efficiency decides how much space you actually get

Hong Kong's office core is a narrow band along the north shore of Hong Kong Island. Central and Admiralty hold the prime towers between the harbour and the slopes of the Peak, linked by footbridges to the MTR and to each other. West of Central, Sheung Wan offers older, smaller buildings; to the east, Wan Chai and Causeway Bay mix offices with shopping and nightlife, and Quarry Bay has grown into a business district of its own. Across the harbour, Tsim Sha Tsui and the area around Kowloon Station add towers on the Kowloon side, while Kowloon East — Kwun Tong and Kowloon Bay — is the government-backed second core built on former industrial land.

The market is dominated by finance: banks, asset managers, insurers, private-equity firms and the law and accounting practices that serve them, alongside the regional offices of mainland Chinese companies and a growing number of family offices. Prime towers in Central tend to be single-owner buildings held by large landlords, while many older buildings in Sheung Wan, Wan Chai and Kowloon are strata-titled, with floors owned by different investors. A wave of new supply in recent years has given tenants more choice and negotiating room than the city is historically known for, and many firms have used it to trade up to better buildings or consolidate. Rents rise and fall with financial-market sentiment and the flow of business from the mainland, so timing matters.

Where the offices are

CentralThe prime financial core around Queen's Road Central, Des Voeux Road and the harbourfront, with the stock exchange, bank headquarters and leading law firms in Grade A towers linked to Central and Hong Kong stations. Suits firms for whom the address is part of the product.
AdmiraltyNext to Central and home to the government headquarters, with large Grade A towers above a four-line MTR interchange. Similar tenants to Central, sometimes with larger floors.
Sheung WanOlder and mid-generation buildings west of Central, many strata-titled, plus some newer towers. Suits small professional firms, startups, galleries and trading companies that want to be a short walk from Central.
Wan Chai and Causeway BayA dense mix of office towers, malls, hotels and the convention centre, popular with insurers, media, consumer brands and service firms, with a wide range of building ages and sizes.
Quarry Bay and Island EastA decentralised cluster on the island's east side with large, modern floor plates. Suits back-office, technology and regional teams that want quality space at a discount to Central.
Kowloon EastKwun Tong and Kowloon Bay, a former industrial district turning into a second business core, with new towers beside converted industrial buildings. Best value for large floors; check the land-use terms of any industrial building carefully.

Retail and restaurant corridors

Central's retail is luxury-led and vertical: flagship stores along Queen's Road Central and Des Voeux Road, and malls wired into the MTR and the footbridge network. Causeway Bay is one of the busiest shopping districts in the city, with dense street retail and large malls, while Tsim Sha Tsui, especially along Canton Road and Nathan Road, depends heavily on visitors, so its sales rise and fall with tourism. Food and drink cluster in SoHo and Lan Kwai Fong, along Wyndham Street, and in the side streets of Sheung Wan and Wan Chai. Because ground-floor frontage is so expensive, many restaurants, salons and bars trade from upper floors, relying on reputation and online discovery rather than passing trade.

Central's footfall is driven by weekday office crowds, with a heavy lunch peak; on Sundays its pedestrian streets and footbridges become a gathering place for the city's domestic workers on their day off, which changes the trading pattern for shops and cafés. Restaurants need a licence from the Food and Environmental Hygiene Department, which depends on ventilation, drainage and fire-safety arrangements, and alcohol requires a separate liquor licence.

Coworking and flexible space

Flexible workspace is widespread across the core. Operators occupy whole floors in Central and Admiralty towers, smaller converted buildings in Sheung Wan, and larger centres in Wan Chai, Causeway Bay, Quarry Bay and Kowloon East. Typical options are hot desks and dedicated desks on monthly terms, and private offices on agreements from a few months to a couple of years, with reception, meeting rooms and IT included. Many centres also offer a registered-office address and company-secretarial support, useful because every Hong Kong company needs a registered office. Flexible space suits firms establishing a regional presence, teams waiting for a long-term lease to begin, and startups, which can also look at the government-backed science and technology parks and incubators outside the core.

How commercial leases work here

Office rent in Hong Kong is quoted per square foot per month, and — unlike many markets — it is usually calculated on gross floor area, which includes a share of lifts, lobbies, corridors and plant rooms. The ratio of usable to gross area, known as efficiency, varies a lot between buildings, so compare offices on the effective rent per usable square foot. Rent is normally exclusive of management fees, which cover common-area services and air-conditioning during building hours, and of government rates, a property tax charged quarterly on a property's rateable value that leases typically pass to the tenant; some leases also pass on government rent. There is no VAT or GST in Hong Kong.

Leases commonly run two to three years. Three-year terms often come with an option to renew at the market rent, and longer leases usually include a rent review partway through. Stamp duty is payable on leases, calculated on the rent and the term, and is customarily split equally between landlord and tenant; the lease should be stamped promptly after signing. Landlords usually grant a rent-free period for fit-out, and tenants are expected to reinstate the premises at the end of the term. Building works may need approval under the Buildings Department's controls, and fit-outs must meet Fire Services Department requirements.

Getting your team there

The MTR makes the whole core accessible. Central and Hong Kong stations connect the Island, Tsuen Wan, Tung Chung and Airport Express lines; Admiralty is the interchange for the Island, Tsuen Wan, South Island and East Rail lines; and Sheung Wan, Wan Chai, Causeway Bay and Quarry Bay sit on the Island Line. Kowloon East is served by the Kwun Tong Line. Staff commuting from the New Territories benefit from the East Rail Line's harbour crossing to Admiralty, and the high-speed rail station in West Kowloon links the city with Shenzhen and Guangzhou for regional travel. Trams and the Star Ferry add slower but pleasant options, and the footbridge network keeps most of Central walkable in the rain. Parking is scarce and expensive and very few staff drive, so when choosing a building, a covered connection to a station is worth far more than a car-park allocation.

What to check before you sign

  • Efficiency and measurement. Ask for the gross, saleable and usable areas and the efficiency ratio, then compare rents on usable space.
  • Management fees and rates. Get the current management fee and rateable value in writing, and ask how often fees have risen.
  • After-hours air-conditioning. Many buildings charge extra outside office hours, which matters if your team works to other time zones.
  • Land-use terms. Check that the building's government lease and occupation permit allow your use — critical for converted industrial buildings in Kowloon East and for any restaurant or shop.
  • Typhoon and rainstorm arrangements. Confirm how the building operates under typhoon signals and black rainstorm warnings, and align your staff policies with the Labour Department's guidance.
  • Reinstatement. Record the condition at handover and agree in writing what must be removed at the end.
  • Signage. Shopfront signs fall under the Buildings Department's signboard controls; confirm what is allowed before ordering anything.

Hosting events downtown

Hong Kong is one of Asia's busiest event cities. The Hong Kong Convention and Exhibition Centre on the Wan Chai waterfront hosts the major trade fairs and conferences, and AsiaWorld-Expo near the airport takes the largest shows. Hotel ballrooms in Central, Admiralty and Tsim Sha Tsui handle dinners and conferences, while heritage spaces such as Tai Kwun and the venues of the West Kowloon Cultural District suit cultural and brand events. Rooftop bars, private dining rooms and hired junk boats on the harbour are classic options for client entertaining. Autumn is peak conference season, and March fills up around Art Basel; in summer, build in contingency plans for typhoon signals and rainstorm warnings, which can cancel events at short notice. Ticketed events may need a public entertainment licence and temporary liquor licences, and harbourfront and park sites are booked through the relevant government departments.

Is downtown the right base?

Central remains the default for banks, law firms and anyone who meets clients in person every day. For everyone else, the city offers real choice: Sheung Wan for small firms wanting a Central-adjacent address, Wan Chai and Causeway Bay for consumer and media businesses, Quarry Bay for quality space at a discount, and Kowloon East for large teams and the best value. Tsim Sha Tsui and the Kowloon Station area suit firms with frequent travel to the mainland. Because the MTR connects all of these within minutes, choosing a secondary district rarely costs you access. Read the downtown Hong Kong guide for how Central works beyond office hours.
