In most downtowns you can find an apartment on your own. But in fast-moving rental markets, unfamiliar cities, or buildings that only lease through agents, a professional can save you weeks of searching — or charge you a fee you didn't need to pay. The difference comes down to three things: who the agent works for, how they're paid, and whether they are who they say they are.

This guide explains the main kinds of rental help, how fee customs differ from market to market, how to vet anyone before you hand over documents or money, and the scams that target people moving downtown.

Who's who in the rental market

The job titles vary by country, but most rental help falls into one of five roles.

RoleWorks forUsually paid byMost useful for
Apartment locatorYou, during the searchA commission from participating buildings, where that is the local customLarge managed apartment communities
Landlord's leasing or listing agentThe landlordThe landlord, with costs passed to tenants where the law allowsSeeing what a particular building has available
Tenant's agent or brokerYouYou, or a share of the landlord's commission, depending on the marketCompetitive markets, private landlords and condo rentals
Property managerThe ownerThe ownerRepairs and day-to-day issues after you move in
Relocation or destination consultantUsually your employerYour employer, as part of a relocation packageCorporate and international moves, area tours, school searches

The column that matters most is the second one. An agent who works for the landlord owes their main duties to the landlord, however friendly they are. That isn't sinister — it's the job. Just don't expect them to tell you the rent is too high.

How fees work in different markets

Fee customs are local, and they change. Treat the patterns below as a guide to what to ask, not a statement of the rules where you're moving.

Markets where buildings pay locators

In several fast-growing US metros, particularly in Texas and across parts of the South and Southwest, free apartment locators are common. Large apartment communities pay the locator a commission when a referred tenant signs, so the service costs you nothing directly. The catch is that a locator only earns from buildings that pay, so private landlords and some buildings never appear on their lists. Many buildings also require you to name the locator on your first visit or application; tour on your own first and the building may refuse to credit them.

Markets where tenants have paid brokers

In some dense, competitive markets, tenants have traditionally paid a broker's fee on signing, sometimes a substantial one. That is changing. New York City now generally requires whoever hires the broker to pay them, so a tenant can't be charged by an agent the landlord engaged. Germany has applied a similar "whoever commissions the agent pays" principle to rentals since 2015. If you hire your own agent to search on your behalf, expect to pay them.

Markets that restrict tenant fees

In England, most fees charged to tenants by letting agents and landlords were banned in 2019, leaving rent, a capped deposit and a short list of permitted charges. Scotland has long barred letting agents from charging tenants for finding them a home. In much of Canada and Australia, the landlord normally pays the agent who lists a rental. In markets like these, any request for a "finder's fee" or "admin fee" deserves a pointed question about whether it's legal.

Markets where tenants pay an agency fee

In some markets, including Dubai, Hong Kong and many Indian cities, tenants commonly pay an agency fee or brokerage. Here the key checks are registration and paperwork. In Dubai, brokers should be registered with the Real Estate Regulatory Agency and the tenancy registered through Ejari. In Hong Kong, estate agents are licensed by the Estate Agents Authority. In Singapore, agents appear on the Council for Estate Agencies' public register.

When an agent is worth it — and when it isn't

An agent tends to pay for themselves when:

  • You're moving from another city or country and can't view many places in person.
  • The market is fast enough that good units are gone within days of listing.
  • You're renting in a locator market and the service costs you nothing.
  • You're new to the country and need help with applications, local credit checks or guarantor requirements.
  • You want a condo unit or a private landlord's home that is only marketed through agents.

You can usually skip one when you have time, know the neighbourhoods, and most listings come directly from landlords or management companies. Before hiring anyone, ask your employer whether a relocation package includes a destination consultant — that help may already be paid for.

How to vet an agent

  1. Check the licence or registration. Where agents must be licensed, the regulator usually runs a free public lookup. Search the agent's name and their firm, and confirm the licence is active.
  2. Confirm the firm. Agents usually work under a licensed brokerage or agency. Call the firm's main number — found independently, not the one in the message you received — and confirm the agent works there.
  3. Get the arrangement in writing. Who they represent, what they'll do, how they're paid, and what you owe, if anything, and when.
  4. Test their local knowledge. A good agent can explain the standard lease, deposit rules, how applications work and which buildings have known issues, without overselling.
  5. Watch how they handle money. Professionals take payment through traceable channels, issue receipts and hold deposits the way local law requires.

Questions to ask before you work with anyone

  • Who do you represent in this deal — me, the landlord or both?
  • How are you paid, and by whom? Will I owe anything, and when?
  • Which buildings or landlords don't you work with?
  • Which regulator are you licensed or registered with?
  • What is the full move-in cost: deposit, first month's rent and every fee?
  • What's negotiable here — rent, move-in date, parking, a rent-free period?
  • What happens to an application fee or holding deposit if I'm declined or change my mind?
  • How will you store and share my personal documents?

Red flags and rental scams

Rental scams follow a small number of scripts. Most rely on urgency, and on you paying before you've confirmed that the person actually controls the apartment.

  • Pay before you see. A request for a deposit, holding fee or first month's rent before any viewing is the biggest warning sign there is.
  • Untraceable payment. Wire transfers to personal accounts, cryptocurrency, gift cards, peer-to-peer payment apps or cash without a receipt.
  • Too good for the block. A rent well below similar units nearby, with photos that look like a show suite.
  • The absent owner. A landlord who is "working overseas" and will post the keys once you've paid.
  • Copied listings. Photos or text that appear elsewhere under a different address or name. A reverse image search takes a minute.
  • Manufactured pressure. "Three other people are applying today." Busy markets are real, but legitimate agents still let you read the lease.
  • Too much information too early. Requests for your passport, social insurance or social security number, or banking details before you've applied for a specific unit.
  • No paper trail. No written lease, no licence number, no business address.

Another variation is the hijacked listing: a scammer copies a real unit's photos and address, then advertises it at a lower rent. To check, ask the building's management office whether the unit is available and who is marketing it, and where land or property-tax records are public, confirm the owner's name.

The safe-payment rule

Pay nothing until you have seen the unit in person or on a live video call where you direct the camera, confirmed that the landlord or manager owns or controls it, and read the lease. Then pay by a traceable method into an account in the name of the landlord, management company or licensed agency, and keep the receipt.

Protecting yourself on application and payment

Landlords and agents can reasonably ask for photo ID, proof of income, an employment letter, previous landlord references and consent to a credit check. Share these only once you're applying for a specific unit, by a secure method rather than an open email thread where possible. Some renters add a line such as "for a rental application at this address only" across copies of their ID.

Deposit rules vary widely. Some places cap deposits, some don't allow security deposits at all, and some — England, for example — require most deposits to be protected in a government-approved scheme. Ask what's legal before you pay, and never hand over a deposit in cash.

If you're new to the country and have no local credit history, offer alternatives: an employer letter confirming salary, proof of savings, a guarantor, or, where the law permits, a larger deposit. If you think you've been scammed, contact your bank immediately, report it to the police and to the platform where the listing appeared, and keep every message.

Getting the most from a good agent

  • Give a sharp brief. Budget, must-haves, deal-breakers, move-in date and the address you'll commute to. Our rent budget calculator gives you a defensible range.
  • Be ready to apply on the spot. Keep a digital folder of documents so you can apply the same day you find the right place.
  • Ask for candour. A good agent will tell you when a unit is overpriced or a building has a reputation for slow repairs.
  • Respond quickly. In fast markets, the agent's best listings go to the clients who answer first.
