The most common regret among new downtown residents isn't price or size. It's sleep. Sirens, bars letting out, delivery trucks at dawn and streetlights through the blinds can wear you down. The good news: most problems can be spotted before you sign, and many can be fixed after.

Spotting noise before you sign

  • Visit at the worst time. For nightlife streets, that's late on a Friday or Saturday. For loading zones and garbage pickup, it's early morning.
  • Find out what's below and beside you. Restaurants mean kitchen exhaust fans and deliveries; bars mean late crowds; gyms mean dropped weights.
  • Check the windows. Double- or triple-glazed windows make a dramatic difference. Close them during your visit and listen.
  • Look at the bedroom's position. A bedroom facing an interior courtyard is often far quieter than one facing the street.
  • Ask about construction plans for nearby lots.
  • Trace the emergency routes. Streets leading to a hospital or fire station carry sirens around the clock; a block or two away can make a big difference.

Light: the other sleep thief

Downtown nights are bright. Streetlights, illuminated signs and neighbouring towers can light up a bedroom. Check whether the unit has blackout blinds or the window frames that allow them, and look at what's directly across from the bedroom window.

Light also comes from inside the building: corridor lighting under the bedroom door and the standby lights on electronics. A draught stopper at the foot of the door and a little tape over small device lights cost almost nothing. In higher-latitude cities, summer sunrise matters as much as streetlights; an east-facing bedroom will be bright long before your alarm goes off.

Not all noise behaves the same way. High-pitched sounds such as voices and traffic hiss are blocked fairly well by good windows and soft furnishings. Low-frequency sound — bass from a club, idling trucks, rooftop chillers and fans — travels through walls and glass much more easily, and earplugs help less with it. If there's a venue with amplified music nearby, or large mechanical equipment on the roof next door, listen at night with the windows shut before you commit.

Questions to ask before you sign

  • What's directly above, below and beside the bedroom?
  • When are garbage collections and deliveries for the building and the businesses below?
  • Are the windows single, double or triple glazed?
  • Is the building concrete or wood-frame? Concrete usually transmits less sound between units.
  • Are quiet hours in the building rules, and are they enforced?
  • Have there been noise complaints about nearby venues?

Fixing problems after you move in

Fixing noise

  • Soft surfaces absorb sound. Rugs, curtains, upholstered furniture and full bookshelves reduce echo and some outside noise.
  • Window inserts — removable secondary panes — can reduce street noise without replacing windows. Ask your landlord first.
  • Seal gaps around doors and windows with weather-stripping.
  • Move the bed to the wall furthest from the noise source.
  • White noise masks irregular sounds like sirens and voices.

Fixing light

Blackout curtains or blinds solve most light problems. A sleep mask is the cheapest fix. Warm, dim lighting in the evening helps your body wind down despite the bright city outside.

When the noise is a neighbour or a venue

Start with the lowest-key fix. Many neighbours simply don't know their footsteps, music or late-night laundry carry; a friendly note or conversation solves more problems than a formal complaint. If it continues, keep a short log of dates, times and what you heard, then raise it with building management, citing the quiet-hours rules if there are any.

For noise from outside the building, such as a venue, construction outside permitted hours or a persistent alarm, most cities have noise bylaws and take complaints through a non-emergency or 311 line. Licensed venues may also have conditions on their licence about music and closing times, which the licensing authority enforces. Renters in many places also have a legal right to the "quiet enjoyment" of their home; if a landlord ignores a serious, ongoing problem, local tenant advice services can explain your options.

Heat and air: the hidden sleep factor

Downtown apartments, especially in glass towers, can get hot at night. Bedrooms that must keep windows open for cooling let in street noise. Check how the unit is cooled, whether you can control it, and whether windows open enough for cross-ventilation. A quiet fan or a portable air conditioner allowed by the building can make the difference between closed windows and a sleepless summer.

In winter the problem can flip. Heating in some older buildings is controlled for the whole building and can leave bedrooms too warm and dry. A thermostat you control, a humidifier and a window that opens a crack are worth asking about. If you're viewing in winter, ask what the unit is like in July — and if you're viewing in summer, ask about January.

Shift workers and day sleepers

If you work nights, your downtown noise map looks different. Daytime brings construction, which city rules typically allow during working hours; deliveries and waste collection; school playgrounds and recess; and building work scheduled for business hours, such as fire alarm tests, elevator maintenance, suite repairs and window cleaning.

  • Choose a bedroom away from active construction sites, loading docks and schools, and check planning portals for projects about to start.
  • Ask management to add you to notices about alarm testing, water shut-offs and contractor work, so you can plan your sleep around them.
  • Fit full blackout blinds, and consider a door sign or intercom setting that stops daytime deliveries waking you.
  • Use white noise during the day, when irregular street sounds are at their peak.

Building a wind-down routine in a bright city

Downtown life makes it easy to stay out late and keep screens on. A consistent bedtime, dim warm light in the last hour of the evening and a dark, cool bedroom help your body ignore the city's brightness. If noise or poor sleep persists despite these changes, it's worth talking to a healthcare professional.

The two-visit rule

Never sign for a downtown apartment after a single daytime viewing. Go back at night. The ten minutes you spend standing in a bedroom at 11 p.m. on a Friday may save you a year of poor sleep.

When noise is a dealbreaker

If you're a light sleeper, look at upper floors facing away from the street, buildings set back from major roads, or residential blocks a few streets away from nightlife districts. Use our Find Your Downtown quiz with nightlife set low — it will favour city centres with quieter residential districts.

If you're already in a lease with a sleep problem you can't fix, ask the landlord whether a quieter unit in the same building is coming free. A transfer is often simpler for both sides than breaking the lease.
