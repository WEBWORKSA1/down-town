Sleeping Well Downtown: Noise, Light and Picking the Right Unit

How to judge an apartment for sleep before you sign — and how to fix noise and light problems after you move in.

By the Down.Town editorsUpdated October 20263 min read

The most common regret among new downtown residents isn't price or size. It's sleep. Sirens, bars letting out, delivery trucks at dawn and streetlights through the blinds can wear you down. The good news: most problems can be spotted before you sign, and many can be fixed after.

Spotting noise before you sign

  • Visit at the worst time. For nightlife streets, that's late on a Friday or Saturday. For loading zones and garbage pickup, it's early morning.
  • Find out what's below and beside you. Restaurants mean kitchen exhaust fans and deliveries; bars mean late crowds; gyms mean dropped weights.
  • Check the windows. Double- or triple-glazed windows make a dramatic difference. Close them during your visit and listen.
  • Look at the bedroom's position. A bedroom facing an interior courtyard is often far quieter than one facing the street.
  • Ask about construction plans for nearby lots.

Light: the other sleep thief

Downtown nights are bright. Streetlights, illuminated signs and neighbouring towers can light up a bedroom. Check whether the unit has blackout blinds or the window frames that allow them, and look at what's directly across from the bedroom window.

Fixing noise after you move in

  • Soft surfaces absorb sound. Rugs, curtains, upholstered furniture and full bookshelves reduce echo and some outside noise.
  • Window inserts — removable secondary panes — can reduce street noise without replacing windows. Ask your landlord first.
  • Seal gaps around doors and windows with weather-stripping.
  • Move the bed to the wall furthest from the noise source.
  • White noise masks irregular sounds like sirens and voices.

Fixing light

Blackout curtains or blinds solve most light problems. A sleep mask is the cheapest fix. Warm, dim lighting in the evening helps your body wind down despite the bright city outside.

Questions to ask before you sign

  • What's directly above, below and beside the bedroom?
  • When are garbage collections and deliveries for the building and the businesses below?
  • Are the windows single, double or triple glazed?
  • Is the building concrete or wood-frame? Concrete usually transmits less sound between units.
  • Are quiet hours in the building rules, and are they enforced?
  • Have there been noise complaints about nearby venues?

Heat and air: the hidden sleep factor

Downtown apartments, especially in glass towers, can get hot at night. Bedrooms that must keep windows open for cooling let in street noise. Check how the unit is cooled, whether you can control it, and whether windows open enough for cross-ventilation. A quiet fan or a portable air conditioner allowed by the building can make the difference between closed windows and a sleepless summer.

Building a wind-down routine in a bright city

Downtown life makes it easy to stay out late and keep screens on. A consistent bedtime, dim warm light in the last hour of the evening and a dark, cool bedroom help your body ignore the city's brightness. If noise or poor sleep persists despite these changes, it's worth talking to a healthcare professional.

The two-visit rule

Never sign for a downtown apartment after a single daytime viewing. Go back at night. The ten minutes you spend standing in a bedroom at 11 p.m. on a Friday may save you a year of poor sleep.

When noise is a dealbreaker

If you're a light sleeper, look at upper floors facing away from the street, buildings set back from major roads, or residential blocks a few streets away from nightlife districts.
