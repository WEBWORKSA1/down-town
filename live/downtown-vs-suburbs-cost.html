Downtown vs. Suburbs: How to Run the Real Cost Comparison

Rent is only half the story. A clear method for comparing housing, transport and time costs between a downtown apartment and a suburban home.

By the Down.Town editorsUpdated October 20263 min read

The usual argument goes: downtown is expensive, the suburbs are cheap. That's true if you only compare rent or mortgage payments. It often stops being true once you add transport and the value of your time. Here's a method for running the comparison honestly, with your own numbers.

The formula

For each option, add up four monthly costs:

  1. Housing: rent or mortgage, property tax, building or HOA fees, insurance and utilities.
  2. Transport: car payments or depreciation, insurance, fuel or charging, parking, maintenance, tolls, transit passes, ride-hailing and bike costs.
  3. Time: monthly commuting hours multiplied by a value per hour you choose.
  4. Lifestyle extras: anything you'd have to pay for in one place but not the other — a gym membership downtown vs. a home gym in the suburbs, delivery fees, childcare differences.

Total monthly cost = housing + transport + time + extras. Compare the totals, not the rents.

A worked example

The numbers below are illustrative — they show how the method works, not prices in any particular city. Swap in your own.

In this example, the suburban house is 500 cheaper on housing but almost 1,000 more expensive in total once two cars and commute time are counted. Your numbers may point the other way — that's the point of running them.

Estimating transport honestly

Car costs are the most underestimated line in household budgets because they arrive in irregular chunks: a tyre here, a repair there, insurance once a year. To estimate:

  • Use depreciation, not just your loan payment. A car loses value every month whether or not you finance it.
  • Add annual insurance, registration and maintenance, then divide by twelve.
  • Include parking at home and at work. Downtown parking can cost as much as a transit pass several times over.

Our car vs. car-free calculator does this for you and shows the break-even point.

What value should you put on time?

There is no right answer, but there are reasonable anchors. Some people use their after-tax hourly wage. Others use a lower figure, since commuting time isn't always lost — you can read on a train, but not while driving. Pick a value you'd honestly pay to get an hour back, and run the comparison with it. Then run it at zero to see how much the decision depends on time.

The costs that don't fit in a spreadsheet

  • Space. A suburban home usually offers more room per dollar. If you work from home, a dedicated office may be worth a lot.
  • Schools. School quality and catchment boundaries can dominate the decision for families.
  • Social life. Living within walking distance of friends, restaurants and events changes how often you go out — for better or worse for your budget.
  • Health. Walking and cycling for daily errands adds activity without extra time; long drives subtract it.
  • Resilience. Households with one car or none are less exposed to fuel price spikes; households with more space are better set up for working from home.

Questions to ask yourself before deciding

  • How many days a week will each adult commute, and how might that change?
  • Could the household drop a car entirely, or only one of two?
  • How much space do you need for work, children, hobbies or guests?
  • Which school options matter, and where are they?
  • How often do you go out, and how much do you spend getting there?
  • How long do you expect to stay? Moving costs can tip a short-term decision.

Run your own numbers

Start with the rent budget calculator for the housing line and the car vs. car-free calculator for transport. Then add your commute hours at the value you choose.

The bottom line

Downtown living looks expensive when you compare rents in isolation. Compared on total cost, it is often competitive — and sometimes cheaper — for households that can drop a car. For households that need space, a car anyway, or a particular school, the suburbs can still win. The only wrong comparison is the one that stops at rent.
