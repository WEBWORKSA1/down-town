A long commercial lease is a big bet. A pop-up shop lets you test a downtown location for weeks or months before committing. It's also a marketing event in its own right, and higher vacancy in many downtowns since 2020 has made short-term space easier to find.

Done well, a pop-up answers the question every landlord and lender will ask: will people at this address buy what you sell, at prices that cover the rent? Done badly, it's an expensive party. The difference lies in the format you choose, the terms you agree, the permits you sort out early and the numbers you collect every day you're open.

Why pop-ups work

  • Lower risk. You pay for a short period and avoid long commitments.
  • Real data. You measure actual sales, foot traffic and customer interest at a specific address.
  • Buzz. Limited-time openings create urgency.
  • Landlord relationships. A good pop-up can turn into a long-term lease on better terms.

Choosing the right pop-up format

Not every pop-up needs a full storefront. Match the format to what you're testing:

FormatBest forTypical length
Vacant storefrontTesting a permanent location and full product rangeOne to six months
Shop-in-shopBorrowing an established store's customersA few weeks to a season
Market stall or kioskTesting products and price points cheaplyA day to a few weekends
Event activationBrand awareness during festivals and conferencesOne to ten days

Finding space

Ask local brokers about short-term availability, contact landlords of vacant storefronts directly, and check with the downtown business association — many run programmes connecting pop-ups with vacant spaces. Some cities offer incentives or simplified permits for temporary uses.

Lease or licence?

Most short-term deals are licences rather than leases: permission to occupy for set dates without the rights a lease carries. In England and Wales, for example, a licence generally gives no security of tenure, and the government has published a model "meanwhile use" lease for temporary occupation of empty buildings. In North America you'll see licence or temporary occupancy agreements. Whatever the label, get it in writing and read the handback terms.

Where vacancy programmes help

Vacant-storefront programmes, often run by business improvement districts or city economic development teams, can bring reduced fees, help with permits and a ready-made marketing push. In England, councils also have powers to auction short leases on persistently vacant high-street units, which can open doors for small operators. Ask what's available before you approach landlords cold.

What to negotiate

  • A flat fee or percentage of sales instead of standard rent.
  • Clear dates, including setup and takedown.
  • Which utilities and services are included.
  • Insurance requirements and liability.
  • An option to convert to a longer lease on agreed terms.
  • The deposit, and exactly when it comes back.
  • The condition at handover and handback — photograph everything on day one.
  • Access hours for deliveries and setup.
  • Use of the window and fascia for your signage.

Permits, insurance and tax

Short doesn't mean exempt. Start this list six weeks out, because city departments don't speed up for a pop-up:

  • Signage. Banners and fascia signs can need permission even for a few weeks, and heritage districts are stricter.
  • Food. Selling food usually needs a temporary permit or registration with the local health authority, and some cities require a commercial kitchen for preparation.
  • Alcohol. In England and Wales, a Temporary Event Notice can cover short events; Canadian provinces and many US states issue special-occasion or temporary permits.
  • Sales tax. You may need to register for sales tax, GST, HST or VAT where you sell, even briefly. Ask an accountant if you're trading outside your home city, state or country.
  • Insurance. Public or general liability, product liability and cover for your stock. North American landlords often ask to be named as an additional insured.
  • Fire and occupancy. Keep exits clear, especially if you expect queues, and respect any occupancy limit.

Setup on a budget

Use modular fixtures you can reuse, focus spending on lighting and the window, and keep inventory lean. Make sure you can accept card payments and capture customer contacts.

  • Card reader with an offline mode, plus a charged backup.
  • Reliable mobile data or Wi-Fi.
  • Bright, warm lighting for the window and the counter.
  • Modular shelving, rails or plinths you can reuse.
  • Clear signage with your name, dates and hours.
  • A tally counter or app to count visitors.
  • A QR code or sign-up sheet for your mailing list.
  • Bags, packaging, a cleaning kit and a first-aid kit.

A pop-up timeline that works

  1. Eight weeks out: shortlist locations, count foot traffic, and contact landlords or programmes.
  2. Six weeks out: agree terms in writing, arrange insurance and check whether you need temporary permits for signage, food or alcohol.
  3. Four weeks out: order fixtures and stock, set up payments, and plan the opening.
  4. Two weeks out: announce the dates to your email list and social channels, and contact neighbouring businesses and local media.
  5. Opening week: run an event, collect contact details from every customer you can, and track sales daily.
  6. After closing: compare results with your permanent-store threshold and decide: sign, try another location, or stay online.

What to measure

Track daily foot traffic, conversion, average sale and repeat visits. Survey customers on where they live and work. Compare results with what you'd need to cover permanent rent, using the storefront revenue estimator.

Worked example: reading a four-week test

Suppose the estimator says a permanent store at this address needs about 60 transactions a day to cover its costs. You count visitors and sales every day of a four-week pop-up; here are illustrative daily averages:

Week one is inflated by your own network and the novelty, so ignore it. Weeks two to four settle in the low 50s, below the line. But the trend is rising, and about two in five visitors buy, so the problem isn't the product. The address draws people in; to carry permanent rent, more of them need to come through the door, or each buyer needs to spend more so that fewer transactions cover the costs. That points to a better window, a higher average sale, or a lower rent than you were quoted.

Common pop-up mistakes

  • Too short to learn anything. A single weekend tells you about the weekend. Run at least four weeks if you're testing a permanent location.
  • Too much stock. Leftover inventory eats the profit. Order lean and restock fast.
  • No data capture. If you don't count visitors and collect contacts, you've paid for an expensive party.
  • Ignoring the neighbours. Nearby businesses can send you customers — or complain about your queue.
  • Leaving permits to the last week. A sign or food permit that arrives after closing day is no use to anyone.
  • Judging on opening week. Your friends and followers are not your market. Decide on the steady weeks.
