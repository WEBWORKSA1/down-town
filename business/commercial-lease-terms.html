Commercial Lease Terms Decoded: What to Negotiate and Why

Gross vs. net, CAM, tenant improvements, exclusivity, co-tenancy and personal guarantees — the clauses that decide whether a downtown lease works.

By the Down.Town editorsUpdated October 20263 min read

A commercial lease can run for five or ten years and commit you to hundreds of thousands in payments. Most first-time tenants focus on the rent per square foot and miss the clauses that matter more. Here's a plain-language guide to the terms you'll meet and what to ask for. It's general information, not legal advice — have a lawyer review any lease before you sign.

Rent structures

TypeWhat you payWatch for
Gross leaseOne rent amount; the landlord covers most building costs.Higher base rent; check what's excluded.
Net leaseBase rent plus some building costs.Which costs are passed through.
Triple net (NNN)Base rent plus your share of property tax, insurance and maintenance.Unpredictable increases; ask for history and caps.
Percentage rentBase rent plus a share of sales above a threshold.Reporting obligations and the threshold level.

Common area maintenance (CAM)

CAM charges cover shared spaces and services. Ask for the last few years' actual CAM costs, what's included, and whether there's a cap on annual increases. Exclude capital improvements that mainly benefit the landlord.

Tenant improvement (TI) allowance

A TI allowance is money the landlord contributes to your fit-out. Landlords often offer more TI in exchange for a longer lease. Clarify how and when it's paid — upfront, in stages or as reimbursement.

Rent-free period

A few months of free rent while you build out is common, especially where vacancy is high. Make sure the free period starts when you get access, not when you open.

Term and renewal options

A shorter initial term with renewal options gives you flexibility. Make sure renewal rent is defined — a fixed increase or market rent with a cap.

Use clause and exclusivity

The use clause defines what you may do in the space. Keep it broad enough to let your business evolve. An exclusivity clause stops the landlord leasing nearby space in the same building or development to a direct competitor.

Co-tenancy

In shopping centres or mixed-use developments, a co-tenancy clause can reduce your rent if key anchor tenants leave. It matters if your traffic depends on them.

Assignment and subletting

If you sell your business or need to exit, you'll want the right to assign the lease or sublet. Negotiate for the landlord's consent "not to be unreasonably withheld".

Personal guarantee

Landlords often ask small business owners to guarantee the lease personally. Try to limit it — for example, to a fixed number of months' rent, or have it fall away after a period of on-time payments.

Signage, hours and operations

Check your rights to exterior signage, any required opening hours, and rules about deliveries, noise and outdoor seating.

Relocation and demolition clauses

Some leases let the landlord move you to another space or end the lease for redevelopment. Negotiate for notice periods and compensation.

A negotiation checklist

  1. Total occupancy cost estimate for year one and year five.
  2. Rent-free period and when it starts.
  3. Tenant improvement allowance amount and payment schedule.
  4. Caps on CAM and pass-through increases.
  5. Renewal options and how renewal rent is set.
  6. Exclusivity for your core product or service.
  7. Assignment and subletting rights.
  8. Limits on any personal guarantee.
  9. Signage rights, including projecting and window signs.
  10. Permitted hours and any required opening hours.
  11. Responsibility for HVAC, roof and structural repairs.
  12. Notice and compensation for relocation or redevelopment.

The letter of intent

Most leases begin with a letter of intent (LOI) that sets out the main terms before lawyers draft the lease. It's usually non-binding, but it frames everything that follows, so negotiate the important points at this stage. Changing terms later is harder.

Working with a tenant broker

Tenant-side brokers represent the business, not the landlord, and are often paid through the landlord's commission. They know local rents, incentives and which landlords are flexible. For a first lease, a good tenant broker and a lawyer who specialises in commercial leases can save far more than they cost.
