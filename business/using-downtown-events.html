Downtown events bring crowds that most small businesses could never attract on their own: a film festival fills the streets with visitors for ten days, a marathon puts tens of thousands of spectators on the sidewalks, a holiday market turns a quiet square into a destination. But crowds don't automatically turn into sales. Some businesses have their best week of the year; others find their regulars stay away and the festival-goers walk straight past. The difference is planning.

The ten steps below are grouped by when you do them: plan, prepare, partner, follow up and measure. Most of the work happens weeks before the crowd arrives.

Plan: pick the events worth your effort

1. Build an event calendar for your block

List every major recurring event within walking distance: festivals, parades, conferences, sports seasons, holiday markets and school calendars. For each one, note the dates, the expected crowd size, who comes (families, tourists, business visitors, students) and when they're on the street. Our downtown festival calendar lists signature events for every city we cover. Your local business association usually publishes a more detailed calendar.

Add the periods that empty downtown, too: school holidays, long weekends and, in Japan, Golden Week from late April to early May, when office districts go quiet while leisure districts fill. Knowing your slow weeks is as useful as knowing your busy ones.

2. Decide which events are worth it

Not every crowd is your crowd. A marathon brings spectators who stand in one place for hours — good for a coffee counter, not for a furniture showroom. A conference brings business visitors with expense accounts at lunch and dinner. A music festival brings young crowds who want quick, cheap food. Score each event on two questions: will these people want what I sell? and will they pass my door? Focus effort on the events that score high on both.

Know your city's calendar

The big dates differ from downtown to downtown, and so does what they do to trade. A few patterns worth knowing:

  • Festival summers. Montréal's festival season peaks with the jazz festival from late June to early July, filling the Quartier des Spectacles for days at a time.
  • Film and sport. The Toronto International Film Festival takes over downtown Toronto in September; Melbourne fills with tennis fans for the Australian Open in January.
  • Marathons. The London Marathon (April) and the New York City Marathon (November) bring huge crowds and long road closures to London and New York.
  • Lunar New Year. In Singapore and Hong Kong it falls in January or February: a gifting peak, followed by days when many businesses close.
  • Ramadan. In Dubai, trade shifts towards the evening after iftar, and the month moves earlier each year on the Gregorian calendar.
  • Christmas markets. In Berlin and across German-speaking Europe they run from late November through December.

Prepare: offer, stock, staff and hours

3. Adjust what you offer

  • Simplify. A shorter menu or a curated selection speeds up service when crowds arrive.
  • Make it portable. Grab-and-go items, smaller packaging and items people can carry around for the rest of the day.
  • Theme it. A limited item named after the event gives people a reason to choose you and to post about it.
  • Bundle for gifts around holidays — pre-wrapped bundles at a few price points sell quickly.

4. Staff and stock for the peak

Look at last year's sales for the same event if you have them. Schedule extra staff for the peak hours, not the whole day, and brief everyone on the simplified offer. Order extra stock early — suppliers get busy too. Plan for card-payment volume and have a backup if your connection drops.

5. Adjust your hours and your door

Events often run outside normal trading hours. Opening an hour earlier for a morning parade or staying open late after a concert can capture a crowd that your neighbours miss. Make your entrance obvious: a sidewalk sign (where permitted), a clear view inside and a staff member at the door if the street is busy.

Partner: organisers, sponsors and your business association

6. Work with organisers and the business association

Ask organisers about vendor spots, sponsorship packages, inclusion in printed or digital guides and official partner offers. Business improvement districts and associations often coordinate shop-window competitions, extended-hours campaigns and passport-style promotions that send visitors from shop to shop. Joining these is usually cheap and effective.

Read the vendor and sponsor terms

Vendor agreements set fees, insurance, hours, power and clean-up duties. Large events often sell category exclusivity to sponsors, so you may not be able to sell or advertise a rival product on site. Major international sporting events can also bring temporary advertising and street-trading restrictions around venues. And event names and logos are usually trademarks: call your special something descriptive rather than borrowing the official name.

Follow up: turn visitors into regulars

7. Turn one-time visitors into regulars

The real value of an event is the customers who come back after it ends. Offer a small reason to return — a discount on the next visit, a stamp card, an invitation to a follow-up evening — and collect email addresses or social follows at the till. Follow up within a week while the visit is still fresh.

8. Protect your regulars

Events can push away the locals who keep you in business the other 50 weeks of the year. Tell them in advance when you'll be busy, offer pre-orders or quiet hours, and thank them afterwards. A regular who feels looked after will forgive a crowded Saturday.

Host your own events

9. Create crowds on your own schedule

You don't have to wait for the city's calendar. Workshops, launches, tastings, talks and collaborations with neighbouring businesses create crowds on your schedule and at your scale. Two or three businesses on the same block running a joint evening can draw more people than any of them alone.

Check the basics first: your occupancy limit, whether alcohol needs a temporary permit, whether amplified sound or sidewalk use needs city approval, and whether your insurance covers the event. Playing recorded music to customers usually needs a public-performance licence from the local music licensing body. If your shop is too small, our guide to choosing a downtown event venue and the venue budget calculator will help.

Measure and repeat

10. Compare every event with a normal week

After each event, record sales, transactions, average spend, staff hours and any new contacts captured. Compare with a normal week. Over a year, you'll know exactly which events are worth the effort — and which you can skip.

Sales rose by more than half, but staff hours rose by about 60% and each customer spent less. The day only paid if the margin on the extra sales covered 20 extra staff hours and any waste. The nine returning customers are the real prize: if that number grows each year, the event is building your business, not just filling one Saturday.

Your event-week checklist

  • Confirm dates, road closures and transit changes with the organiser or the city.
  • Book deliveries around closures, and order extra stock and packaging.
  • Brief staff on the simplified offer and the peak hours.
  • Test card readers, and set up an offline mode or backup.
  • Prepare door and window signage, and a sidewalk sign if permitted.
  • Set up contact capture: a QR code to your newsletter or a stamp card.
  • Tell regulars when you'll be busy and offer pre-orders.
  • Plan for bins, cleaning and washroom pressure.
  • Write down a normal week's numbers so you have something to compare with.
