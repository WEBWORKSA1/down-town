Opening a Downtown Storefront: The Step-by-Step Playbook

From validating the location to opening day — a practical sequence for launching a shop, café or studio in a city centre.

By the Down.Town editorsUpdated October 20263 min read

A downtown storefront gives you something no website can: people walking past your door every day. It also comes with higher rent, stricter rules and tougher competition than a suburban strip. This playbook walks through the sequence that experienced operators follow, in the order that saves the most money and the most regret.

1. Prove the location before you fall in love with the space

Count foot traffic yourself. Stand across the street at the hours you'd be open — weekday lunch, weekday evening, Saturday afternoon — and count people walking past for fifteen minutes at a time. Note who they are: office workers, residents, students, tourists. A location with heavy weekday traffic and a dead weekend may be ideal for a lunch café and wrong for a gift shop.

Then plug your counts into our storefront revenue estimator to see what share of passers-by you'd need to convert to cover rent.

2. Check zoning and permitted use

Before negotiating, confirm with the city that your use is permitted at the address. Restaurants, bars, places of worship, clinics and businesses with late hours often need specific zoning or special permits. Some downtowns restrict certain uses on main streets to protect retail continuity.

3. Understand the true occupancy cost

Base rent is only part of it. Ask for a full estimate of occupancy costs, which may include:

  • Common area maintenance (CAM) charges
  • Property tax pass-throughs
  • Building insurance pass-throughs
  • Utilities, and whether the space is separately metered
  • Business improvement district levies, where they apply

Many retail operators aim to keep total occupancy costs to a modest share of expected sales — often cited as roughly 10% or less for retail, with restaurants commonly aiming in a similar range. Use these as sanity checks, not rules.

4. Negotiate the lease

Commercial leases are negotiable. Typical points include rent-free months during fit-out, a tenant improvement allowance, renewal options, an exclusivity clause that stops the landlord leasing to a direct competitor, and limits on personal guarantees. Our guide to commercial lease terms explains each one. Have a lawyer review the lease before you sign.

5. Plan the fit-out

Get contractor quotes early, and ask what the landlord will deliver: a "vanilla box" with basic finishes, or a bare shell. Budget for permits, inspections and a contingency — fit-outs commonly run over budget and over time. Signage often needs separate approval, and heritage districts may have design rules.

6. Register, license and insure

Register the business, obtain the local business licence and any specific permits (food handling, liquor, signage, sidewalk patio), and arrange liability and property insurance. Your landlord will usually require proof of insurance before handing over the keys.

7. Build demand before opening day

  • Set up and verify your online business profile so you appear in local searches as soon as you open.
  • Put a "coming soon" sign in the window with a way to sign up for opening news.
  • Introduce yourself to neighbouring businesses and the local business association.
  • Plan an opening event that gives people a reason to come in.

8. Open, measure and adjust

Track daily sales, the number of customers and average spend. Compare them with your foot-traffic counts to measure conversion. If people walk past but don't come in, work on your window and signage. If they come in but don't buy, work on product, pricing and staff. Our guide to turning foot traffic into customers goes deeper.

A realistic timeline

StageTypical duration
Location search and foot-traffic counts1–3 months
Letter of intent and lease negotiation1–2 months
Design and permits1–4 months, depending on the city and the use
Fit-out and inspections1–4 months
Hiring, training and soft opening2–6 weeks

Restaurants, bars and any business needing major plumbing, ventilation or a change of use usually sit at the long end of each range. Negotiate your rent-free period with this timeline in mind.
