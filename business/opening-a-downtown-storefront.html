A downtown storefront gives you something no website can: people walking past your door every day. It also comes with higher rent, stricter rules and tougher competition than a suburban strip. This playbook walks through the sequence that experienced operators follow, in the order that saves the most money and the most regret.

The eight steps fall into three phases. The order matters: each phase commits more money than the one before, so the cheapest time to discover a problem is as early as possible. A space that fails the foot-traffic test costs you a few afternoons; one that fails a permit check after you've signed can cost you the business.

Phase 1: Before you sign

1. Prove the location before you fall in love with the space

Count foot traffic yourself. Stand across the street at the hours you'd be open — weekday lunch, weekday evening, Saturday afternoon — and count people walking past for fifteen minutes at a time. Note who they are: office workers, residents, students, tourists. A location with heavy weekday traffic and a dead weekend may be ideal for a lunch café and wrong for a gift shop.

Then plug your counts into our storefront revenue estimator to see what share of passers-by you'd need to convert to cover rent.

Look at the surroundings too: what generates visits nearby (a transit station, office towers, a university, a civic market), which neighbours complement you and which compete, and practical details such as which side of the street gets the sun, where the bus stop is, and whether scaffolding or roadworks are coming.

2. Check zoning and permitted use

Before negotiating, confirm with the city that your use is permitted at the address. Restaurants, bars, places of worship, clinics and businesses with late hours often need specific zoning or special permits. Some downtowns restrict certain uses on main streets to protect retail continuity.

3. Understand the true occupancy cost

Base rent is only part of it. Ask for a full estimate of occupancy costs, which may include:

  • Common area maintenance (CAM) charges
  • Property tax pass-throughs
  • Building insurance pass-throughs
  • Utilities, and whether the space is separately metered
  • Business improvement district levies, where they apply

Many retail operators aim to keep total occupancy costs to a modest share of expected sales — often cited as roughly 10% or less for retail, with restaurants commonly aiming in a similar range. Use these as sanity checks, not rules.

Worked example: does the rent work?

Turn the rule of thumb into a daily target you can test on the pavement:

  1. Take the landlord's total annual occupancy cost and multiply by ten. That's the yearly sales the space needs at a 10% occupancy ratio.
  2. Divide by the number of days you'll trade — say six days a week, or about 310 days a year.
  3. Divide again by your expected average sale. The answer is the transactions you need per day.
  4. Compare it with your counts. If the maths says 90 transactions a day and about 2,000 people pass during opening hours, you need roughly one passer-by in 22 to buy something.

Whether that's realistic depends on what you sell: a coffee counter on a commuter route and a furniture showroom live in different worlds. If the plan only works with heroic conversion, the rent is too high for you, however good the space looks.

4. Negotiate the lease

Commercial leases are negotiable. Typical points include rent-free months during fit-out, a tenant improvement allowance, renewal options, an exclusivity clause that stops the landlord leasing to a direct competitor, and limits on personal guarantees. Ask, too, for a permit contingency: the right to walk away if you can't secure the permits you need within an agreed period. Our guide to commercial lease terms explains each one, and negotiating rent covers the tactics. Have a lawyer review the lease before you sign.

Phase 2: Build and get licensed

5. Plan the fit-out

Get contractor quotes early, and ask what the landlord will deliver: a "vanilla box" with basic finishes, or a bare shell. Budget for permits, inspections and a contingency — fit-outs commonly run over budget and over time. Signage often needs separate approval, and heritage districts may have design rules.

A new fit-out generally has to meet current building and accessibility codes, which can mean a step-free entrance, a wider door or an accessible washroom. Food businesses also need to plan for ventilation and extraction, grease management, fire suppression and enough electrical capacity. Our restaurant space checklist covers what to inspect before you commit.

6. Register, license and insure

Register the business, obtain the local business licence and any specific permits (food handling, liquor, signage, sidewalk patio), and arrange liability and property insurance. Your landlord will usually require proof of insurance before handing over the keys. The names and the order change from country to country:

MarketWhat most storefronts need
United StatesZoning approval, building permits and a certificate of occupancy, a city business licence, a state sales-tax permit, a health permit for food and a liquor licence where you serve alcohol.
CanadaZoning bylaw compliance, building permits, a municipal business licence where required, GST/HST registration (GST plus QST in Québec) once you pass the small-supplier threshold, a public-health inspection for food and a provincial liquor licence.
United KingdomThe right planning use class (many shop, café and office uses in England fall within Class E), building regulations sign-off, a business rates account, food business registration with the council at least 28 days before trading, and a premises licence for alcohol.
European UnionCommercial registration, any municipal opening declaration, and food business registration under EU food hygiene rules; details vary by country and city.
AustraliaCouncil development approval for a change of use or fit-out, food business registration with the council, an ABN and GST registration above the threshold.
JapanA food business permit from the local public health centre, with a certified food hygiene supervisor on staff, plus fire-safety notifications.

Requirements vary by city and by use. Confirm the current list with your municipality before you sign a lease.

Phase 3: Launch

7. Build demand before opening day

  • Set up and verify your online business profile so you appear in local searches as soon as you open.
  • Put a "coming soon" sign in the window with a way to sign up for opening news.
  • Introduce yourself to neighbouring businesses and the local business association.
  • Plan an opening event that gives people a reason to come in.

8. Open, measure and adjust

Consider a soft opening — a few days of limited hours or invited guests — to test the till, the stock and the staff before the public arrives. Then track daily sales, the number of customers and average spend. Compare them with your foot-traffic counts to measure conversion. If people walk past but don't come in, work on your window and signage. If they come in but don't buy, work on product, pricing and staff. A realistic timeline

StageTypical duration
Location search and foot-traffic counts1–3 months
Letter of intent and lease negotiation1–2 months
Design and permits1–4 months, depending on the city and the use
Fit-out and inspections1–4 months
Hiring, training and soft opening2–6 weeks

Restaurants, bars and any business needing major plumbing, ventilation or a change of use usually sit at the long end of each range. Negotiate your rent-free period with this timeline in mind.

The pre-opening checklist

  • Foot-traffic counts at your actual trading hours, on at least three different days.
  • Written confirmation that your use is permitted at the address.
  • A total occupancy cost figure, not just base rent.
  • A break-even target in transactions per day.
  • A lease reviewed by a commercial lawyer, with a permit contingency.
  • Landlord's delivery condition in writing: vanilla box or shell.
  • Fit-out quotes with a contingency of 10 to 20%.
  • Business registration, tax registration and licences in hand.
  • Insurance certificates sent to the landlord.
  • Payment systems, Wi-Fi and a backup for both tested.
  • Online profile live, with hours, photos and the opening date.
  • Working capital set aside for several months of fixed costs.

Five mistakes that sink first-year storefronts

  1. Signing before the use is confirmed. A café that can't get a ventilation or food permit is just an expensive empty room.
  2. Treating base rent as the rent. Pass-throughs and levies can add a large share on top.
  3. Spending the working capital on the fit-out. Overruns are normal; running out of cash in month three is fatal.
  4. Keeping the wrong hours. Office districts can empty at weekends and after 6 p.m.; nightlife streets wake up late. Trade when your block is busy.
  5. Opening quietly. No signage, no online profile and no opening list means your first weeks are wasted.
