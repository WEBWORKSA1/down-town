Opening downtown usually costs more than opening elsewhere: higher rent, bigger fit-outs, more permits. The good news is that many cities want ground-floor businesses back on their main streets, and a range of funding routes exists. Here's an overview of what to research. Programmes change often, so always confirm current details with the provider.

Very few downtown openings are funded from a single source. Most use a stack: the owner's own money, a loan or two, the landlord's contribution and, with luck, a grant. The skill is matching each source to the cost it suits, applying in the right order, and keeping enough cash in reserve to survive the first slow months.

How much do you actually need?

Before you apply for anything, build a start-up budget with three parts:

  • One-time costs: lease deposit, fit-out, equipment, fixtures, signage, initial inventory, permits, legal and accounting fees.
  • Working capital: enough cash to cover operating losses until sales reach break-even. Many advisers suggest planning for several months of fixed costs, because new stores rarely hit their targets in the first months.
  • Contingency: a buffer — often 10 to 20% of one-time costs — for overruns, which are common in fit-outs.

Lenders will look at this budget closely. An honest, detailed plan builds more confidence than an optimistic one. Get written quotes for the big items rather than estimates, and check your sales assumptions against real foot-traffic counts before you put them in a forecast.

Grants

Grants don't need to be repaid, which makes them competitive. Look for:

  • Façade and storefront improvement grants — many cities and business improvement districts help pay for signage, lighting and façade repairs.
  • Vacant storefront programmes — incentives to fill empty ground-floor spaces, sometimes including rent support.
  • Sector grants — for arts, culture, food, tech or green businesses.
  • Competitions — pitch contests run by associations, banks and foundations.

How downtown grants usually work

Read the fine print before you count on the money. Many storefront grants are reimbursements: you pay the contractor first and claim afterwards, so you still need the cash up front. Most cover a share of eligible costs and expect you to match the rest. Work started before approval is often ineligible, and in heritage districts the design may need sign-off first. Grants can also count as taxable income, so ask your accountant how to treat them.

Loans and guarantees

Government-backed programmes rarely lend to you directly. Instead, they guarantee part of a loan made by a bank or community lender, which makes lenders more willing to fund a first-time business with limited collateral.

WhereProgrammes to research
United StatesThe Small Business Administration guarantees loans made by lenders, including the 7(a) programme, and offers microloans through intermediaries. Its 504 programme funds real estate and major equipment through certified development companies. Community development financial institutions (CDFIs) also lend to small businesses.
CanadaThe Canada Small Business Financing Program shares risk with lenders on loans for equipment and leasehold improvements. Futurpreneur supports young entrepreneurs, and BDC offers financing and advice. Regional development agencies run their own programmes.
United KingdomThe government-backed Start Up Loans scheme, run through the British Business Bank, provides personal loans for business purposes with free mentoring. The bank also backs guarantee schemes offered by accredited lenders.
IrelandLocal Enterprise Offices offer grants, training and mentoring; Microfinance Ireland makes small loans to businesses that struggle to borrow from banks.
European UnionNational promotional banks — KfW in Germany, Bpifrance in France — lend or guarantee through your own bank, and EU-backed guarantees reach small firms through local lenders.
AustraliaThere's no single national small-business lender; the government's business.gov.au portal lists federal, state and territory grants, and state small business commissioners offer advice.
Singapore and Hong KongEnterprise Singapore's Enterprise Financing Scheme shares loan risk with participating lenders; Hong Kong's SME Financing Guarantee Scheme does the same.
Japan and IndiaJapan Finance Corporation lends to small firms and start-ups, and local credit guarantee corporations back bank loans. In India, MUDRA loans and the CGTMSE guarantee scheme support micro and small enterprises.

Other routes

  • Equipment financing for kitchens, fixtures and tools.
  • Revenue-based financing, repaid as a share of sales. Merchant cash advances work similarly but can be very expensive once you convert the fee into an annual rate.
  • Crowdfunding, which can also build an early customer base.
  • Landlord contributions — tenant improvement allowances and rent-free periods are a form of funding. Our guide to commercial lease terms explains how to negotiate them.
  • Friends and family — common and flexible, but put the terms in writing so a loan isn't mistaken for a gift or a share of the business.

Matching funding to the cost

CostFunding that often fits
Fit-out and leasehold improvementsLandlord TI allowance, term loans, government-backed loan programmes
Kitchen, machines and equipmentEquipment financing or leasing
Façade, signage and lightingFaçade improvement grants where available
InventorySupplier credit terms, lines of credit
Early operating lossesOwner equity, investors, lines of credit

The principle: long-lived assets get long-term money, and day-to-day swings get flexible money. Never fund a fit-out from the credit line you'll need for payroll in a slow month.

Worked example: a funding stack for a café

Say a café's start-up budget comes to 100 units in whatever currency you use: 70 of one-time costs, a contingency of 10 and working capital of 20. Here's one way it could be funded.

CostUnitsFunded by
Fit-out and leasehold improvements40Landlord TI allowance (15) and a government-backed term loan (25)
Kitchen equipment18Equipment financing, secured on the equipment
Façade and signage4Façade grant covering half (2), owner equity (2)
Inventory, permits and fees8Owner equity
Contingency10Owner equity
Working capital20Owner equity (10) and an undrawn line of credit (10)

Three things make this stack work. The owner puts in 30 units, which lenders want to see. Each loan matches an asset with a long life. And because the façade grant is paid after the work, owner equity covers it in the meantime. Change the numbers to fit your plan, but keep the logic.

Preparing to apply

Lenders and grant panels look for a clear business plan, realistic financial projections, evidence of demand and your own investment. Use our storefront revenue estimator to sanity-check your sales projections against foot traffic.

Many lenders still think in terms of the "five Cs" of credit: character (your track record and credit history), capacity (cash flow to repay), capital (your own money in the business), collateral (what secures the loan) and conditions (the market and what the money is for). Prepare evidence for each.

  • A business plan of a few pages, with the location and the customer clearly described.
  • A month-by-month cash-flow forecast for at least the first year.
  • A break-even calculation: the daily sales you need to cover costs.
  • Written quotes for fit-out, equipment and signage.
  • A signed letter of intent or a draft lease.
  • Evidence of demand: pre-orders, a waiting list or results from a pop-up test.
  • Personal and business credit reports, recent tax returns and bank statements.
  • A note on which permits you need and where each one stands.

Where to find local programmes

  • Your city's economic development office or small business centre.
  • The local business improvement district or downtown association — called a BID in the US and UK, a BIA in Ontario and an SDC in Québec.
  • Regional development agencies and chambers of commerce.
  • Community foundations and banks' community investment programmes.
  • Free advisory services such as small business development centres and volunteer mentoring networks, which can help you prepare applications.

Red flags

Be cautious with any lender that guarantees approval, charges large upfront fees, or pressures you to sign quickly. Read every term, compare the total cost of borrowing, and get independent advice on large commitments. Be especially careful with fees quoted as a flat "factor" rather than an annual rate, penalties for repaying early, and personal guarantees secured on your home.
