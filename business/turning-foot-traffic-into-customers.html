Downtown storefronts pay a premium for foot traffic. The return on that premium depends on how many passers-by notice you, come in and buy. Think of it as a funnel with three stages — and work on each one.

Each stage has its own levers and its own number. Windows, signs and lighting decide whether people notice you. The doorway, visible prices and a reason to step in decide whether they enter. Product, layout and staff decide whether they buy. Measure the stages separately and you'll know exactly where the leak is, instead of guessing.

Stage 1: Get noticed

  • Windows are your billboard. Change displays regularly, keep them uncluttered and light them well. A window that changes gives regulars a reason to look again.
  • Use projecting signs. People on the sidewalk see signs that stick out from the façade far more easily than flat ones. Check local signage rules.
  • Light the entrance. On winter afternoons, a bright, warm entrance pulls people in.
  • Sidewalk signs can work well where permitted — keep the message short and change it often.

Window display ideas that change behaviour

  • Show one hero product with its price rather than everything you sell.
  • Use height. Products at eye level for someone walking get noticed; items on the floor of the window don't.
  • Show people. A visible, busy interior is the strongest signal that a shop is worth entering.
  • Tell a seasonal story. Rotate displays around holidays, weather and local events.
  • Light for dusk. Many downtown streets are busiest after dark in winter. Bright, warm window lighting matters.

Stage 2: Get them inside

  • Make the door easy. An open or glass door with a visible interior feels welcoming.
  • Show prices and offers. Many people won't walk in if they can't tell whether they can afford it.
  • Create a reason to enter now — a seasonal display, a demonstration or a tasting.
  • Remove the step. A level or ramped entrance helps wheelchair users, parents with buggies and anyone with a suitcase.

The first ten seconds inside

Retailers call the area just inside the door the "decompression zone": people slow down and adjust before they look closely. Keep it clear, place a welcoming display just beyond it, and make sure staff acknowledge people within the first few seconds without pressuring them.

Stage 3: Turn visitors into buyers

  • Greet everyone. A simple greeting increases the chance of a sale.
  • Put best-sellers within reach of the entrance.
  • Offer small, affordable items near the counter.
  • Let people try. Samples, testers and a fitting room turn browsing into buying.
  • Make paying quick. Accept contactless payment and open a second till at peaks; a long queue loses sales.
  • Capture contact details with a loyalty programme or email list.

Measure conversion

Count passers-by for a sample period, count customers who enter, and count transactions. The ratios tell you which stage of the funnel needs work. Our storefront revenue estimator shows how small improvements in conversion change monthly revenue.

A simple conversion benchmark you can run this week

Pick three one-hour windows — weekday lunch, weekday evening, Saturday afternoon. In each, count three numbers: people who walk past, people who come in, and transactions. Divide to get two rates:

  • Entry rate = people who enter ÷ people who pass. This measures your window, signage and entrance.
  • Purchase rate = transactions ÷ people who enter. This measures product, price and service.

Repeat the count a month later after making one change. Changing one thing at a time tells you what worked.

Worked example: reading the numbers

A gift shop counts 900 passers-by, 18 entries and 12 sales in a weekday lunch hour: an entry rate of 2% and a purchase rate of about 67%. On Saturday afternoon it counts 1,200 passers-by, 60 entries and 24 sales: 5% entry, 40% purchase.

The weekday lunch crowd isn't stopping, but the few who come in buy. That's a Stage 1 and 2 problem: the window speaks to weekend browsers, not office workers with half an hour. A sidewalk sign aimed at the lunch crowd and a window built around quick gifts would be the first test. On Saturday the door works but fewer buy, which points to staffing and range: is someone free to help, and is there something at a browser's price?

Online visibility for an offline business

Most people search before they visit. Keep your online business profile complete: hours, photos, categories and up-to-date information. Ask happy customers for reviews and respond to them. Being listed in local guides and directories — including Down.Town's business listings — adds more places for people to find you.

A few details make a disproportionate difference: identical name, address and phone number everywhere you're listed; holiday and event-day hours updated in advance; a photo of the shopfront so people recognise it from the street; and the nearest transit stop or landmark in your description. Reply to reviews briefly and politely, including the critical ones — future customers read the replies.

Partnerships and events

Work with neighbouring businesses on joint promotions, events and window displays. Join the local business association — they often run events, marketing campaigns and seasonal promotions that bring extra visitors. Our guide to using downtown events covers how to plan for festivals, game days and holiday markets.

Bringing people back

A downtown shop's best customers are often people who work or live within a few blocks. Loyalty cards, a short email newsletter, birthday offers and invitations to in-store events turn a single visit into a habit. Partnering with nearby offices — for example, an employee discount — can bring steady weekday traffic.

Signs, sidewalks and street rules

Most cities regulate what you put on, over or facing the public sidewalk, and the rules differ more than you might expect:

  • United States and Canada: sign permits usually come from the city's building or zoning department, and sidewalk signs, displays or café seating may need an encroachment or patio permit.
  • Québec: the Charter of the French Language governs commercial signage, and French must be markedly predominant on exterior signs.
  • England: illuminated and projecting signs often need advertisement consent from the council, and tables and chairs on the pavement need a pavement licence.
  • Historic centres in Europe and many Japanese cities: local rules can limit sign size, colour, materials and lighting.

Whatever the rules, keep a clear walking path: sidewalk boards are a hazard for blind and partially sighted pedestrians, and some cities ban them for that reason. Your business association usually knows what the city actually enforces.

A 30-day street-level plan

  • Week 1: run the three-window count and calculate your entry and purchase rates.
  • Week 2: rebuild the window around one hero product with a price, and fix the lighting.
  • Week 3: work on the entrance — door open where you can, prices visible, a sidewalk sign if permitted.
  • Week 4: brief staff on greeting and one add-on suggestion, and start capturing contacts at the till.
  • Day 30: count again at the same three hours and compare. Keep what worked; change the next thing.
