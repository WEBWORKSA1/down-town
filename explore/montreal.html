In 30 seconds

  • Best for: festivals, food and a European-feeling old town at North American prices
  • French is the official language; most downtown businesses serve customers in both French and English
  • Summer is festival season; winter brings Montréal en Lumière and the underground city

Downtown Montréal runs from the slopes of Mount Royal down to the St. Lawrence River, where Old Montréal's stone streets and the Old Port hold three centuries of history. In between sit the department stores of Sainte-Catherine Street, the university campuses and the Quartier des spectacles, a district designed around festivals. The office towers of René-Lévesque Boulevard and McGill College Avenue form the business core, framed by the mountain to the north and the river to the south.

The city's European feel comes from its French language, its café culture and its stone architecture, but its rhythm is North American: grid streets, a métro that runs on rubber tires and a large indoor pedestrian network that keeps downtown moving through winter. Montréal is at its best when the city is outside — terraces, free festival stages, cyclists on the canal — and its winters are long, but it handles them with more style than most.

Landmarks worth your time

  • Notre-Dame Basilica. A Gothic Revival church famous for its deep-blue, gold-starred interior.
  • Mount Royal. The park on the city's mountain, with the Kondiaronk lookout over the downtown skyline.
  • Montréal Museum of Fine Arts. One of Canada's largest art museums, spread across several pavilions on Sherbrooke Street.
  • Place Jacques-Cartier. Old Montréal's sloping square of terraces and street performers leading down to the port.
  • Bonsecours Market. A domed 19th-century market hall now used for Quebec craft and design shops.
  • Lachine Canal. A cycling and walking corridor that starts at the Old Port and runs west through former industrial districts.
  • Pointe-à-Callière. The city's archaeology and history museum, built over the site where Montréal was founded, with ruins and an old stone sewer to explore underground.
  • Mary, Queen of the World Cathedral. A scaled-down version of St. Peter's Basilica in Rome, standing among the office towers on René-Lévesque Boulevard.

A perfect day downtown

Morning: grab a bagel and walk up Mount Royal from Peel Street to the Kondiaronk lookout for the view over the towers to the river. Come down through the Golden Square Mile and spend an hour or two in the Montréal Museum of Fine Arts on Sherbrooke Street.

Afternoon: take the métro or walk down to Old Montréal. See the interior of Notre-Dame Basilica, explore the founding-era ruins at Pointe-à-Callière, then drift through Place Jacques-Cartier to the Old Port. In fine weather, rent a BIXI and ride the Lachine Canal west as far as the Atwater Market for a snack.

Evening: book dinner in Old Montréal, or bring a bottle to a bring-your-own-wine restaurant on the Plateau. Finish in the Quartier des spectacles — a free festival stage in summer, a concert or show at Place des Arts the rest of the year — or on the bars of Saint-Laurent Boulevard.

Eat and drink

Montréal punches above its weight on food: bagels baked in wood ovens, smoked meat, poutine, and a fine-dining scene built on Quebec produce. Downtown food halls make for quick lunches, while Old Montréal and nearby Plateau and Mile End reward dinner reservations. Bring your own wine restaurants are a local custom worth trying.

The public markets are the best way to taste Quebec: Atwater Market, by the Lachine Canal near downtown, and Jean-Talon Market in Little Italy, a short métro ride north, sell cheese, cider, maple products and seasonal produce. To eat cheaply, use the food courts along the RÉSO on weekdays, the bakeries and noodle shops of Chinatown, and a late-night poutine after the bars. Beer and wine are sold in corner stores (dépanneurs) and supermarkets as well as in provincial liquor stores, and in early spring the sugar shacks outside the city serve maple-heavy feasts.

Getting around

The STM métro's Green and Orange lines cross downtown, and the REM light rail connects downtown with the South Shore and western suburbs as segments open. The Yellow Line runs under the river to Parc Jean-Drapeau and Longueuil, and the Blue Line serves the north of the city. The 747 express bus runs 24/7 between downtown and the airport. Exo commuter trains leave from downtown stations for the suburbs, and VIA Rail trains to Ottawa, Toronto and Quebec City use Central Station.

BIXI bike share covers the central neighbourhoods, a growing network of protected bike lanes crosses the city, and the RÉSO underground network links métro stations, malls and office towers. Driving is the least pleasant option: right turns on red lights are banned on the island of Montréal, parking signs can be layered and confusing, summer roadworks close lanes everywhere, and cars left in the way of winter snow-clearing are towed. Walking and transit are almost always quicker downtown.

Where to stay

Old Montréal is the romantic choice — stone buildings, cobbled lanes and the river at the end of the street — though it is quieter at night in winter. The Centre-ville core around Peel and Sainte-Catherine is the practical one, with métro stations, shopping and the RÉSO for bad weather. The Quartier des spectacles and Quartier Latin put you at the heart of the festivals and tend to be better value. The Golden Square Mile suits museum-goers, and the Plateau, just north of downtown on the Orange Line, is the pick if you'd rather stay among cafés and walk-up flats. Book well ahead for the Grand Prix weekend and the summer festival run.

Living downtown

Downtown housing ranges from high-rise condos and student apartments to lofts in Old Montréal and Griffintown. Rents remain lower than Toronto or Vancouver, though they have risen. Leases traditionally renew on July 1, so plan moves around 'moving day' to avoid competition for trucks and movers.

Residential leases in Quebec use a mandatory government form, and disputes go to the Tribunal administratif du logement (TAL). Landlords may ask for the first month's rent in advance but not a security or damage deposit. Leases renew automatically unless the tenant gives notice — landlords can end them only in limited cases — and if you refuse a proposed rent increase, the landlord must apply to the TAL to have it set. Buyers pay a land transfer duty, known locally as the welcome tax. Downtown suits students, young professionals and anyone who wants festivals and the mountain on the doorstep; families often prefer the Plateau, Rosemont or Verdun.

Doing business downtown

Aerospace, artificial-intelligence research, video games, life sciences and finance drive the economy, alongside a large festival and tourism sector. Retail concentrates on Sainte-Catherine Street. Quebec's language law requires French on commercial signage and in customer service, with French markedly predominant on exterior signs — check the current rules from the Office québécois de la langue française before branding a storefront.

The main office core runs along René-Lévesque Boulevard and McGill College Avenue, with the Quartier international around the convention centre and a creative and tech cluster in Old Montréal, the Cité du Multimédia and Griffintown. The RÉSO turns the downtown basements into a retail corridor that keeps trading through winter, and provincial tax credits have long supported the digital and video-game industries. JulyJust for Laughs — A large comedy festival with indoor shows and outdoor events.
Feb–MarchMontréal en Lumière — A winter festival of food, light installations and an all-night arts event.

Dates move from year to year. Check the organiser's official site before booking travel.

Seasons and weather

Montréal has four emphatic seasons. Winter, from December to March, is cold and snowy, with deep freezes and wind chill on the worst days; the city clears snow efficiently, and the RÉSO and métro let you avoid the street. Spring arrives in a rush of melting snow and slush in March and April. Summers are warm to hot and often humid, which is when the festivals and terraces take over. Autumn is crisp and colourful on Mount Royal from late September into October. Pack insulated, waterproof boots with good grip, a warm coat, hat and gloves for winter, and light clothes plus a rain layer for summer.

Safety and practicalities

Downtown Montréal is generally safe, including late at night in the busy districts. Keep an eye on bags in festival crowds and on busy métro platforms, and expect visible homelessness around some métro stations and in parts of the Quartier des spectacles and the Village. Sidewalks can be treacherous with ice in winter, and roadworks and orange cones reroute pedestrians all summer. Many older métro stations still lack elevators, so check STM's accessibility information before relying on them. Signs and announcements are in French, but staff in hotels, museums and most downtown shops switch to English readily.

Local tips

  • Learn a few French greetings — 'Bonjour-Hi' is the classic bilingual hello.
  • Book festival-season hotels months ahead.
  • Walk up Mount Royal from Peel Street for the city's best free view.
  • Prices exclude GST and Quebec's QST, so expect the total at the till to be noticeably higher than the sticker.
  • If you rent a car, remember there is no right turn on a red light anywhere on the island.
