City centres are some of the most rewarding places to photograph: dramatic architecture, reflections in glass, crowds in motion, signs and lights after dark. This guide covers the basics that work with any camera, including a phone — and it's a good primer if you're thinking of entering our Downtown Lens photo contest.

A downtown changes completely between dawn and midnight. The same corner can be an empty study in lines at 7 a.m., a blur of commuters at 9, a hard-shadowed canyon at noon and a wash of neon after dark. Good city photographers plan around light and people rather than ticking off landmarks, so that's where we start.

When to shoot

  • Golden hour — the hour after sunrise and before sunset — gives warm, low light that brings out textures in stone and brick.
  • Blue hour — the short period after sunset — balances the sky's colour with city lights. It's the best time for skylines.
  • Rainy days create reflections in streets and puddles, and umbrellas add colour to street scenes.
  • Early weekend mornings offer empty streets for architecture shots.
  • Midday is harsh in open squares, but light bouncing between towers can be soft and even in narrow streets, and hard shadows suit black-and-white.

Latitude matters too. In northern cities such as London, Berlin and Dublin, the low winter sun can feel like golden hour all afternoon. Near the equator, in Singapore, golden hour is brief and the sun rises and sets at much the same time all year. Check sunrise and sunset times, and which way the sun will fall on your subject, before you go.

Where to stand

  • Across water. Rivers and harbours give the cleanest skyline views.
  • Bridges offer elevated, central viewpoints.
  • Street level, looking up emphasises height.
  • Elevated walkways and rooftops give unusual angles.

Composition

  • Leading lines: streets, rails and bridges draw the eye.
  • Frames: arches, doorways and tree branches frame a subject.
  • Reflections: glass towers and puddles double a scene.
  • Scale: a person in front of a building shows its size.
  • Patterns: windows, tiles and repeated balconies make strong abstract images.

Architecture without distortion

Tilt a camera up at a tower and its sides converge as if it's falling backwards. To keep verticals straight, hold the camera level, step back or find a higher vantage point such as a bridge or a raised plaza, and crop afterwards. A longer lens — the 2× or 3× camera on many phones — compresses layers of buildings into a dense stack, which often says more about a downtown than a wide shot. Most editing apps can correct remaining perspective in a couple of taps.

Settings that work

On a phone

  • Turn on the grid to keep horizons and verticals straight.
  • Tap and hold to lock focus and exposure, then slide exposure down for bright skies and neon.
  • Use the phone's real lenses rather than pinch-zooming beyond them.
  • Wipe the lens. Fingerprints cause the smeary glow around lights at night.
  • Shoot in RAW if your phone offers it, and use burst mode for moving subjects.

What to carry

  • A spare battery or power bank — cold weather and night modes drain both.
  • A microfibre cloth for lens and screen.
  • A small tabletop tripod or a bean bag for walls and railings.
  • A zip bag or rain cover.
  • A wrist strap for crowded streets and bridges.
  • An offline map with your viewpoints pinned.

Photographing people

Street photography is about life in the city, but people deserve respect. Laws on photographing people in public vary by country, and some places require consent for certain uses. Be especially careful with children and in sensitive locations. If someone objects, delete the image. For contest entries, you'll need permission from anyone who is the main subject of a photo.

How the rules differ

As general orientation, not legal advice: in the US, the UK, Australia and most of Canada, taking photos of people in public places is generally lawful, though using a recognisable person's image to advertise or sell something usually needs a signed release. Québec and France give people a stronger right to their own image, and publishing a recognisable photo of someone can require consent. Germany restricts publishing images of identifiable people without consent, with exceptions such as crowds at public gatherings. Japanese courts recognise portrait rights, and many people there expect not to be photographed by strangers. In the United Arab Emirates, photographing or publishing images of people without consent can be a criminal offence, so in Dubai always ask first.

The simplest etiquette works everywhere: make eye contact, smile, show the picture and offer to send it. Avoid photographing security checkpoints, government and military sites, and anyone in a vulnerable moment.

Night photography

At night, steady your camera on a wall or railing, use your phone's night mode, and look for light sources — signs, shop windows, traffic trails. Long exposures turn traffic into streaks of light.

Use a two- or three-second self-timer so pressing the shutter doesn't shake the frame, and turn the flash off: it lights the nearest metre and flattens everything else. Wet pavements after rain double every light source, and illuminated shop windows and neon make strong subjects for the Storefronts & Signs category.

Respecting places

Follow rules in museums, places of worship and private buildings. Don't block sidewalks or doorways. Some buildings and transit systems restrict tripods; check before you set up.

Privately owned plazas, atriums and malls can set their own rules, and security staff may ask you to stop. Commercial shoots with crews, models, lighting or equipment on the sidewalk often need a permit from the city's film office; casual photography doesn't. Drones are the exception to almost everything: most city centres sit inside controlled airspace, and civil aviation authorities require registration and often specific permission. If you plan to sell your photos, note that some countries, France among them, limit commercial use of images of recent copyrighted buildings and public art.

Taking it further: photo walks and exhibitions

Downtowns are ideal for group photo walks. Keep the group small enough to move along a sidewalk without blocking it, meet at a transit stop, plan a loop that ends at a viewpoint for blue hour, and agree in advance on how you'll handle people who don't want to be photographed. Share the route and timing with everyone beforehand.

When you have a body of work, show it. Public libraries, community centres and university galleries often host local photography shows. For a launch, a group exhibition or a workshop of your own, a small event space gives you walls, lighting and a fixed date to work towards.

Preparing a Downtown Lens entry

Downtown Lens has five categories: Skyline After Dark, Street Life, Storefronts & Signs, Green Downtown and On the Move. Before you submit:

  • Choose the category first, then your single strongest frame for it.
  • Check that the photo is your own, taken within the last two years, and not AI-generated or composited. Standard editing is fine.
  • Confirm you have permission from any identifiable person who is the main subject.
  • Export as a JPEG at least 2,000 pixels on the long edge.
  • Write a short caption naming the city and the place.
  • Remember the limit: up to five entries per person across all categories.
