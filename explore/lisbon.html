Lisbon's downtown is the Baixa, the flat grid of streets rebuilt after the earthquake, tsunami and fires of 1755, running from the riverside Praça do Comércio north to the squares of Rossio and Praça da Figueira. Chiado and Bairro Alto climb the hill to the west; Alfama and the castle rise to the east. From Restauradores, the tree-lined Avenida da Liberdade runs north to the Marquês de Pombal roundabout, where the modern business district of the Avenidas Novas begins.

The centre works because so much is packed into it: a walkable grid, layered hillside neighbourhoods, free viewpoints, a mild climate and food that is good at every price. It is also still better value than most western European capitals. The catches are physical and social. The hills are relentless, the iconic trams are crammed in summer, and short-term rentals have emptied many historic buildings of long-term residents. Pace yourself, use the Metro to gain height, and walk downhill whenever you can.

The districts

Baixa PombalinaThe planned post-earthquake grid between the river and Rossio, with pedestrianised Rua Augusta at its centre. Busy with shoppers by day, much quieter after dark.
ChiadoThe elegant hillside quarter of bookshops, theatres, old cafés and the national opera house, linked to the Baixa by steep streets, stairways and a Metro station with long escalators.
Bairro Alto and Príncipe RealBairro Alto's narrow lanes are quiet by day and packed with street-side drinkers at night. Príncipe Real, just north, is calmer, with a garden square, concept stores and grand townhouses.
Alfama and the castle hillThe oldest neighbourhood: a tangle of alleys, stairways and laundry lines below the castle, with the cathedral, viewpoints and fado houses.
Avenida da LiberdadeA broad nineteenth-century boulevard of shaded promenades, mosaic pavements, luxury flagships and offices, running up to Marquês de Pombal and Parque Eduardo VII.
Cais do SodréThe riverside district west of the Baixa: the station for trains to Cascais, ferries across the Tagus, the Ribeira market hall and a nightlife strip on Rua Nova do Carvalho.

Landmarks worth your time

  • Praça do Comércio and the Arco da Rua Augusta. The vast riverside square stands where the royal palace stood before 1755, which is why locals still call it Terreiro do Paço. Climb the triumphal arch for a view down the Baixa grid.
  • Castelo de São Jorge. Restored walls and towers on the hill above Alfama, with the best panorama of the river and the red roofs below. Go early or late to beat the queues.
  • Lisbon Cathedral (Sé). A fortress-like Romanesque cathedral begun in the twelfth century, a short walk downhill from the castle on the tram 28 route.
  • Carmo Convent. The roofless Gothic nave above Rossio was left in ruins as a memorial to the 1755 earthquake and now holds a small archaeological museum.
  • Santa Justa Lift. A neo-Gothic iron lift of 1902 linking the Baixa to Largo do Carmo. Queues at the bottom can be long; you can also walk up to Carmo and see it from the top.
  • The miradouros. Free viewpoints on every hill: São Pedro de Alcântara in Bairro Alto, Santa Luzia and Portas do Sol in Alfama, and Graça and Senhora do Monte for the widest views.
  • National Tile Museum. The Museu Nacional do Azulejo, in a former convent east of the centre, traces five centuries of Portuguese tiles, including a long panel showing Lisbon before the earthquake.
  • Belém. Tram 15 or a train from Cais do Sodré reaches the riverside district of the Jerónimos Monastery and the Belém Tower, both UNESCO World Heritage monuments.

A perfect day downtown

Morning. Start the way Lisboetas do, with a quick coffee and a pastel de nata standing at a pastry-shop counter. Walk the length of Rua Augusta to the river at Praça do Comércio, then climb through Alfama, or ride tram 28 if you are early enough to get on, to the Sé and up to the castle before the tour groups arrive.

Afternoon. Wander back down through Alfama's lanes via the Santa Luzia viewpoint and stop for a set lunch of the day's dish. Cross the Baixa to Rossio, then head up to Chiado and the Carmo ruins. Spend the late afternoon browsing Chiado's bookshops before walking up to the São Pedro de Alcântara viewpoint, which looks across to the castle as the light turns gold.

Evening. Lisbon dines late, so book for around half past eight or nine. Afterwards, join the crowds drinking in Bairro Alto's lanes or along the river at Cais do Sodré, or book a fado performance in Alfama or Mouraria, where silence is expected while the singers perform.

Eat and drink

Lisbon eats from the Atlantic: grilled fish, sardines in summer, octopus, clams with garlic and coriander, and bacalhau, the salt cod Portugal prepares in countless ways. The bifana, a garlicky pork sandwich, is the city's fast food, and pastry shops are everywhere, with the custard tart, pastel de nata, as the obvious start. Order a bica for an espresso. Near Rossio, tiny counters pour ginjinha, a sour-cherry liqueur. Wine is excellent value, from crisp vinho verde to robust Alentejo and Douro reds, plus port.

For markets, the Mercado da Ribeira at Cais do Sodré has a traditional produce market in the morning and a busy food hall alongside, and the neighbourhood market in Campo de Ourique has food counters popular with locals. The best-value meal is lunch: small restaurants and snack bars post a prato do dia, a daily dish that often comes with a drink and coffee. Petiscos, Portuguese small plates, are good for sharing. Note that bread, olives and butter brought to the table (the couvert) are charged only if you eat them, so send them back if you don't want them.

Getting around

The Metropolitano de Lisboa runs four colour-coded Metro lines; Baixa-Chiado (Blue and Green lines), Rossio, Restauradores and Terreiro do Paço are the downtown stations. Carris, the city transport company, runs the buses, the historic yellow trams, the Santa Justa Lift and the funiculars. Tram 28 rattles from Martim Moniz through Graça, Alfama, the Baixa and Chiado, and tram 15 runs along the river to Belém. The Glória funicular closed after a fatal derailment in September 2025, so check which lifts and funiculars are running before you plan around them. Suburban trains leave Cais do Sodré for the Cascais coast and Rossio for Sintra, and ferries cross the Tagus from Cais do Sodré and Terreiro do Paço.

The airport is unusually close to the city and is the terminus of the Metro Red line; change at Alameda or São Sebastião for the centre, or take a taxi. Walking is the best way to see downtown, but plan routes along the contours where possible and use Metro escalators and lifts to gain height. The municipal Gira bike share includes e-bikes, which help on the hills, and a riverside path runs west from Cais do Sodré toward Belém. Driving in the centre is a poor idea: streets are narrow and steep, parking is scarce, and older vehicles face restrictions in the Baixa. Rent a car only for trips out of town.

Where to stay

The Baixa and Chiado are the easiest bases for a first visit: flat or nearly so, central and right on the Metro. Alfama and the castle hill have the most atmosphere, but expect stairs, cobbles and fado drifting through the windows at night. Bairro Alto suits night owls only; its lanes stay loud until the early hours at weekends. Príncipe Real is quieter and more residential, with boutique stays. Avenida da Liberdade and Marquês de Pombal suit business travellers, with bigger rooms, wide pavements and quick Metro access to the airport. Cais do Sodré is handy for beach trains and nightlife. If you rent an apartment, check that the listing shows its local accommodation (alojamento local) registration number.

Living downtown

The Baixa's Pombaline buildings were among the first in Europe designed with earthquakes in mind, built around a flexible timber cage. Beyond them, residents find nineteenth-century tiled apartment buildings in Graça, Arroios and Estrela, early twentieth-century blocks in the Avenidas Novas, and modern flats in Parque das Nações. Central Lisbon has become far more expensive than local salaries suggest, partly because so many historic flats were converted to short-term rentals. Many residents choose Campo de Ourique, Alvalade or Arroios for daily life and come downtown for work and evenings out.

Leases must be in writing and registered with the tax authority, and you will need a Portuguese tax number (NIF) to sign one. Landlords commonly ask for a deposit plus rent in advance, and often a guarantor (fiador); newcomers without local credit history may be asked for more months upfront instead. Annual rent increases usually follow a government coefficient tied to inflation unless the lease says otherwise. Older homes rarely have central heating and can be cold and damp in winter, so ask about insulation and heating at viewings. The centre suits remote workers, professionals and retirees who don't mind hills; families with buggies may prefer flatter districts.

Doing business downtown

Lisbon's office cores sit just north of the historic centre. The Avenidas Novas, around Saldanha, Picoas and Avenida da República, hold the larger office buildings used by banks, insurers and consultancies, while Avenida da Liberdade and Marquês de Pombal are the prestige addresses favoured by law firms and private banks. Parque das Nações, the former Expo 98 site near the airport, has modern offices popular with technology and shared-service operations. Tourism, technology, nearshore service centres, finance and government are the big employers, and the arrival of international tech firms and conferences has made the city a regular base for European teams.

Street retail splits between the tourist footfall of the Baixa, the fashion and bookshops of Chiado and the luxury flagships of Avenida da Liberdade. Long-established shops can be recognised under the city's Lojas com História (Shops with History) programme, which gives some of them extra protection. Portugal's Empresa na Hora service lets you register a company quickly, and AICEP, the trade and investment agency, supports foreign investors. Signature events

Late AprilFreedom Day (25 de Abril) — Parades and red carnations on Avenida da Liberdade marking the 1974 revolution that ended the dictatorship.
Late May–JuneLisbon Book Fair — Publishers' and booksellers' stalls fill the slopes of Parque Eduardo VII for several weeks, with readings and late openings.
JuneFestas de Lisboa — A month of neighbourhood street parties with grilled sardines, the marchas populares parade on Avenida da Liberdade and the biggest night on the eve of St Anthony's Day.
NovemberWeb Summit — A huge technology conference at Parque das Nações that fills hotels across the city.

Dates move from year to year. Check the organiser's official site before booking travel.

Seasons and weather

Lisbon has a sunny Mediterranean climate shaped by the Atlantic. Spring is the sweet spot, warm and bright, with purple jacaranda blossom across the city in late May and June. Summer is dry and hot, often into the thirties Celsius, and heatwaves can push higher; plan walking for mornings and evenings. Autumn stays warm well into October. Winter is mild, rarely near freezing, but brings most of the year's rain and some stormy days, and many older buildings are poorly heated. Pack sunscreen and a hat in summer, a light jacket for breezy evenings year-round, an umbrella in winter, and in every season shoes with good grip for the polished stone pavements.

Safety and practicalities

Lisbon is one of Europe's safer capitals, but pickpockets target tram 28, the queues at the Santa Justa Lift, Rossio and the busiest Baixa streets, so keep bags zipped and phones out of back pockets. In the Baixa and Bairro Alto you may be offered drugs on the street; ignore it and walk on. The Metro closes around 1am, after which night buses, taxis and ride-hailing take over. Hills, stairways and cobbles make the centre hard going for wheelchair users and parents with buggies, although many Metro stations have lifts. In summer, carry water and take the heat seriously. Tap water is safe to drink, and EU roaming rules apply to EU phone plans.

Local tips

  • Use the long escalators inside Baixa-Chiado Metro station to climb from the Baixa to Chiado.
  • Board tram 28 at Martim Moniz early in the morning, or take tram 12, which loops around the castle hill with fewer crowds.
  • Send back the couvert if you don't want it; you pay only for what you eat.
  • Check Monday closures for museums and monuments before you plan the day.
  • Catch sunset from a miradouro rather than a paid rooftop.
  • Eat your main meal at lunch, when the prato do dia is the best deal in town.
