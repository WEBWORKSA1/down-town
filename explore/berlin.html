In 30 seconds

  • Best for: 20th-century history, museums and creative culture
  • Register your address within two weeks of moving in
  • Berlin is relatively affordable for a capital, though rents have risen fast

Mitte, meaning 'middle', is Berlin's historic centre and the borough where the city's division and reunification are most visible. The Brandenburg Gate, the Reichstag and the line of the Berlin Wall all sit here, alongside the museums of Museum Island and the grand boulevard of Unter den Linden.

Mitte is also a living district of courtyards, cafés and galleries, especially around Hackescher Markt. It is the place to understand Berlin's past and its creative present. What makes it work is scale and transit: the big sights are a walk or a tram ride apart, and the city never feels as crowded as Paris or London. What can frustrate is the patchwork — grand Prussian avenues give way to wide GDR-era plazas and brand-new glass blocks, and the action is spread across several centres rather than one.

The districts

Museum IslandA UNESCO-listed island of major museums in the Spree, with the Berlin Cathedral and the rebuilt palace that houses the Humboldt Forum at its southern end.
Hackescher MarktCourtyards, galleries and cafés in the old Spandauer Vorstadt, with independent fashion on the surrounding streets and nightlife along Torstraße.
Unter den Linden and FriedrichstraßeThe ceremonial boulevard from the Brandenburg Gate to Museum Island, crossed by Berlin's classic shopping and office street.
Potsdamer PlatzA rebuilt commercial district with film venues, corporate towers and the Berlinale's main stage, on what was wasteland beside the Wall.
AlexanderplatzA large square under the TV tower, the transport hub of the East, ringed by department stores and post-war blocks.
Government quarterThe Reichstag, the Chancellery and parliamentary offices on a bend of the Spree, next to the main railway station and the Tiergarten.

Landmarks worth your time

  • Brandenburg Gate. The 18th-century gate that stood in no man's land during the Cold War and became the symbol of reunification in 1989.
  • Reichstag dome. A walk-up glass dome above the German parliament with a spiral ramp and city views — free, but register online in advance.
  • Museum Island. Five major museums on an island in the Spree, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, including the Pergamon complex and the Neues Museum. The Pergamonmuseum's main building is closed for a long renovation, so check what is open before you plan around it.
  • Berlin Wall Memorial. The most complete preserved stretch of the Wall and its 'death strip', with an open-air exhibition on Bernauer Straße and a viewing platform over the border installations.
  • Memorial to the Murdered Jews of Europe. A field of concrete slabs just south of the Brandenburg Gate, with an underground information centre telling individual stories.
  • TV Tower. The East German-era tower above Alexanderplatz, with a viewing sphere and rotating restaurant.
  • Gendarmenmarkt. Often called Berlin's most beautiful square, framed by twin churches and the concert hall.
  • Tiergarten. The city's great central park, running west from the Brandenburg Gate, with lakes, beer gardens and long, shady paths.

A perfect day downtown

Morning: start at the Reichstag dome with a pre-booked slot, then walk through the Brandenburg Gate and along Unter den Linden. Detour to the Holocaust memorial on the way, and pause at Bebelplatz, scene of the 1933 book burning, where a sunken glass panel looks down into a room of empty shelves.

Afternoon: cross to Museum Island and choose one or two museums rather than five — the Neues Museum and the Alte Nationalgalerie are good picks. Then wander north through the courtyards around Hackescher Markt and on to the Berlin Wall Memorial on Bernauer Straße, which takes an hour to do properly.

Evening: ride up the TV Tower for the lights, or skip it and sit by the Spree. Eat around Rosenthaler Platz or Torstraße, where dinner starts late by German standards and bars run well past midnight.

Eat and drink

Berlin's food is cheap, casual and international. Döner kebab and currywurst are the street-food classics, but Mitte also has modern German bistros, Vietnamese canteens and natural-wine bars around Torstraße. For markets, a restored 19th-century market hall in neighbouring Kreuzberg hosts a weekly street-food evening, and Turkish markets along the canal are a short ride away.

Look for seasonal German menus: white asparagus in spring, chanterelles in late summer, goose and red cabbage before Christmas. Bakeries open early and sell filled rolls that make a cheap breakfast, and lunch menus at sit-down restaurants are often far better value than dinner. Late-night corner shops, called Spätis, sell drinks and snacks long after supermarkets close, and drinking a beer outside one is a Berlin pastime. Keep some cash for smaller places and market stalls.

Getting around

The U-Bahn and S-Bahn run frequently, and trams cover much of the former East, including Mitte. The east–west S-Bahn line links Alexanderplatz, Hackescher Markt, Friedrichstraße and the main station, while the U5 runs under Unter den Linden to Museum Island and the Brandenburg Gate. Most U-Bahn lines and many S-Bahn lines run all night at weekends, with night buses on weeknights. Berlin Brandenburg Airport is reached by the FEX airport express, regional trains or the S-Bahn; it lies in fare zone C, so buy an ABC ticket.

The Deutschlandticket, a monthly subscription, covers local and regional public transport across Germany, which makes weekend trips cheap. Cycling is easy on flat streets with a growing network of bike lanes, but watch for tram tracks and cobblestones. Driving downtown is rarely worth it: the centre inside the S-Bahn ring is a low-emission zone requiring a green sticker, and most streets have paid parking.

Where to stay

First-timers and museum lovers do best between Museum Island and Hackescher Markt, where you can walk to most sights and the S-Bahn is on your doorstep. Business travellers often pick Potsdamer Platz or the streets around Friedrichstraße for the office towers and quick rail links. Alexanderplatz is the most connected base and has plenty of large hotels, though it feels more functional than charming. For nightlife and a more local feel, look just north of Mitte towards Prenzlauer Berg, or east to Friedrichshain, both a few stops away.

Living downtown

Most Berliners rent, often in high-ceilinged pre-war Altbau buildings or post-war blocks. Rent-brake rules limit increases on new leases in tight markets, but demand far exceeds supply, so expect competition and requests for proof of income, a credit report and a rental history. Rents are usually quoted 'cold' (Kaltmiete), before heating and service charges; the 'warm' figure includes them. Deposits are capped by law at three months' cold rent, and many newcomers start in a shared flat while they search.

By law you must register your address (Anmeldung) within two weeks of moving in, and you'll need the landlord's confirmation to do it. Appointments at the citizens' offices can be scarce, so book as soon as you have a lease. Mitte suits people who want culture and transit on the doorstep and don't mind tourists; families and those wanting more space for the money often look to Prenzlauer Berg, Pankow or further out on the S-Bahn.

Doing business downtown

Berlin is Germany's startup capital and the seat of federal government, with media, creative industries and tourism close behind. The office cores are Friedrichstraße and the streets around Gendarmenmarkt, Potsdamer Platz, the new quarter north of the main station and Alexanderplatz, with tech and media firms also clustered along the Spree in Friedrichshain and Kreuzberg. Associations, lobbyists and embassies follow the government to Mitte.

Mitte suits galleries, design shops and cafés that depend on visitors and office workers. Founders should budget time for German bureaucracy: trade registration, tax numbers and bank accounts all take paperwork, and a commercial lease will need careful review. Signature events

FebruaryBerlinale — A major international film festival, centred on Potsdamer Platz, with many public screenings.
SeptemberBerlin Marathon — A fast marathon course through the city, finishing through the Brandenburg Gate.
OctoberFestival of Lights — Projection art on landmarks, best seen on an evening walk through Mitte.

Dates move from year to year. Check the organiser's official site before booking travel.

Seasons and weather

Winters are long, grey and cold, with short days and temperatures around freezing; Christmas markets brighten December, and museums are quieter. Spring arrives in April and May with blossom and the first outdoor terraces. Summer is the best season: long evenings, open-air cinemas and lakes a train ride away, with occasional heatwaves and thunderstorms. Autumn is crisp and often sunny into October. Pack layers and a waterproof jacket at any time of year, and shoes that can handle cobblestones.

Safety and practicalities

Berlin is generally safe, including at night, but pickpockets work the crowds at Alexanderplatz, around the Brandenburg Gate, on busy U-Bahn lines and at the big stations. Ignore clipboard petitioners at tourist sights. Walking in bike lanes is the most common visitor mistake — they are often marked on the pavement in red. Many stations have lifts, but not all, and the BVG app shows which are out of service. Smaller cafés may still be cash-only or set a card minimum, so carry some euros. EU roaming covers phones from elsewhere in the EU, and public Wi-Fi is patchy, so a local data plan helps.

Local tips

  • Register in advance for the Reichstag dome; same-day slots are rare in summer.
  • Buy a 24-hour or 7-day ticket if you'll take more than two rides a day — and validate paper tickets.
  • Most shops close on Sundays; plan groceries for Saturday.
  • Return glass and plastic bottles marked for the deposit (Pfand) at supermarket machines for a refund.
  • Follow the double row of cobblestones set into the streets to trace the route of the Wall.
