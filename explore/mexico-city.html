Mexico City's Centro Histórico sits on top of the Aztec capital Tenochtitlan. The Zócalo, one of the largest public squares in the world, is surrounded by the Metropolitan Cathedral, the National Palace and the excavated ruins of the Templo Mayor. West of the square, pedestrian streets lead to the Alameda Central and the start of Paseo de la Reforma, the boulevard of monuments and glass towers that carries the city's business life.

The centre has been revived over the last two decades with pedestrian streets, restored buildings and new cultural venues. A short ride west, Roma and Condesa offer leafy streets and a large international community. The Centro's pleasures are its density of history, art and street food; its frustrations are crowds, noise and the slow sinking of a city built on a drained lakebed, which leaves many old buildings visibly tilting.

Landmarks worth your time

  • Zócalo. Officially Plaza de la Constitución, one of the largest public squares in the world and the stage for national celebrations.
  • Metropolitan Cathedral. A vast cathedral built over three centuries, blending Gothic, Baroque and Neoclassical styles.
  • Templo Mayor. The excavated ruins and museum of the Aztec main temple, discovered beneath the city streets.
  • Palacio de Bellas Artes. A marble arts palace with murals by Mexico's great muralists and performances of folkloric ballet.
  • National Palace. The seat of government, with Diego Rivera's murals of Mexican history.
  • Torre Latinoamericana. An observation deck on a mid-century skyscraper with views across the centre.
  • Palacio Postal. An ornate early-20th-century post office opposite Bellas Artes, all gilded ironwork and marble stairs, still in use today.
  • Antiguo Colegio de San Ildefonso. A former Jesuit college near the Zócalo where José Clemente Orozco and other muralists painted some of the movement's earliest works.

A perfect day downtown

Grab a tamal and a cup of atole from a street cart, then be at the Templo Mayor when it opens, before the tour groups arrive. Cross the Zócalo to the Metropolitan Cathedral, and bring photo ID if you hope to see Diego Rivera's murals in the National Palace, where access is sometimes restricted.

Walk west along the pedestrianised Calle Madero, ride up the Torre Latinoamericana for the view, and look into the Palacio Postal before reaching Bellas Artes and the Alameda. Eat the main meal of the day in the mid-afternoon, as locals do, with a comida corrida set menu at a neighbourhood fonda or a market counter.

Watch the light fade over the Zócalo from a rooftop terrace, then choose between an old cantina in the Centro, the mariachis of Plaza Garibaldi (go with a group and keep valuables out of sight), or a taxi to Roma or Condesa for dinner and bars.

Eat and drink

This is one of the world's great food cities. In the Centro you can eat tacos al pastor at a street stand, tamales from a morning cart, and traditional dishes in century-old cantinas and cafés. Mercado de San Juan is famous for specialty ingredients, while Roma and Condesa hold many of the city's most celebrated restaurants.

Eat on the street with confidence by choosing busy stalls with fast turnover. Tacos de canasta, sold from baskets on bicycles in the morning, tortas, quesadillas and churros with hot chocolate are all Centro staples. Markets have fondas, small counters serving home-style lunches, and the small Chinatown on Calle Dolores adds variety. Mexico City eats late: lunch, the main meal, runs through the mid-afternoon, and dinner is often lighter and later. For drinks, try mezcal, or pulque, the fermented agave drink served in traditional pulquerías.

Getting around

The Metro is extensive and inexpensive, Metrobús bus rapid transit runs along major avenues, and Ecobici bike share works well in the central districts. On Sunday mornings, Paseo de la Reforma closes to cars for cyclists and walkers. For late nights, use ride-hailing or authorised taxis.

The Metro stations at Zócalo/Tenochtitlan, Allende, Bellas Artes and San Juan de Letrán put you in the heart of the Centro, and the front carriages are reserved for women and children. Metrobús Line 4 runs through the historic centre to the airport terminals, and Line 7 runs along Reforma. At the airport, buy a ticket from an official authorised-taxi booth inside the terminal rather than accepting offers in the arrivals hall. Driving is slow, traffic is heavy at peak hours, and the Hoy No Circula scheme keeps some cars off the road on certain days according to their plate number. The Centro is flat and walkable, but pavements are uneven and kerbs can be high.

Where to stay

The Centro Histórico puts you among the sights, often in hotels converted from colonial mansions; choose a street near Calle Madero, the Zócalo or the Alameda, which stay lively into the evening. The Reforma corridor and Juárez suit business travellers, with easy access to the corporate towers and the Metrobús. Roma and Condesa are the favourites for longer stays, with tree-lined streets, parks, cafés and nightlife. Polanco, beside Chapultepec Park, is the upscale choice for luxury shopping and business meetings.

Living downtown

The Centro offers restored apartments in historic buildings, while Roma and Condesa are popular with international residents and remote workers. The city sits at roughly 2,240 metres, so expect thinner air at first. Ask about a building's structural safety and earthquake history before signing a lease.

Residential leases usually run for 12 months and are paid in pesos. Landlords commonly ask for a deposit of one month's rent and a fiador, a guarantor who owns property in the city; newcomers without one often use a póliza jurídica, a legal-protection policy issued after the tenant has been vetted. Furnished apartments for remote workers are easy to find in Roma, Condesa and Juárez, usually at a premium. Building codes were strengthened after the 1985 and 2017 earthquakes, so newer or properly retrofitted buildings are worth seeking out. The Centro suits culture lovers who don't mind noise; most expatriate professionals settle in Roma, Condesa, Polanco or Coyoacán.

Doing business downtown

The Paseo de la Reforma corridor hosts corporate towers and international firms, while the Centro is known for streets where shops cluster by trade — one street for lighting, another for books, another for wedding dresses. Tourism and hospitality businesses benefit from steady visitor flows year-round.

Corporate Mexico City has several cores. Reforma's towers house banks, insurers and international firms; Polanco mixes corporate offices with luxury retail; Santa Fe, in the west, is a car-oriented district of headquarters; and Insurgentes Sur offers mid-rise offices along the Metrobús. The Centro itself is home to federal and city government, small professional firms and specialist wholesale trade. Businesses register with the tax authority, the SAT, for a tax identification number (RFC), and premises need a land-use certificate and an operating notice filed with the city. Seasons and weather

Mexico City's altitude keeps it mild year-round. The dry season, roughly November to April, brings sunny days, cool mornings and chilly nights, especially in December and January. April and May are the warmest months, before the rains, and also when air quality tends to be at its worst; on bad days the city issues environmental alerts and extends driving restrictions. The rainy season, roughly June to September, usually means bright mornings and heavy afternoon or evening downpours that can flood streets for a while. Pack layers for the daily temperature swings, sunscreen for the strong high-altitude sun, and an umbrella in summer.

Safety and practicalities

The Centro is busy and visibly policed by day, and most visits pass without trouble. Pickpockets work crowded Metro carriages, markets and big events, so keep phones out of back pockets and bags closed. The blocks north and east of the Zócalo empty out once shops close; stick to busier streets after dark and book ride-hailing or authorised taxis at night rather than hailing on the street. If you hear the city's earthquake alarm, follow locals calmly to open space. Altitude can bring headaches and breathlessness on the first day, so drink water and go easy on alcohol. Uneven pavements and Metro stations without lifts make the city challenging with a wheelchair or pram. Mobile coverage is good, and a local SIM or eSIM is easy to buy.

Local tips

  • Visit museums early, and note that many close on Mondays.
  • Drink bottled or filtered water.
  • Join the Sunday car-free ride on Paseo de la Reforma.
  • Carry small notes and coins for street food, markets and tips.
  • Learn a few Spanish phrases; English is less widely spoken in the Centro than in Roma or Polanco.
  • Pack a light jacket even in the dry season — evenings cool off quickly at this altitude.
