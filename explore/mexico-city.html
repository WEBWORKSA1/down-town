Downtown Mexico City

A UNESCO-listed historic centre built on an Aztec capital, with grand plazas, murals and car-free streets.

Mexico City's Centro Histórico sits on top of the Aztec capital Tenochtitlan. The Zócalo, one of the largest public squares in the world, is surrounded by the Metropolitan Cathedral, the National Palace and the excavated ruins of the Templo Mayor.

The centre has been revived over the last two decades with pedestrian streets, restored buildings and new cultural venues. A short ride west, Roma and Condesa offer leafy streets and a large international community.

Landmarks worth your time

  • Zócalo. Officially Plaza de la Constitución, one of the largest public squares in the world and the stage for national celebrations.
  • Metropolitan Cathedral. A vast cathedral built over three centuries, blending Gothic, Baroque and Neoclassical styles.
  • Templo Mayor. The excavated ruins and museum of the Aztec main temple, discovered beneath the city streets.
  • Palacio de Bellas Artes. A marble arts palace with murals by Mexico's great muralists and performances of folkloric ballet.
  • National Palace. The seat of government, with Diego Rivera's murals of Mexican history.
  • Torre Latinoamericana. An observation deck on a mid-century skyscraper with views across the centre.

Eat and drink

This is one of the world's great food cities. In the Centro you can eat tacos al pastor at a street stand, tamales from a morning cart, and traditional dishes in century-old cantinas and cafés. Mercado de San Juan is famous for specialty ingredients, while Roma and Condesa hold many of the city's most celebrated restaurants.

Getting around

The Metro is extensive and inexpensive, Metrobús bus rapid transit runs along major avenues, and Ecobici bike share works well in the central districts. On Sunday mornings, Paseo de la Reforma closes to cars for cyclists and walkers. For late nights, use ride-hailing or authorised taxis.

Living downtown

The Centro offers restored apartments in historic buildings, while Roma and Condesa are popular with international residents and remote workers. The city sits at roughly 2,240 metres, so expect thinner air at first. Ask about a building's structural safety and earthquake history before signing a lease.

Doing business downtown

The Paseo de la Reforma corridor hosts corporate towers and international firms, while the Centro is known for streets where shops cluster by trade — one street for lighting, another for books, another for wedding dresses. Tourism and hospitality businesses benefit from steady visitor flows year-round.

  • Drink bottled or filtered water.

Questions people ask

What is the best area to stay in downtown Mexico City?

It depends on your plans. Zócalo and the Cathedral district is the most central choice: the vast main square, ringed by the cathedral, the National Palace and Aztec ruins. For a different feel, consider Alameda Central — a historic park beside the Palacio de Bellas Artes and the start of Avenida Juárez.

How do you get around downtown Mexico City?

The Metro is extensive and inexpensive, Metrobús bus rapid transit runs along major avenues, and Ecobici bike share works well in the central districts. Walking is the best way to explore the core itself.

When is the best time to visit Mexico City?

Down.Town's pick is October–April. Signature events include Day of the Dead (Late Oct–Nov 2).

Is downtown Mexico City a good place to live?

The Centro offers restored apartments in historic buildings, while Roma and Condesa are popular with international residents and remote workers. It scores 8/10 for walkability, 8/10 for transit and 8/10 for value in the Down.Town Index.
