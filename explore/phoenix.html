In 30 seconds

  • Best for: winter sun, a fast-growing arts scene and big-league sport within a few blocks of the light rail
  • Visit October to April; in summer, do anything outdoors before 10am or after sunset
  • Arizona stays on Mountain Standard Time all year, with no daylight saving, so double-check meeting times

Downtown Phoenix is the compact grid around Central Avenue and Washington and Jefferson streets, where office towers, City Hall, the federal courthouse, the Phoenix Convention Center, the downtown arena and Chase Field sit within a short walk of one another. Arizona State University's downtown campus and the Phoenix Biomedical Campus fill the blocks to the north, and beyond them Roosevelt Row has grown from a handful of artist-run galleries into the city's most walkable neighbourhood of murals, cafés and new apartment buildings. Central Avenue then carries the light rail north through Midtown, home to the city's two big museums.

Downtown is still a work in progress, and that is part of its interest. For decades the metro grew outwards and the centre emptied after office hours; a university campus, light rail and thousands of new apartments have changed that, though you will still find surface car parks between revived blocks. What makes it work is a winter climate few US cities can match, a rail line that links the core with Tempe and the airport, and an arts scene that punches above its weight. What makes it hard is summer: from late spring to early autumn the sun turns wide streets into ovens, and the city's rhythm shifts to early mornings and late evenings.

Landmarks worth your time

  • Heritage Square. A block of restored 1890s homes, including the turreted Victorian Rosson House, now a museum with guided tours; the shady courtyards make a good pause between sights.
  • Arizona Science Center. Hands-on science galleries and a planetarium next to Heritage Square. The Children's Museum of Phoenix, in a 1913 schoolhouse a few blocks east, is the other family favourite.
  • Civic Space Park. A small park on the ASU campus, shaded by a vast floating net sculpture, Her Secret Is Patience, which glows in changing colours after dark.
  • Orpheum Theatre. A 1929 Spanish Baroque Revival movie palace, restored and owned by the city, now hosting concerts, comedy and touring shows.
  • Japanese Friendship Garden. A tranquil stroll garden with koi ponds and a teahouse in Hance Park, created with Phoenix's sister city Himeji; check opening hours before you go, especially in summer.
  • Heard Museum. A respected museum of American Indian art and cultures on Central Avenue, with strong galleries on the Native nations of the Southwest.
  • Phoenix Art Museum. A broad collection spanning American, Asian, European, Latin American and contemporary art, plus fashion design, beside the light rail in Midtown.
  • Arizona Capitol Museum. The copper-domed former territorial capitol west of downtown, completed at the turn of the 20th century and now a free museum of state history.

A perfect day downtown

Morning: start early, and in summer very early. Walk the shady paths of Heritage Square and peek into the Rosson House, then cross to the Arizona Science Center if you have children in tow. On Saturdays, the Downtown Phoenix Farmers Market sets up near Van Buren Street, a good place for breakfast burritos, local honey and Arizona citrus in season.

Afternoon: ride the light rail north on Central Avenue to the Heard Museum and the Phoenix Art Museum, which make the hottest hours pleasant. The Burton Barr Central Library and Hance Park are a short walk away, and the Japanese Friendship Garden is at its best in the softer winter light.

Evening: head back to Roosevelt Row as the heat breaks to wander the murals and galleries, then eat on a patio, misted in the warm months and warmed by heaters in winter. If there is a game on, the crowd will carry you to Chase Field or the arena; otherwise, see what is playing at the Orpheum or the Herberger Theater Center.

Eat and drink

Phoenix eats with a Sonoran accent. Its Mexican food owes most to the neighbouring Mexican state of Sonora: thin, supple flour tortillas, carne asada grilled over mesquite, and the Sonoran hot dog, wrapped in bacon, tucked into a soft bolillo roll and piled with beans, onions, tomatoes and salsa. Arizona also claims the cheese crisp, a giant flour tortilla baked flat under melted cheese, and makes a strong case for the chimichanga. Native American frybread, prickly-pear everything and date shakes made with dates grown in the state's south-west round out the local canon, while a new generation of chef-led kitchens downtown cooks with desert ingredients such as tepary beans, mesquite flour and citrus.

Eating well cheaply is easy if you follow the locals: taquerias and food trucks for lunch, generous happy hours timed for the after-work crowd, and the Saturday farmers market. Breakfast and brunch are a serious institution, partly because mornings are the most comfortable time to be out. Downtown's dining is concentrated around Roosevelt Row, the blocks near the arena and the Convention Center, and along Central Avenue into Midtown; on game and concert nights, book ahead or eat early. Craft breweries and cocktail bars cluster in the Warehouse District and along Grand Avenue.

Getting around

Valley Metro runs the light rail. Since 2025 it has operated as two lines that meet at the Downtown Phoenix Hub on Central Avenue: the A Line heads east through Tempe, past Arizona State University's main campus, to Mesa, and the B Line runs north–south along Central Avenue from north Phoenix to south Phoenix. For Sky Harbor, take the A Line to 44th Street/Washington and change to the free PHX Sky Train, which runs to the terminals; it is quick from downtown and avoids airport parking. Pay with a reloadable Copper Card or the Valley Metro app and scan each time you board; once you reach the daily, weekly or monthly cap, further rides cost nothing. Buses fill in the grid but run less often.

Downtown itself is walkable, and the core, Roosevelt Row and the ASU campus are all within about 20 minutes of each other on foot, but distance is rarely the problem: sun and shade are. In summer, plan routes along shaded streets and arcades and carry water. Cycling is pleasant in winter, with bike lanes on several downtown streets and canal paths further out, and dockless e-scooters are common. For most trips beyond the light rail, Phoenicians drive: I-10, I-17, Loop 202 and SR 51 all ring or cross downtown, and garages and surface lots are plentiful, though prices jump on game and event nights. Ride-hailing fills the gaps, especially late at night.

Where to stay

The downtown core is the practical choice for conventions, games and light rail access: you can walk to the Convention Center, Chase Field and the arena, and ride to the airport. Roosevelt Row and the streets around the ASU campus suit travellers who want independent cafés, galleries and bars outside the door, though much of the accommodation there is in apartments and short-term rentals rather than hotels. Midtown along Central Avenue is quieter, close to the museums and still on the light rail. If you are visiting in winter for sun rather than the city, the resort areas of north-east Phoenix, the Camelback Corridor and neighbouring Scottsdale offer pools and golf, but you will need a car. In summer, whatever you book, make sure the pool has shade and the air conditioning is reliable.

Living downtown

Downtown housing is mostly new. Mid-rise apartment buildings with pools, gyms and covered parking have filled lots around Roosevelt Row, Evans Churchill and the ASU campus over the past decade or so, alongside a smaller number of condo towers. For something older, the historic districts just north of downtown, including Roosevelt, F.Q. Story, Willo and Encanto-Palmcroft, offer 1920s and 1930s bungalows and revival-style houses on tree-lined streets, close enough to cycle or take the light rail to work. Compared with coastal cities, Phoenix still offers more space for the money, though rents and home prices rose sharply during the pandemic-era boom.

Leases usually run 12 months, and Arizona law caps security deposits and prepaid rent at one and a half months' rent. Since 2025, Arizona cities can no longer levy their transaction privilege tax on residential rent, which removed a line many Phoenix tenants used to see on their bills. Budget instead for summer electricity, which can be the biggest variable cost of the year, and check whether your building has covered parking: a car left in the July sun can be too hot to touch. Downtown suits students, young professionals, healthcare and university staff and anyone who wants to drive less; families more often choose the historic districts or the suburbs for space and schools.

Doing business downtown

Downtown Phoenix is the seat of city, county and state government, home to the federal courthouse and a base for law firms, banks, utilities and the consultants who serve them. The office towers cluster around Central Avenue and Washington and Jefferson streets, while the Warehouse District's brick buildings house technology, design and marketing firms that prefer exposed beams to marble lobbies. Higher education and medicine are the growth engines: ASU's downtown campus brings thousands of students and staff into the core, and the Phoenix Biomedical Campus combines medical schools, research institutes and laboratories. Across the wider Valley, semiconductor plants, data centres, logistics and financial back offices drive the economy, mostly in suburban business parks rather than downtown.

Retail downtown is shaped by events and seasons. Convention days, concerts and games at the arena and Chase Field drive restaurant and bar takings around the core, while Roosevelt Row's independent shops and galleries rely on residents, students and the monthly First Friday crowds. Expect a busy winter and spring and a summer slump, when locals stay indoors and convention calendars thin out. Seasons and weather

Winter, from December to February, is why people come: sunny days warm enough for patios, cool evenings, nights that can dip towards freezing and occasional rain. Pack a light jacket or fleece for after dark. Spring, in March and April, is close to perfect, with desert wildflowers in good years and the city at its busiest. From May the heat builds quickly; June is usually dry and fierce, and through July and August afternoon highs regularly top 105°F (about 40°C), with nights that barely cool. The monsoon, officially mid-June to the end of September, brings humidity, spectacular lightning, sudden downpours that flood streets and washes, and towering walls of dust known as haboobs. October and November bring the patios back to life. Whatever the season, pack sunscreen, sunglasses, a hat and a refillable bottle, plus a layer for buildings where the air conditioning is set to arctic.

Safety and practicalities

Heat is the biggest risk in Phoenix, and it catches visitors out every year. In summer, avoid strenuous walking or hiking in the middle of the day, drink water before you feel thirsty and learn the signs of heat exhaustion: dizziness, nausea, headache and heavy sweating. The region's heat relief network opens cooling centres and hydration stations in the hot months, and public libraries are air-conditioned refuges. During monsoon storms, never drive into a flooded road or wash; Arizona's so-called stupid motorist law can make drivers who go around barricades pay for their own rescue. Downtown is generally safe and busy around the core, the campus and Roosevelt Row. Homelessness is visible in parts of the city centre, and car break-ins are the most common problem, so leave nothing on show. The light rail is well used and has security staff, but stay alert late at night. Newer buildings, the light rail and most museums are step-free, and mobile coverage is strong across the core.

Local tips

  • Arizona does not change its clocks, so in summer Phoenix shares the time with Los Angeles and in winter with Denver.
  • Use the free PHX Sky Train between the light rail and the airport terminals rather than paying for airport parking.
  • In summer, plan sightseeing in heat order: outdoors before 10am, museums through the afternoon, outdoors again after sunset.
  • Park in a garage or in shade; a car left in the sun gets hot enough to damage phones and scorch hands on the steering wheel.
  • Ride the A Line to Tempe for Arizona State University's main campus and the paths around Tempe Town Lake.
  • On game nights, take the light rail rather than driving; event parking near the core fills quickly and costs more.
