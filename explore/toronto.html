In 30 seconds

  • Best for: food from every corner of the world, sports and big-city energy without the coastal prices of New York
  • Union Station is the hub for the airport train, regional trains and the subway
  • Winters are long; PATH keeps you indoors across much of the core

Downtown Toronto runs from the lakeshore up toward Bloor Street, and within that span it packs the country's financial centre, an entertainment district, two major universities' worth of students and one of North America's densest clusters of residential towers. Its edges are set by the Don Valley to the east and Bathurst Street, more or less, to the west, with the CN Tower as the compass point everyone uses.

The city works at street level and underground. The PATH network of walkways connects office towers, malls and transit beneath the Financial District, while streetcars and the subway carry people above. In summer, the island ferries and the waterfront trail pull everyone back toward the lake. The trade-off for all that energy is cost and congestion: housing is expensive, construction cranes are everywhere and traffic can crawl, so the people who enjoy downtown most are the ones who walk, cycle and ride transit.

Landmarks worth your time

  • CN Tower. The city's defining landmark, with observation levels and a glass floor.
  • St. Lawrence Market. A historic food market known for its peameal bacon sandwiches and Saturday farmers' market.
  • Nathan Phillips Square. City Hall's plaza, with the illuminated Toronto sign and a winter skating rink.
  • Toronto Islands. A short ferry ride to beaches, bike paths and the best skyline view in the city.
  • Art Gallery of Ontario. A major collection in a building reworked by architect Frank Gehry, on the edge of Chinatown.
  • Distillery District. Pedestrian streets of restored Victorian industrial buildings, with shops and a winter market.
  • Royal Ontario Museum. Canada's largest museum of world cultures and natural history, at the northern edge of downtown, with a crystal-shaped extension on Bloor Street.
  • Union Station. The restored Beaux-Arts station and its great hall, the hub for the subway, GO trains, VIA Rail and the airport train.

A perfect day downtown

Morning: start at St. Lawrence Market (closed Mondays) for a peameal bacon sandwich, then walk east through the Old Town's Victorian streets to the Distillery District before the crowds arrive. Loop back along Front Street to Union Station and its great hall.

Afternoon: in summer, take the ferry from the foot of Bay Street to the Toronto Islands and walk or cycle to the beaches on the far side. Otherwise, head up to the Art Gallery of Ontario, then wander through Chinatown and the vintage shops and food stalls of Kensington Market next door.

Evening: ride up the CN Tower for sunset, or catch the ferry back at golden hour for the skyline. Have dinner on King Street West or Ossington, then pick a show in the Entertainment District, a game at the stadium beside the tower, or a late drink on Queen West.

Eat and drink

Toronto eats globally: Chinatown and Kensington Market sit on the western edge of downtown, Little Italy and Koreatown are a streetcar ride away, and the core has everything from food courts to chef-driven rooms. The city's restaurant promotion weeks offer fixed-price menus that make high-end dining more accessible. Further afield but easy by transit, Greektown on the Danforth, Little Portugal, Gerrard Street's Little India and the Chinese malls of the northern suburbs show how wide the city's range really is.

To eat well cheaply, use the PATH food courts on weekdays, the market's sandwich and pastry counters, the dumpling and noodle houses of Chinatown and the street-food stalls of Kensington. Patio season runs from late spring to early autumn, when restaurants spill onto sidewalks and curb lanes. Beer and wine are sold in the provincial liquor stores and, increasingly, in corner stores and supermarkets.

Getting around

The TTC's Line 1 forms a U through downtown, and streetcars run along King, Queen and other east–west streets. Line 2 runs east–west along Bloor Street at the top of downtown, and the Ontario Line, a new subway through the core, is under construction. The UP Express connects Union Station with Pearson Airport, and GO Transit trains and buses fan out to the region from Union; fares are integrated, so a TTC ride is free when you transfer to or from GO using PRESTO or contactless. Billy Bishop airport on the Toronto Islands is reached by a pedestrian tunnel at the foot of Bathurst Street.

Bike Share Toronto has hundreds of stations, protected bike lanes run along several main routes, and much of the core is walkable year-round thanks to the PATH. Driving downtown is slow: King Street prioritises streetcars over through traffic, long-running reconstruction on the Gardiner Expressway causes lane closures, and parking in towers and municipal Green P lots adds up quickly. When the subway closes for the night, the Blue Night bus network takes over on main routes.

Where to stay

The Financial District and the streets around Union Station are the most practical base for business and for arriving on the airport train. The Entertainment District suits theatre-goers, festival visitors and anyone who wants nightlife on the doorstep, though it can be loud late at weekends. St. Lawrence and the Old Town are quieter and handy for the market and the Distillery District. Yorkville, at the top of downtown, is the upscale shopping and gallery choice, and the Harbourfront puts the lake and the island ferries outside your window. During TIFF in September, rooms near King Street fill up fast.

Living downtown

Condominium towers dominate housing; many rentals are individually owned condo units, so lease terms and quality vary by landlord. Neighbourhoods like St. Lawrence and CityPlace offer different trade-offs between space, noise and price. Factor in condo fees and parking costs if you buy, along with Toronto's municipal land transfer tax, which buyers in the city pay on top of the provincial one.

Most residential tenancies in Ontario must use the province's Standard Lease. Landlords can collect a deposit for the last month's rent but not a damage deposit, and annual increases are capped by a provincial guideline — except in units first occupied after November 15, 2018, which are exempt from that cap, so read the lease carefully on newer condos. Disputes go to the Landlord and Tenant Board. Downtown suits students, young professionals and couples who value walking to work and nightlife over square footage; families tend to look at the streetcar neighbourhoods just beyond the core.

Doing business downtown

Bay Street is Canada's financial centre, home to the major banks and the stock exchange. Tech, media and professional services fill the King West and Liberty Village offices. Street retail is strongest along Queen West and Yonge Street, where pedestrian volumes are among the highest in the country. Newer office towers have spread south of Union Station towards the lake, while the hospitals and universities around University Avenue anchor a large life-sciences and research cluster.

The PATH is a retail asset in its own right, with food courts and services that do most of their trade on weekday lunchtimes. Bloor-Yorkville is the luxury corridor, and the Distillery District and Kensington Market suit independent and experiential brands. Read our guide to office and commercial space in downtown Toronto.

Signature events

JunePride Toronto — One of the largest Pride celebrations in the world, centred on the Church-Wellesley Village.
SeptemberToronto International Film Festival — A major film festival that fills the Entertainment District with premieres.
Early OctoberNuit Blanche — An overnight festival of contemporary art installations across the city.

Dates move from year to year. Check the organiser's official site before booking travel.

Seasons and weather

Toronto's summers are warm to hot and often humid, with thunderstorms and the occasional heat warning; the lake breeze helps near the water. Autumn is the best season — mild, clear days and colour in the ravines through October. Winters are cold and grey rather than brutal by Canadian standards, with snow on and off from December to March and wind off the lake that makes it feel colder. Spring is short and changeable. Pack light layers and sunscreen in summer, a warm coat, waterproof boots and gloves in winter, and an umbrella year-round.

Safety and practicalities

Downtown Toronto is generally safe and busy at most hours. Watch for pickpockets in crowds around Union Station, Yonge and Dundas and big events, and expect visible homelessness and mental-health crises around some transit hubs and parks; the usual big-city awareness is enough. After the subway closes, stick to Blue Night buses, taxis or rideshares. Most but not all subway stations have elevators, so check accessibility before you travel. In summer, take heat warnings seriously and drink plenty of water; in winter, sidewalks can be icy and slushy. Free Wi-Fi is available in subway stations and libraries.

Local tips

  • Buy a PRESTO card or use contactless payment — fares cap per trip with free transfers within two hours.
  • Take the ferry to Ward's Island in the late afternoon for the skyline at sunset.
  • Use the PATH map apps on winter days; the network is large and easy to get turned around in.
  • Shelf prices exclude Ontario's 13% HST, so budget a little extra at the till.
  • On summer weekends, buy island ferry tickets online in advance to skip part of the queue.
