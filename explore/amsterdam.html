Amsterdam's Centrum is defined by the 17th-century canal ring, a half-moon of concentric canals lined with narrow gabled houses. The ring is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and still works as a residential and commercial district, wrapped around a medieval core that grew up where the Amstel river was dammed.

Bikes rule the streets. Most locals cycle everywhere, and visitors who walk need to watch the bike lanes carefully. Free ferries behind Central Station carry people across the IJ to the creative district of Amsterdam-Noord. The centre is compact enough to cross on foot in under an hour, which is its great strength; its weakness is popularity, with the busiest lanes crowded from spring to autumn and housing stretched thin.

The districts

Old CentreThe medieval core around Dam Square, with the Oude Kerk, the red-light streets and the busy shopping lanes between Central Station and the Munt tower.
Canal RingThe canal belt of grand merchant houses along the Herengracht, Keizersgracht and Prinsengracht.
JordaanA neighbourhood of small canals, cafés and boutiques, once working-class and now one of the most sought-after addresses in the city.
Nine StreetsSpecialty shopping streets crossing the canals, full of independent fashion, vintage and design shops.
PlantageA leafy quarter east of the centre with the zoo, the botanical garden and the Jewish Cultural Quarter.
NoordFormer shipyards and industrial land across the IJ, now studios, festivals and waterside terraces, a free ferry ride away.

Landmarks worth your time

  • Rijksmuseum. The national museum of Dutch art and history, home to Rembrandt's 'The Night Watch' and Vermeer's masterpieces.
  • Van Gogh Museum. The world's largest collection of Van Gogh's work, on Museumplein just south of the canal ring.
  • Anne Frank House. The canal-side house where Anne Frank and her family hid; tickets are sold online only and go quickly.
  • Dam Square and the Royal Palace. The city's central square, with the 17th-century former city hall turned royal palace, open to visitors when not in use for state occasions.
  • Begijnhof. A hidden medieval courtyard of almshouses and gardens, a calm escape near the shopping streets.
  • Canal cruises. Boat trips through the UNESCO-listed canal ring — the most relaxed way to see the merchant houses.
  • Westerkerk. The Renaissance church beside the Anne Frank House, where Rembrandt is buried; its tower is the tallest in the old city.
  • Jewish Cultural Quarter. The Portuguese Synagogue, the Jewish Museum and the National Holocaust Museum, grouped together in the Plantage.

A perfect day downtown

Morning: be at the Rijksmuseum or Van Gogh Museum at opening with a timed ticket, then walk north up the Spiegelgracht and into the canal ring while the light is soft and the streets are quiet. Stop at the flower market on the Singel on the way.

Afternoon: browse the Nine Streets, then cross into the Jordaan for its small canals and courtyard almshouses. Visit the Anne Frank House at your booked time, climb the Westerkerk tower if it is open, and take an hour-long canal cruise to see the city from the water.

Evening: take the free ferry from behind Central Station to Noord for sunset over the IJ, then come back for dinner and a beer in a brown café in the Jordaan or along the Haarlemmerstraat.

Eat and drink

Herring stands, cheese shops, apple pie and Indonesian rijsttafel are the local classics, and 'brown cafés' — old wood-panelled pubs — are the place for a beer. Food halls in converted tram depots and the creative venues across the IJ in Noord have broadened the scene considerably.

Eat like a local with bitterballen (crisp fried ragout balls) and a beer at the after-work borrel, fried fish (kibbeling) from a market stall, warm stroopwafels and Surinamese roti, a legacy of the Dutch colonial past. The Albert Cuyp street market in De Pijp and the Noordermarkt in the Jordaan, with an organic farmers' market on Saturdays, are the best places to graze cheaply. Dutch lunch is light, often a sandwich, while dinner tends to be early, from around 6 p.m.

Getting around

Trams run through the centre, the metro reaches the north and south, and trains from Central Station link to Schiphol in about 15–20 minutes. GVB runs the trams, metro, buses and the free ferries across the IJ, while national rail services run from Central Station and from Amsterdam Zuid. The north–south metro line cuts under the old centre from Noord to Zuid. Whether you use an OV-chipkaart or a contactless bank card, tap in and tap out every time, or you will be charged a higher fare. Trams and the metro stop around midnight; night buses take over.

Cycling is the fastest way around: rent a bike, use two locks and follow the hand signals of the crowd. Driving into the centre makes little sense — streets are narrow, parking is scarce and expensive, and an environmental zone restricts older vehicles. Visitors who arrive by car usually leave it at a Park and Ride site on the edge of the city and finish the trip by public transport.

Where to stay

First-timers who want everything on the doorstep stay in the canal ring, where you can walk to the museums, Dam Square and the Jordaan. The Old Centre is the most convenient for trains but noisy late at night, especially near the red-light streets. Couples and repeat visitors often prefer the Jordaan or the Nine Streets for their village feel. Families do well in the Plantage, near the zoo and green space. For more room for the money, look at De Pijp, Oost or Noord, all a short tram, metro or ferry ride from the centre. A percentage-based tourist tax is added to overnight stays.

Living downtown

Housing is very tight, and the centre is among the most expensive places to live in the Netherlands. Much of the city's housing belongs to housing associations with long waiting lists, so newcomers mostly rent privately. Recent national rules have extended rent regulation to more mid-range homes through a points system, made open-ended leases the norm and capped deposits at two months' basic rent. You must register your address with the municipality to receive a citizen service number (BSN), needed for work, banking and health insurance.

The city limits holiday rentals with registration rules and a cap on nights per year, which has returned some homes to residents. Canal houses are beautiful but often have very steep stairs, and furniture is lifted in through the windows with hoisting beams. Downtown suits people who live by bike and want culture and cafés outside the door; families and those who need more space tend to look at Oost, Zuid, Noord or nearby towns with fast train links.

Doing business downtown

Tourism, creative agencies, tech and European headquarters drive the economy, with the Zuidas district in the south as the main office hub. Smaller firms, agencies and start-ups take canal-house offices in the centre, while Sloterdijk in the west and the area around Amstel station in the east offer larger floors closer to rail links.

Central storefronts benefit from huge visitor numbers but operate under rules designed to protect residents and reduce overtourism, including limits on certain types of tourist-focused shops in the centre. Register your business with the Chamber of Commerce (KVK), and check zoning before you sign. Read more about office and commercial space in downtown Amsterdam.

Signature events

Late AprilKing's Day — A city-wide street party and free market on the king's birthday, when the whole city wears orange.
Early AugustPride Amsterdam — Canal Parade and celebrations, with decorated boats along the Prinsengracht.
Dec–JanAmsterdam Light Festival — Light art along the canals, seen on foot or by boat.

Dates move from year to year. Check the organiser's official site before booking travel.

Seasons and weather

Amsterdam has a mild, damp maritime climate, and rain can arrive in any month. Spring brings tulip season and the first café terraces, though winds stay cool. Summer is pleasant rather than hot, with long evenings that suit canal-side drinks. Autumn turns wet and blustery from October, and winter is grey and chilly, with occasional frost and very short days. Pack a proper rain jacket rather than an umbrella, which the wind will defeat, and comfortable shoes for cobbles and bridges.

Safety and practicalities

The city is safe by any standard, but pickpockets work Central Station, the Damrak, crowded trams and the red-light streets. Never buy drugs from street dealers, and note that smoking cannabis on the street in the red-light area is banned and photographing the windows is not tolerated. Canals rarely have railings, so take care on the edges late at night. Bike theft is common, so lock up properly. Steep stairs and cobblestones make the old centre harder for wheelchair users, though most trams are low-floor. Card payments work almost everywhere, and connectivity is excellent.

Local tips

  • Stay out of red bike lanes when walking — cyclists will not stop for you.
  • Take the free ferry behind Central Station to Amsterdam-Noord for views and creative venues.
  • Book the big museums ahead in spring and summer.
  • Always tap out when leaving a tram, metro or train, or you will pay a higher fare.
  • Visit the Jordaan's markets on a Saturday morning, before the crowds.
