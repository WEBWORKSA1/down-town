In 30 seconds

  • Best for: tax-efficient living and doing business at a global crossroads
  • Summers are extremely hot; most outdoor life happens from October to April
  • Respect local customs, especially during Ramadan

Dubai's modern centre is the master-planned district around the Burj Khalifa, the tallest building in the world, along with the neighbouring financial free zone and Business Bay. It is a district of malls, fountains and towers built in barely two decades. (The developer markets this district under its own brand name; Down.Town is not connected to it.)

The older centre lies along Dubai Creek, in Deira and Bur Dubai, where wooden boats still carry passengers across the water and souks sell gold, spices and textiles. Seeing both halves is the best way to understand the city. The new centre is clean, safe and spectacular but built around cars and air-conditioning; the old one is walkable, crowded and far more affordable, home to the communities from South Asia, Iran and the Arab world who built the city's trade.

Landmarks worth your time

  • Burj Khalifa. The world's tallest building, with observation decks; the sunset slots sell out first.
  • Dubai Fountain. Choreographed fountain shows in the evening, free to watch from the lakeside promenade.
  • Dubai Opera. A performing-arts venue near the fountain lake, shaped like a traditional dhow.
  • Al Fahidi Historical Neighbourhood. Restored wind-tower houses and museums in Bur Dubai.
  • Dubai Creek abras. Traditional wooden boats crossing the creek for a small fare.
  • Gold and Spice Souks. Traditional markets in Deira.
  • Museum of the Future. A torus-shaped building wrapped in Arabic calligraphy on Sheikh Zayed Road, with immersive exhibitions on technology and society.
  • Dubai Frame. A giant picture-frame structure in Zabeel Park, with a glass-floored bridge looking towards the old city on one side and the new on the other.

A perfect day downtown

Morning: start in Old Dubai while it is cool. Walk the lanes of Al Fahidi and the covered textile souk, then cross the Creek by abra for a few dirhams to wander the spice and gold souks in Deira.

Afternoon: retreat from the heat. Take the metro to the Museum of the Future or the Dubai Frame, or browse the galleries of the financial free zone. Lunch in Karama, where South Asian and Middle Eastern cooking is excellent and inexpensive.

Evening: ride the Red Line to the Burj Khalifa for a sunset slot on the observation deck, then watch the fountain show from the lakeside. Finish with dinner in the financial district or a walk along the Business Bay canal when the evening air cools.

Eat and drink

Dubai's dining ranges from hotel restaurants run by internationally known chefs to small Indian, Pakistani, Iranian, Lebanese and Emirati eateries in the older districts, where a full meal costs a fraction of the tower-top price. Brunches on Fridays and weekends are a social institution. The financial free zone has many upscale restaurants and bars.

Look out for Emirati dishes such as machboos (spiced rice with meat or fish) and luqaimat (sweet dumplings with date syrup), plus shawarma, grilled fish and cups of sweet, spiced karak tea from roadside cafeterias. Fresh dates and Arabic coffee are part of every welcome. Alcohol is served only in licensed venues, mostly in hotels, and the legal drinking age is 21. During Ramadan, many restaurants serve during the day, but eating and drinking in public during fasting hours is best avoided out of respect.

Getting around

Dubai Metro's Red Line runs along Sheikh Zayed Road, serving the Burj Khalifa district, the free zones and the airport; the Green Line covers Old Dubai. A Nol card pays for metro, tram and buses. Trains have a premium Gold Class carriage and a section reserved for women and children, and eating or drinking on board is banned. Abras and water buses cross the Creek, and the Roads and Transport Authority runs both the public transport and the taxi fleet.

Taxis and ride-hailing are common and inexpensive by global standards. Walking between districts is impractical in summer heat, although air-conditioned bridges connect several metro stations to towers and malls. Drivers pay electronic road tolls on the main highways, and parking in the centre is paid by zone. Traffic on Sheikh Zayed Road is heavy at rush hour, so the metro is often faster.

Where to stay

For first-timers, the Burj Khalifa district puts the fountain, the tower and the metro within walking distance, but it is also the priciest base. Business travellers favour the financial free zone, Sheikh Zayed Road and Business Bay for quick access to meetings and the metro. Deira and Bur Dubai offer the best value and the most atmosphere, close to the souks and the airport. Beach-focused trips are better served by Jumeirah or the Marina, a long metro ride away. Hotels add a per-night tourism fee to every stay.

Living downtown

Most residents rent apartments in towers, with annual leases often paid in one to four cheques. The UAE does not levy personal income tax, which draws many professionals. Summer heat shapes daily life: most activity moves indoors from June to September, and air-conditioning costs should be included in your budget.

Residential leases must be registered through Ejari, the city's tenancy registration system, which you also need to connect utilities. Rent increases on renewal are governed by the official rental index, and landlords must give advance notice of changes. Budget for a security deposit, agency fees and a municipal housing fee that appears on utility bills. Downtown suits people who want convenience, views and amenities on the doorstep; families often look to suburbs with villas, schools and parks.

Doing business downtown

Trade, finance, real estate, tourism and logistics dominate. The financial free zone operates its own common-law courts, and dozens of free zones offer company-setup routes with full foreign ownership. A federal corporate tax applies to profits above a threshold. Retail in mall-based districts is highly competitive; street-level independents do better in older neighbourhoods.

Mainland companies are licensed by the Department of Economy and Tourism and can trade anywhere in the emirate, while free-zone companies usually operate within their zone or through local distributors. VAT applies to most goods and services. Office location affects your licence and visa options, so choose space and licence together. Read our guide to office and commercial space in downtown Dubai.

Signature events

Dec–JanDubai Shopping Festival — Sales, entertainment and fireworks across the city.
December 31New Year's Eve — Fireworks at the Burj Khalifa, with crowds gathering hours early.
Varies (lunar calendar)Ramadan — A month of fasting with evening iftar gatherings and late-night markets.

Dates move from year to year. Check the organiser's official site before booking travel.

Seasons and weather

Winter, from November to March, is the season to visit: warm, sunny days, cool evenings and outdoor dining. April, May and October are hot, and June to September brings extreme heat and humidity, when even short walks are draining. Rain is rare, but sudden heavy downpours can flood roads. Pack light, breathable clothing that covers shoulders and knees for souks and religious sites, sunglasses and sunscreen, and a light layer for the powerful air-conditioning indoors.

Safety and practicalities

Dubai has very low crime, and walking at night is safe, but laws are strict. Avoid public displays of affection, offensive gestures and swearing, including online, and don't photograph people without their permission. Drug offences carry severe penalties, so check before travelling with prescription medicine. Heat is the main hazard: drink water, avoid midday sun in summer and carry water in the car. The metro is step-free with lifts. Some internet calling services are restricted, and a local SIM or eSIM is easy to buy at the airport.

Local tips

  • Book Burj Khalifa observation tickets for sunset in advance.
  • Ride an abra across the Creek.
  • Dress modestly in traditional areas and religious sites.
  • Use the metro at rush hour; Sheikh Zayed Road traffic can be slow.
  • During Ramadan, shops and restaurants keep late hours, and evenings are lively after iftar.
