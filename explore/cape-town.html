In 30 seconds

  • Best for: dramatic scenery and outdoor living
  • Check weather before going up Table Mountain

Cape Town's City Bowl is a natural amphitheatre formed by Table Mountain, Lion's Head and Signal Hill, opening onto Table Bay. The CBD's grid holds historic buildings, the Company's Garden and busy streets, with the Foreshore's newer towers built on land reclaimed from the sea.

Bo-Kaap's colourful houses sit on the slopes above, and the V&A Waterfront is a working harbour transformed into shops, restaurants and museums. The setting is the city's great asset: a mountain national park begins at the top of residential streets. The challenges are equally real — the legacy of apartheid planning, visible inequality and a downtown that empties after office hours on some blocks — so plan your evenings and movements with care.

The districts

CBDThe grid of historic buildings, Greenmarket Square and the Company's Garden, with Bree Street's restaurants and Long Street's bars.
Bo-KaapThe colourful Cape Malay heritage quarter on the slopes of Signal Hill, a living community with mosques and family homes.
V&A WaterfrontA working harbour turned into a district of shops, restaurants, hotels and museums.
Gardens and De WaterkantResidential streets below Table Mountain, and a quarter of cafés and design shops near the Waterfront.
ForeshoreOffice towers, the convention centre and the main railway station on reclaimed land between the old town and the harbour.
WoodstockAn older inner suburb east of the CBD with street art, design studios, galleries and weekend food markets.

Landmarks worth your time

  • Table Mountain. Reached by a rotating cableway or by hiking trails, with views over the city and the ocean when the weather allows.
  • Company's Garden. A historic public garden in the heart of the city, beside museums and the parliament buildings.
  • Bo-Kaap. Bright houses, mosques and a museum telling the story of the Cape Malay community.
  • Zeitz MOCAA. A museum of contemporary African art inside a dramatically carved former grain silo.
  • Robben Island. The UNESCO-listed island prison where Nelson Mandela was held, reached by ferry from the Waterfront.
  • Castle of Good Hope. A 17th-century star fort, the oldest surviving colonial building in South Africa.
  • District Six Museum. A moving museum on the community forcibly removed from the inner city under apartheid, a short walk from the Castle.
  • Lion's Head and Signal Hill. A popular hike to the top of Lion's Head for sunrise or sunset, and a road up Signal Hill for views over the bay.

A perfect day downtown

Morning: if the sky is clear and the wind calm, go straight up Table Mountain by cableway, or hike up via the Platteklip Gorge path if you are fit. Weather changes fast, so do the mountain first and the museums later.

Afternoon: walk through the Company's Garden, stop at the Iziko South African Museum or the National Gallery, then wander down to Greenmarket Square and the steep, colourful streets of Bo-Kaap. Visit the District Six Museum to understand how the city was shaped.

Evening: head to the Waterfront for the harbour at sunset and Zeitz MOCAA if it is open late, or drive up Signal Hill to watch the sun drop into the Atlantic. Dinner on Bree Street, then take a ride-hailing car home.

Eat and drink

Cape Malay cooking — bobotie, curries and koeksisters — sits alongside excellent seafood and a fine-dining scene that draws on wine regions an hour away. Bree Street in the CBD is a strip of restaurants and bars, and the Waterfront's food market is good for a casual lunch.

Try snoek, a firm local fish often grilled over coals; a gatsby, the city's enormous filled roll; and a braai, the South African barbecue that anchors weekend social life. Biltong and droëwors make good snacks for a hike. Weekend food markets in Woodstock and near the Waterfront are the most enjoyable way to eat cheaply, and local wine is very good value in restaurants. Tap water is safe to drink.

Getting around

MyCiTi bus rapid transit links the CBD with the Waterfront, the Atlantic seaboard and the airport. Ride-hailing is widely used and recommended at night. Minibus taxis are a large part of local transport but can be confusing for newcomers. Metrorail commuter trains run from Cape Town station to the suburbs, but services have been unreliable in recent years, so check before you plan around them.

Walking within the CBD, Gardens and the Waterfront is pleasant by day. Most residents drive, and hiring a car makes sense for trips to the Cape Peninsula and the winelands; South Africa drives on the left. Downtown street parking is paid, and informal car guards will offer to watch your car for a small tip. Cycling is growing, with a promenade path along the Atlantic coast, but bike lanes are limited and the hills are steep.

Where to stay

The Waterfront is the easiest base for first-timers, with security, harbour views and everything within walking distance. De Waterkant and Green Point put you between the Waterfront and the CBD, close to the stadium and the Sea Point promenade. The CBD and Bree Street suit those who want restaurants and nightlife on the doorstep. Gardens, Tamboerskloof and Oranjezicht offer quieter guesthouses and mountain views. For beaches, many visitors stay on the Atlantic seaboard in Sea Point or Camps Bay and come into town by MyCiTi or car.

Living downtown

City Bowl apartments and houses in Gardens, Tamboerskloof and De Waterkant are popular with professionals. Security is a practical consideration: many buildings have controlled access, and residents tend to drive or use ride-hailing after dark. Power supply has become far more stable in recent years after a long period of scheduled outages. Leases usually run for twelve months with a deposit of one or two months' rent, which the landlord must hold in an interest-bearing account.

Demand for City Bowl homes has grown with people moving from other provinces and from abroad, so expect competition for well-located flats. The 2018 drought left lasting water-saving habits, and many homes have tanks or backup power. Downtown suits outdoor-minded people who value walking to work, mountain trails and restaurants; families often look to the southern suburbs or the Atlantic seaboard for schools and space.

Doing business downtown

Tourism, film and commercial production, technology startups, financial services and the wine trade drive the economy. Bree Street and the Waterfront attract hospitality businesses, while the tech scene clusters in the CBD and Woodstock. Seasonal tourism peaks in the southern summer, December to March.

The Foreshore and the CBD hold most of the corporate offices, with Century City to the north and Claremont in the southern suburbs as alternative office nodes. Many international firms run customer-service and back-office operations from the city, attracted by English-speaking talent and a time zone within an hour or two of Europe's. Signature events

Early JanuaryTweede Nuwe Jaar — The minstrel carnival, traditionally held on 2 January, with troupes parading through the city in satin and sequins.
MarchCape Town Cycle Tour — A mass cycling event around the Cape Peninsula.
MonthlyFirst Thursdays — Galleries open late, and the CBD fills with people on foot.

Dates move from year to year. Check the organiser's official site before booking travel.

Seasons and weather

Cape Town has a Mediterranean climate. Summer, from November to March, is warm, dry and sunny, often with a strong south-easterly wind known as the Cape Doctor. Autumn brings calmer, golden days and is a favourite with locals. Winter, from June to August, is cool and wet, with storms rolling in off the Atlantic and the cableway often closed. Spring is fresh and green, with wildflowers further up the west coast. The UV index is high all year, and the Atlantic water stays cold even in summer. Pack sunscreen, a windproof layer and a warm jumper for evenings.

Safety and practicalities

Petty crime and opportunistic theft are the main risks downtown. Keep phones out of sight on the street, leave nothing visible in a parked car and avoid walking alone through quiet streets after dark; use ride-hailing instead. On the mountain, hike in a group, stay on popular routes, carry water and a jacket, and turn back if cloud rolls in. Swim only at lifeguarded beaches. Outages are now rare, but many businesses keep backup power. Pavements can be uneven and the hills steep, so accessibility varies; the Waterfront is the easiest area for wheelchair users. Mobile data and fibre coverage are good in the City Bowl.

Local tips

  • Go up Table Mountain early and check the cableway's status — wind can close it.
  • Book Robben Island ferries ahead, especially in summer.
  • Use ride-hailing at night rather than walking between districts.
  • Respect Bo-Kaap as a residential neighbourhood: ask before photographing people or homes.
  • Carry a light jacket even on sunny days — the wind and fog can arrive quickly.
