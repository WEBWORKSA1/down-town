In 30 seconds

  • Best for: architecture, museums and food-hall grazing in a city most visitors only see from the freeway
  • Get a TAP card or the TAP app: Metro rail links downtown with Hollywood, Santa Monica, Pasadena and Long Beach
  • Downtown is a cluster of walkable districts — plan your day by neighbourhood, not by distance on the map

Downtown Los Angeles — DTLA to locals — is the dense, high-rise centre of a famously spread-out city, boxed in by freeways on three sides and the Los Angeles River on the fourth. Inside that box sit several very different districts. Bunker Hill and the Financial District hold the glass towers and the big cultural institutions of Grand Avenue. Below them, the Historic Core along Broadway and Spring Street is a grid of early-20th-century banks, department stores and movie palaces. To the east, Little Tokyo and the Arts District run toward the river, and El Pueblo, the city's founding site, sits beside Union Station on the northern edge.

For decades downtown emptied out after office hours. The revival began around 1999, when a city ordinance made it easier to turn old commercial buildings into apartments, and the Historic Core slowly filled with lofts, restaurants and galleries. Today downtown works because of density and rail: it is the one part of Los Angeles where you can genuinely leave the car behind. What still holds it back is unevenness — a polished plaza and a block of shuttered storefronts can be a street apart — so it rewards visitors who plan their route rather than wander at random.

Landmarks worth your time

  • Walt Disney Concert Hall. Frank Gehry's stainless-steel landmark on Grand Avenue, home of the Los Angeles Philharmonic. Free self-guided tours run on many days, and the hidden rooftop garden is worth the climb.
  • The Broad. A contemporary art museum across the street with free general admission; reserve a timed entry online, especially for weekends.
  • Grand Central Market. A food hall trading since 1917, running from Broadway through to Hill Street, with stalls covering much of the city's culinary range.
  • Angels Flight. A short, century-old funicular railway that climbs from Hill Street to Bunker Hill, directly opposite the market — a cheap and cheerful shortcut up the slope.
  • Bradbury Building. An 1893 office building with a sunlit atrium of wrought-iron balconies and open-cage lifts; visitors can usually look in from the ground floor.
  • Los Angeles City Hall. The 1928 tower stood above every other building in the city for decades. A free observation deck near the top is usually open on weekdays; bring photo ID for security.
  • Union Station. The 1939 terminal blends Mission Revival, Spanish Colonial and Art Deco, with a grand waiting room and shaded garden patios.
  • Olvera Street and El Pueblo. A lively market lane in the city's historic heart, home to the Avila Adobe, the oldest surviving house in Los Angeles.

A perfect day downtown

Start at Union Station, where the waiting room is at its calmest in the morning light, then cross Alameda Street to El Pueblo for a walk down Olvera Street and a look inside the Avila Adobe. Continue south to Little Tokyo for breakfast and a loop of its plazas before catching an A or E Line train from Little Tokyo/Arts District station: two stops take you to Historic Broadway, at the heart of the old theatre district.

Spend the late morning on Broadway, looking up at the theatre signs and terracotta façades, then graze your way through Grand Central Market for lunch. Ride Angels Flight up to Bunker Hill for the afternoon: the Broad, the rooftop garden at Walt Disney Concert Hall and a stroll down through Grand Park to City Hall. If you have time, the Central Library's painted rotunda and gardens are a short walk away and free.

In the evening, head east to the Arts District for dinner and a drink among the warehouses, or check the calendar at the concert hall, the Music Center and Broadway's restored theatres, several of which host films and live shows. Downtown's rooftop terraces are at their best at sunset, when the towers turn gold and the hills behind Hollywood fade to purple.

Eat and drink

Los Angeles eats the way it was built — by immigrant communities — and downtown is a good place to taste the range. Tacos from stands and trucks, Oaxacan and Salvadoran specialities, Japanese noodles and sweets in Little Tokyo, dim sum and bakeries in Chinatown and Korean barbecue a short ride west in Koreatown all sit within easy reach. Grand Central Market is the easiest introduction: you can build a lunch from several stalls and eat at shared counters under the old ceiling.

Eating well cheaply is easy if you follow the lunch crowds. Weekday office workers keep counter-service spots in the Financial District busy, many bars and restaurants run early-evening happy hours, and street vendors — legal with a permit in Los Angeles — sell fruit, tacos and bacon-wrapped hot dogs on busy corners. Save the splurge for the Arts District or the Historic Core, where chef-driven restaurants occupy old industrial and banking spaces, and book ahead on weekends.

Getting around

LA Metro's rail lines converge downtown. The B Line subway runs from Union Station under the Financial District to Hollywood and North Hollywood, and the D Line heads west under Wilshire Boulevard toward Koreatown. The A and E light-rail lines now cross downtown through the Regional Connector tunnel, so you can ride straight from Long Beach to Pasadena, or from East Los Angeles to Santa Monica, without changing. Union Station is also the hub for Metrolink commuter trains and Amtrak, including the coastal service to San Diego. From LAX, the FlyAway bus to Union Station is the simplest option with luggage; rail involves a transfer at the airport transit centre.

Within downtown, walking is the best way to connect the districts, though some links — the Historic Core to the Arts District, or Bunker Hill to South Park — are longer than they look, and Bunker Hill is steep. DASH buses run frequent downtown loops, Metro Bike Share docks are scattered through the core, and the bike-lane network is growing. Driving is straightforward outside rush hour, but the surrounding freeways jam at peak times, and parking garages charge a premium on event nights. Never leave anything visible in a parked car.

Where to stay

Bunker Hill and the Financial District are best for business travellers and car-free sightseers, with Metro stations and the Grand Avenue museums close by, though the streets go quiet at night. The Historic Core suits travellers who want character, rooftop bars and restaurants in converted buildings. South Park is the practical choice for the convention centre and arena events, and it is lively when something is on. The Arts District has design-led stays and the best dining but thinner transit, while Little Tokyo offers good value and a direct rail link. If your trip is mostly beaches or Hollywood, staying there and visiting downtown by rail may make more sense.

Living downtown

Downtown's housing stock is a mix of loft conversions in historic office and bank buildings, amenity-heavy rental towers in South Park and Bunker Hill, and live/work units in former warehouses in the Arts District. Houses are almost non-existent, and condominiums are a smaller share of the market than rentals. Supermarkets, gyms and dog parks have followed the residents, but some blocks still feel unfinished, and a few streets east of the Historic Core are dominated by homelessness services and encampments.

Leases usually run for 12 months, with credit and income checks at application. California limits security deposits to one month's rent for most landlords. Many apartments in older buildings fall under the city's Rent Stabilization Ordinance, and a statewide cap on annual increases covers many other units more than 15 years old, though the newest towers generally sit outside both. Parking is often rented separately. Downtown suits young professionals, people who work in the core or along the rail lines, and anyone who prefers a car-light life; households with children usually weigh schools and green space carefully.

Doing business downtown

The Financial District and Bunker Hill form the main office core, home to law firms, accounting and banking, and to some of the tallest towers on the West Coast. Next door, the Civic Center concentrates city, county, state and federal government offices and courts, which keeps legal services and nonprofits nearby. The Arts District has become a base for creative agencies, media and design firms in converted industrial space, while the Fashion District remains a major wholesale hub for apparel and textiles, alongside the wholesale flower, jewellery and toy districts.

Retail is reviving block by block. Broadway has drawn flagship and concept stores into restored theatres and department-store buildings, the Arts District attracts destination shoppers and diners, and Little Tokyo and Chinatown support long-standing independent businesses. Footfall is strongest at weekday lunch in the towers, on weekends in the Arts District and Historic Core, and on event nights in South Park. Businesses operating in the city need a Business Tax Registration Certificate from the city's Office of Finance. See Office and commercial space in downtown Los Angeles.

Dates move from year to year. Check the organiser's official site before booking travel.

Seasons and weather

Los Angeles has a Mediterranean climate, and downtown, a few kilometres inland, runs warmer than the coast. Winter brings mild days, cool evenings and most of the year's rain in short, heavy bursts that can flood underpasses. Spring is pleasant, with purple jacaranda trees blooming along streets in late spring, but May and June mornings are often grey under a marine layer that burns off by afternoon. Summer is hot and dry, and some of the hottest spells come in September and October, when dry Santa Ana winds can also push wildfire smoke across the basin. Pack layers year-round, sunscreen and a refillable water bottle, and something warmer for evenings in winter.

Safety and practicalities

Most visitors have trouble-free trips, but downtown asks for street sense. Homelessness is visible across the core and concentrated on Skid Row, east of the Historic Core, so plan walking routes accordingly and stick to busier streets after dark. Car break-ins are common; leave nothing in view. Metro rail runs until around midnight, and rideshare is the usual choice late at night. In summer heat, carry water and use shaded routes. Metro stations have lifts, though Bunker Hill's slopes are steep for wheelchairs and strollers. California's earthquake early-warning alerts reach phones automatically; if you feel shaking, drop, cover and hold on. Mobile coverage is strong, and the Central Library offers free Wi-Fi.

Local tips

  • Use the Regional Connector: a single A or E Line ride links Little Tokyo, the Historic Core and Bunker Hill.
  • Reserve free timed entry to the Broad a few weeks ahead for weekend visits.
  • Look above Broadway's shopfronts — many original theatre façades and vertical signs survive above modern ground floors.
  • Check the convention centre and arena calendars before booking: big events change traffic, parking and hotel prices in South Park.
  • The Central Library's rotunda and gardens are some of downtown's finest free interiors and a cool escape on hot afternoons.
