In 30 seconds

  • Best for: founding-era history, serious food at fair prices and a downtown you can cross on foot
  • Center City runs river to river on William Penn's original grid, so getting lost is hard
  • Bring a bottle: tight liquor licensing has made BYOB restaurants a local institution

Philadelphia's downtown is called Center City, and its borders are unusually tidy: the Delaware River to the east, the Schuylkill River to the west, Vine Street to the north and South Street to the south. Inside sits the grid William Penn planned in 1682, with City Hall on the central square and four more public squares — Rittenhouse, Washington, Logan and Franklin — anchoring each quarter. Old City, on the Delaware side, holds Independence Hall and the colonial lanes; the high-rise office core spreads west of City Hall along Market Street and John F. Kennedy Boulevard.

What makes it work is density at a human scale. Brick rowhouse blocks run right up to the office towers, so Center City has a large residential population that keeps the streets busy in the evenings and at weekends, not just in office hours. It is also one of the few big East Coast downtowns where renting a flat, eating out and opening a shop still feel within reach, and it sits on the Northeast Corridor rail line between New York and Washington. The trade-offs are ageing transit, uneven upkeep once you leave the core, and summers that can be properly sticky.

The districts

Old City and Society HillIndependence National Historical Park, cobbled lanes and gallery-lined streets; Society Hill, just south, holds one of the country's largest concentrations of 18th-century houses.
Rittenhouse SquareThe most polished quarter: a leafy square ringed by apartment towers, with Walnut Street's flagship shops and a thick web of restaurants on the side streets.
Market East and Midtown VillageThe convention centre, Reading Terminal Market and Jefferson Station, with the bars of 13th Street and the rainbow street signs of the Gayborhood just to the south.
ChinatownA compact, busy neighbourhood north of Market Street around the Friendship Gate on 10th Street, best for noodles, dumplings and late dinners.
Logan Square and the ParkwayThe Benjamin Franklin Parkway, a diagonal boulevard of museums, flags and fountains running from City Hall to the Philadelphia Museum of Art.
Penn's LandingThe Delaware riverfront of piers, seasonal pop-up parks and a winter skating rink, reached from Old City by bridges over Interstate 95.

Landmarks worth your time

  • Independence Hall and the Liberty Bell. Where the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution were debated and signed; the bell sits in its own glass pavilion across the lawn. Timed tickets are needed for the hall for much of the year.
  • City Hall. A vast Second Empire pile crowned by a bronze statue of William Penn; tower tours, when running, give the best view down Broad Street, and Dilworth Park on its west side has a fountain in summer and a rink in winter.
  • Reading Terminal Market. A public market under an 1890s train shed, packed with produce stalls, Pennsylvania Dutch bakers and lunch counters.
  • Philadelphia Museum of Art. One of the country's great encyclopaedic collections, at the top of the steps made famous by the Rocky films; the view back down the Parkway is free.
  • Barnes Foundation. An extraordinary collection of Impressionist, Post-Impressionist and early modern paintings, hung in the founder's idiosyncratic wall arrangements.
  • Elfreth's Alley. Often described as the oldest continuously inhabited residential street in the country; a short, narrow lane of 18th-century houses that people still live in, so keep your voice down.
  • Museum of the American Revolution. A modern, well-paced museum near Independence Hall that tells the war's story through objects, rooms and a theatre built around George Washington's field tent.
  • Schuylkill River Trail. A riverside path and boardwalk on the western edge of Center City, busy with runners and cyclists and linked to the art museum and Boathouse Row.

A perfect day downtown

Start with breakfast at Reading Terminal Market before the lunch crowds arrive, then walk east along Market or Chestnut Street to Independence National Historical Park. See the Liberty Bell first, use your timed slot for Independence Hall, then wander through Elfreth's Alley and past Christ Church Burial Ground, where visitors leave pennies on Benjamin Franklin's grave.

In the afternoon, head west to City Hall and, if tours are running, ride up to the observation deck below the Penn statue. Continue up the Benjamin Franklin Parkway to the Barnes Foundation or the Philadelphia Museum of Art, run the steps if you must, and then drop down to the Schuylkill River Trail for a riverside walk in good weather.

As evening comes, make for Rittenhouse Square while the benches fill up, then eat in Rittenhouse, Midtown Village or Chinatown — with a bottle under your arm if you have chosen a BYOB. Finish with a concert or a play on the Avenue of the Arts, the stretch of Broad Street south of City Hall lined with concert halls and theatres.

Eat and drink

Philadelphia's food reputation starts with sandwiches and does not end there. The cheesesteak is the famous one, but many locals would steer you to a roast pork sandwich with sharp provolone and greens, a well-built hoagie, a figure-eight soft pretzel or a cup of water ice on a hot day. Reading Terminal Market is the easiest place to try several in one go, with Pennsylvania Dutch stalls selling pastries, scrapple and pies alongside lunch counters of every kind. Chinatown adds hand-pulled noodles, dim sum and hot pot, and the Italian Market just south of Center City is worth the walk for its old-school grocers.

The city's other speciality is the neighbourhood restaurant. Pennsylvania tightly limits liquor licences, so many of Center City's best small dining rooms are BYOB: you bring the wine and they open it, sometimes for a small corkage fee. That keeps dinner bills down. Wine and spirits are sold in state-run shops and some supermarkets, so plan ahead. To eat well cheaply, stick to markets, Chinatown, food trucks around the universities and happy hours in Midtown Village and Rittenhouse.

Getting around

SEPTA runs the city's transit. The Market–Frankford Line (the L) runs east–west under Market Street and the Broad Street Line (the B) runs north–south under Broad Street; they meet beneath City Hall. Trolleys run through a tunnel under Market Street to University City, and the PATCO Speedline, run by the Delaware River Port Authority, links stations along Locust Street and at 8th and Market to New Jersey. Every Regional Rail line stops at Jefferson, Suburban and 30th Street stations, and the Airport Line reaches the terminals at Philadelphia International. Amtrak trains to New York and Washington leave from 30th Street Station, just across the Schuylkill.

On the ground, Center City is flat and compact, and walking is the default. Indego bike share docks are spread across the core, and Spruce and Pine Streets carry dedicated bike lanes. Driving is possible but rarely pleasant: the grid is dense with one-way streets, garage parking is costly, and the city's parking enforcement has a fierce and well-earned reputation. If you arrive by car, park once and leave it.

Where to stay

First-time visitors do well around Rittenhouse Square or the Broad Street corridor near City Hall, which put museums, shopping and Old City within a walk and both subway lines on your doorstep. History-focused trips suit Old City and Society Hill, quieter after dark but steps from Independence Hall and the waterfront. Convention-goers usually base themselves in Market East near the convention centre and Reading Terminal Market. Museum lovers and runners will like Logan Square and the Parkway, closest to the Schuylkill River Trail. If you are travelling by train, University City, near 30th Street Station, is practical and has a lively student scene.

Living downtown

Center City housing ranges from brick rowhouses and tiny three-storey "trinity" houses — one room per floor, linked by a winding stair — to converted factory lofts in Old City and Callowhill and high-rise apartments around Rittenhouse and Logan Square. Leases usually run for 12 months. Pennsylvania caps security deposits at two months' rent in the first year of a tenancy, and Philadelphia requires landlords to hold a rental licence and give tenants a certificate of rental suitability before they move in.

Downtown suits people who want to walk to work, eat out often and skip car ownership, and it attracts graduates of the city's many universities, medical staff from the big hospitals and New York and Washington commuters who use the Northeast Corridor. Two things to weigh: the city wage tax applies to residents' earnings (and to non-residents who work in the city, at a lower rate), and public school quality varies widely, so families should research catchments before choosing a block.

Doing business downtown

The main office core is Market Street West, the blocks of towers between City Hall and the Schuylkill, home to law firms, insurers, banks, consultancies and corporate headquarters. Across the river, University City combines two major universities, a large academic medical centre and a fast-growing cluster of life-science labs, with cell and gene therapy as a particular strength. Healthcare and higher education are the region's biggest employers, and the Navy Yard in South Philadelphia offers a campus-style alternative. Philadelphia's own taxes — a business income and receipts tax, the wage tax and a use and occupancy tax on commercial space — are a real factor when firms compare the city with its suburbs.

For retail, Walnut Street between Broad Street and Rittenhouse Square carries the flagship and luxury tenants, Chestnut Street mixes everyday chains and services, and Market East is being reshaped around the convention trade and new housing. Old City and South Street favour independents, galleries and nightlife. See Office and commercial space in downtown Philadelphia for districts, lease conventions and what to check before you sign.

Signature events

JanuaryMummers Parade — String bands, comic brigades and feathered costumes parade on New Year's Day in a tradition that goes back more than a century.
Early MarchPhiladelphia Flower Show — One of the world's longest-running horticultural shows, usually held in the Pennsylvania Convention Center.
Late June–early JulyWelcome America — A multi-day Independence Day festival of free events that builds to a concert and fireworks on the Parkway.
NovemberPhiladelphia Marathon — A weekend of races that starts and finishes on the Parkway near the art museum.

Dates move from year to year. Check the organiser's official site before booking travel.

Seasons and weather

Spring is the city at its best: blossom in the squares, outdoor tables reopening and mild days for walking, though April can still be cool and wet. Summer is hot and humid, with thunderstorms on many afternoons; pack light clothing, sunscreen and a layer for heavily air-conditioned museums. Autumn brings crisp, clear weather and is the most reliable season for walking tours. Winters are cold and grey, with occasional snow that slows the city for a day or two; bring a warm coat, waterproof shoes and gloves, and expect icy brick pavements in Old City.

Safety and practicalities

Center City is busy, well lit and generally comfortable to walk, especially around Rittenhouse, Old City and the Parkway. Use ordinary big-city care: watch your bag in crowds at Independence Mall and on busy trains, and avoid empty platforms and side streets late at night, when a rideshare is the easier option. Some outlying stretches of the subway lines feel rougher than the core, so stay alert if you ride beyond Center City. Old City's brick and cobbled pavements are uneven for wheelchairs and strollers, and not every older subway station has a working lift, so check SEPTA's accessibility information before you ride. Summer heat is the main physical hazard — carry water. Mobile coverage is strong throughout downtown.

Local tips

  • Reserve Independence Hall tickets in advance from spring to early winter; same-day tickets can run out by mid-morning.
  • Check whether a restaurant is BYOB when you book, and buy wine before you set off.
  • Many of the Pennsylvania Dutch stalls at Reading Terminal Market close on Sundays, and some on Mondays, so go midweek or on Saturday for the full experience.
  • Take the Regional Rail Airport Line instead of a taxi at rush hour; it avoids the expressway traffic.
  • Old City's galleries open late on the first Friday of each month.
  • Feed the meter or use the city's parking app — tickets come quickly.
