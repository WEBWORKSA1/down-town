In 30 seconds

  • Best for: food lovers, cyclists and walkers who like a compact, green, low-key downtown
  • Tap a contactless card or phone on MAX, buses and the streetcar; fares cap automatically

Downtown Portland sits on the west bank of the Willamette River, a tight grid of famously short blocks, just 200 feet on a side, running from Tom McCall Waterfront Park up to the foot of the forested West Hills. Pioneer Courthouse Square is the centre of gravity, with the transit mall on 5th and 6th avenues and the green ribbon of the Park Blocks a few streets west. North of Burnside Street, Old Town Chinatown gives way to the Pearl District, a former rail yard and warehouse quarter turned galleries, lofts and parks. To the south, Portland State University's campus leads to the towers of South Waterfront, and a string of bridges links the west side with the Lloyd District and the Central Eastside across the river.

Portland works because it is small, green and easy to get around. Decades of planning traded a riverside freeway for a waterfront park and built light rail instead of new motorways, and the result is a downtown you can cross on foot in half an hour, with coffee roasters, food carts and bookshops along the way. It has also had a hard few years: office workers have been slow to return, some storefronts are empty, and homelessness and addiction are visible on certain streets, particularly in Old Town. The recovery is uneven, with the Pearl, the waterfront and the east-side food streets busy while parts of the retail core remain quiet, but the bones of a great walking city are all still there.

Landmarks worth your time

  • Pioneer Courthouse Square. The brick-paved "living room" of the city, with seasonal events, a giant holiday tree and the Weather Machine sculpture, which gives a small forecast performance at noon.
  • Tom McCall Waterfront Park. A riverside promenade built where a freeway once ran. Walk south, cross the Hawthorne Bridge, return along the Eastbank Esplanade and cross back on the Steel Bridge for the classic loop; cherry trees bloom at the park's north end in early spring.
  • Lan Su Chinese Garden. A walled Suzhou-style garden built by artisans from Portland's sister city, with a teahouse overlooking the lake; peaceful even in the rain.
  • Portland Art Museum. The region's major art museum, on the South Park Blocks, reopened in late 2025 with the glass Mark Rothko Pavilion linking its buildings. Fittingly, Rothko grew up in Portland.
  • Portland Aerial Tram. A short cable-car ride from South Waterfront up to Oregon Health & Science University, with views over the river and, on clear days, Mount Hood.
  • Washington Park. A short MAX ride west for the Oregon Zoo and arboretum trails, with the free International Rose Test Garden and the Portland Japanese Garden a walk or seasonal shuttle away.
  • Union Station. The 1896 railway station with its "Go By Train" neon sign, still served by Amtrak trains to Seattle, Vancouver, California and Chicago.
  • Forest Park. A huge, wild park in the West Hills, threaded by the long Wildwood Trail; trailheads are a short bus or taxi ride from the Pearl.

A perfect day downtown

Morning: start with coffee, which Portland takes seriously, then walk Waterfront Park and the bridges loop while the city wakes up. On Saturdays, choose between the Portland Farmers Market at Portland State University and the Saturday Market's craft stalls by the river. Lan Su Chinese Garden opens mid-morning and makes a calm hour in any weather.

Afternoon: walk or take the streetcar to the Pearl District for galleries and design shops, then ride MAX west to Washington Park for the Rose Test Garden, at its peak in June, and the Japanese Garden. Alternatively, take the streetcar south to South Waterfront and ride the aerial tram for the view, then spend an hour in the Portland Art Museum on the way back.

Evening: cross the river for dinner, since the Central Eastside and the food streets of Division and Hawthorne are a short ride away, or stay downtown for happy hour, a food-cart supper and a concert at the Arlene Schnitzer Concert Hall or a show at Keller Auditorium. In summer the sun sets late enough for one more riverside stroll.

Eat and drink

Portland's food scene runs on Pacific Northwest abundance: Dungeness crab, salmon and oysters from the coast, mushrooms and berries from the forests, and hazelnuts, marionberries and pinot noir from the Willamette Valley, all within a couple of hours' drive. That bounty feeds a dense mix of chef-owned restaurants, bakeries, coffee roasters, craft breweries, cideries and distilleries, along with an unusually strong showing of Thai, Vietnamese, Mexican and Korean cooking. Food carts are a Portland institution, gathered into "pods" on car parks downtown and across the east side, and they are where many local chefs got their start.

To eat well cheaply, follow the happy hours, which are a civic habit here and often run in the late afternoon and again late at night; graze at the cart pods; and shop the Portland Farmers Market at Portland State on Saturdays, which runs all year with shorter winter hours. With no sales tax, menu prices are what you pay before the tip, though some restaurants now add a service charge in place of tipping, so read the bill. Downtown's dining is strongest in the Pearl District and the West End, but the east side, across any of the central bridges, is where many locals go out.

Getting around

TriMet runs the MAX light rail and buses, and the Portland Streetcar runs in partnership with the city. All five MAX lines, Blue, Green, Orange, Red and Yellow, pass through downtown, and the Red Line runs from downtown and the Lloyd District straight to Portland International Airport, an easy ride with luggage. The streetcar loops through the Pearl District, downtown, Portland State and South Waterfront, and crosses the river via the Broadway Bridge and Tilikum Crossing. Pay by tapping a Hop card, a phone or a contactless bank card on the readers; a single fare lasts two and a half hours, and once you have spent the price of a day pass, further rides that day are free. Amtrak serves Union Station, on the edge of the Pearl.

On foot, downtown's 200-foot blocks make walking quick and pleasant, and the bridges are part of the experience: the Steel, Hawthorne and Tilikum crossings all have generous paths. Cycling is a mainstream way to get around, with protected lanes, quiet bike boulevards on the east side and the city's orange bike-share bikes and e-bikes available by the minute. Driving downtown is manageable but slow, with one-way streets, bus lanes and metered parking; the city-run garages are a straightforward option. Leave valuables out of sight in any parked car.

Where to stay

Downtown near Pioneer Courthouse Square is the most convenient base for a first visit, with every MAX line, the streetcar and the main sights close by. The Pearl District is calmer and more upmarket, with restaurants and galleries on the doorstep and an easy walk to Union Station. The riverfront and Old Town put you near Waterfront Park and the nightlife, but some blocks feel rough after dark, so check the exact location before booking. The Lloyd District suits conventions and arena events and has a direct MAX ride to the airport, while short-term rentals in close-in east-side neighbourhoods such as Hawthorne, Division or Alberta trade downtown convenience for local character.

Living downtown

Downtown and its neighbours offer a wide spread of housing: warehouse lofts and newer condos in the Pearl, towers with river and mountain views in South Waterfront, older brick apartment buildings in the West End and Goose Hollow, and student housing around Portland State. Northwest Portland's Nob Hill area, just beyond the Pearl, adds Victorian houses and leafy streets within walking distance of the core. Portland is cheaper than Seattle or San Francisco but no longer a bargain, and Oregon's income tax, plus regional and county taxes on higher earners, offsets the lack of sales tax.

Leases typically run 12 months, with a security deposit and screening fees. Oregon was the first US state to adopt statewide rent stabilisation: it caps annual increases on most buildings more than 15 years old and requires 90 days' written notice of a rise. Portland adds its own rules on screening, deposits and relocation payments when landlords end tenancies without cause or impose large increases. Downtown suits car-free professionals, students, healthcare workers at OHSU and empty-nesters who want culture on foot. Families tend to look at the east side or the western suburbs for larger homes and schools, and anyone sensitive to grey skies should try a Portland winter before signing.

Doing business downtown

Downtown is the region's centre for law, finance, government, consulting and the regional offices of national firms, with city, county and federal buildings clustered around the Park Blocks and Chapman and Lownsdale squares. The Pearl District and Central Eastside house the creative economy Portland is known for: design and advertising agencies, software studios, apparel and footwear designers, and food and drink producers. Oregon Health & Science University anchors a growing health and life-sciences cluster in South Waterfront. The metro's largest private employers, in athletic apparel and semiconductors, are mostly in the western suburbs, which shapes where many professionals live and commute.

Since 2020, downtown's office market has struggled to recover. Vacancy is high, which gives tenants real bargaining power and has prompted landlords to upgrade, reposition or convert older towers. Retail is strongest in the Pearl District, along NW 23rd Avenue and on the east side's main streets; the blocks around Pioneer Courthouse Square depend on office workers and visitors and have more empty units. Oregon has no sales tax, but businesses pay city, county and regional business income taxes, so budget with an accountant. Seasons and weather

Portland's reputation for rain is earned from October to May, when skies are often grey and drizzle comes and goes, but temperatures are mild and heavy downpours are less common than visitors expect. Snow is rare; when it does fall, or when freezing rain coats the hills, the city can grind to a halt for a day or two. Spring is a slow, lovely bloom, with cherry trees along the waterfront in March and April and roses from late May, and the weather changes by the hour. July to September is the reward: dry, sunny and warm, with long evenings. Heatwaves do happen, many older apartments lack air conditioning, and in some late summers smoke from regional wildfires hazes the sky. Pack a hooded waterproof jacket (locals rarely bother with umbrellas), layers and comfortable shoes, plus sunglasses for summer.

Safety and practicalities

Downtown Portland is busy and generally safe during the day, but it has visible homelessness, open drug use on some blocks and more property crime than many visitors expect. Car break-ins are the most common problem, so leave nothing in view, even in garages. Old Town and parts of the transit mall feel rougher at night: stick to busier streets, use ride-hailing late if you are unsure, and sit near the front of the train on MAX after dark if you prefer company. Oregon reversed its drug-decriminalisation experiment in 2024, and the downtown business improvement district funds extra cleaning crews and safety ambassadors. MAX and the streetcar have level boarding, though the West Hills are steep. Summer heat and wildfire smoke can affect vulnerable travellers, so check air-quality alerts in late summer. Mobile coverage is strong across the centre.

Local tips

  • There is no sales tax, but some restaurants add a service charge; check before tipping on top.
  • Tap the same card or phone every time you ride so your fares cap automatically.
  • Walk the bridges loop: Waterfront Park, the Hawthorne Bridge, the Eastbank Esplanade and back over the Steel Bridge.
  • The Portland Farmers Market at Portland State runs every Saturday, all year round.
  • Carry a waterproof layer from October to May; the weather can change in minutes.
  • The Columbia River Gorge, Mount Hood and the coast make easy day trips by car; leave early on summer weekends.
