In 30 seconds

  • Best for: outdoor lovers who want city life with mountains and beaches in reach
  • The seawall is the city's main promenade — walk or cycle a section on day one
  • Rain is common October–April; summers are dry and mild

Downtown Vancouver sits on a peninsula with water on three sides and mountains across the harbour. Urban planners study it for a reason: slender residential towers on low podiums put tens of thousands of people within walking distance of offices, shops and the seawall. The peninsula is bounded by Burrard Inlet and Coal Harbour to the north, False Creek to the south and English Bay and Stanley Park to the west, with Gastown and Chinatown marking its older eastern end.

The result is a downtown where living is the main use. In the evening, the West End, Yaletown and Coal Harbour fill with residents running, cycling and eating out, and Stanley Park offers old-growth forest at the end of the street grid. The flip side is cost — this is one of the most expensive places to live in North America — and the stark contrast of the Downtown Eastside, where poverty and addiction are visible a few blocks from the waterfront towers.

Landmarks worth your time

  • Stanley Park and the seawall. A large urban park with forest trails; the seawall loop around it is about 9 km.
  • Canada Place. The sail-roofed cruise and convention terminal, with harbour views to the North Shore mountains.
  • Gastown Steam Clock. A steam-powered clock that whistles on the quarter hour.
  • Granville Island. A public market and arts district a short ferry ride across False Creek.
  • Vancouver Art Gallery. The city's main art museum, facing a popular public square.
  • English Bay. A downtown beach with sunset views and summer fireworks.
  • Dr. Sun Yat-Sen Classical Chinese Garden. A walled Ming-style scholar's garden of pavilions, rock and water in Chinatown, with a free public park beside it.
  • Jack Poole Plaza. The waterfront plaza beside the convention centre, home to the 2010 Winter Olympic cauldron and the best close-up view of the seaplanes.

A perfect day downtown

Morning: rent a bike and ride the seawall around Stanley Park, counter-clockwise as the rules require, stopping at the totem poles at Brockton Point and the beaches on the western shore. Return along English Bay to the West End for brunch.

Afternoon: walk through the downtown core along Robson Street to the Vancouver Art Gallery, then east to Gastown's cobbled streets and the steam clock. Continue into Chinatown for the classical garden, then catch one of the small ferries across False Creek to Granville Island for the public market.

Evening: ferry back to Yaletown for dinner by the marina, or head to English Bay to watch the sun set over the water. In summer, a stroll along the Coal Harbour waterfront at dusk is hard to beat.

Eat and drink

Vancouver is known for seafood, sushi and Asian cuisines, with Chinatown on the eastern edge of downtown and Richmond's food scene a SkyTrain ride away. Granville Island's market is the best stop for picnic supplies. Downtown has a strong brunch and café culture to match its residential population.

Pacific salmon, spot prawns in late spring and Dungeness crab turn up on menus at every price, and the city's Japanese, Chinese, Korean, Vietnamese and Indian kitchens are among the best in North America. To eat well cheaply, look for lunch specials on Robson and Denman streets, food trucks in the downtown core, noodle and dumpling houses, and izakaya-style happy hours. Craft breweries cluster in the light-industrial streets just east of downtown, and in summer the Granville Island and Yaletown patios fill early.

Getting around

SkyTrain's Expo, Millennium and Canada lines serve downtown, and the Canada Line reaches the airport in about 25 minutes. The SeaBus crosses the harbour to North Vancouver from Waterfront Station, and small ferries hop across False Creek. Mobi bike share and protected bike lanes make cycling an easy choice.

TransLink runs the network. On weekdays SkyTrain and SeaBus fares depend on how many zones you cross, while buses charge a single flat fare; a surcharge applies when you board at the airport stations. Tap in and out at SkyTrain and SeaBus gates, but only tap in on buses. The West Coast Express commuter train runs on weekdays between Waterfront and the Fraser Valley, and a SkyTrain extension under Broadway is under construction. Driving downtown is slow and parking is expensive: no freeway runs into the core, and the bridges and the Stanley Park causeway back up at rush hour.

Where to stay

Coal Harbour and the Waterfront are the obvious base for cruise passengers, convention visitors and business travellers, with Canada Place and Waterfront Station on the doorstep. The downtown core around Robson and Granville streets is best for shopping and transit. Yaletown suits food-focused trips and puts the False Creek seawall at the end of the block. The West End, near English Bay and Stanley Park, feels residential and is often better value. Gastown has the most character, but it borders the Downtown Eastside, so check exactly where a place sits before you book.

Living downtown

Housing is predominantly condo towers, with older rental buildings in the West End. Vancouver is one of Canada's most expensive housing markets, so expect small units and high rents. Many buildings restrict short-term rentals, and the city enforces licensing rules for them.

Residential tenancies in British Columbia fall under the Residential Tenancy Act. Security deposits are capped at half a month's rent, with a separate pet deposit of up to the same amount, and rent can rise only once a year, by no more than a provincially set maximum and with three months' written notice — though rents can reset between tenants. Disputes go to the Residential Tenancy Branch. Owners face the city's empty homes tax and the province's speculation and vacancy tax if a home sits unoccupied. Downtown suits professionals, couples and downsizers who trade space for the seawall, beaches and short walks to work.

Doing business downtown

Tourism, film and television production, technology and the port drive the economy. Robson Street is the main retail corridor, while Gastown suits independent and design-led brands. The cruise season brings large visitor flows between spring and autumn.

The financial district runs along Burrard Street, West Georgia and West Hastings, home to banks, law firms and the head offices of many of the world's mining and resource-exploration companies. The Port of Vancouver, Canada's largest, makes the city the country's main gateway for trade with Asia, and visual effects, animation and game studios fill converted warehouses in Gastown, Yaletown and Mount Pleasant. The Broadway corridor south of False Creek is the region's second office hub. Read our guide to office and commercial space in downtown Vancouver.

Late July–early AugCelebration of Light — An international fireworks competition over English Bay.

Sept–OctVancouver International Film Festival — A major film festival with screenings across downtown.

Dates move from year to year. Check the organiser's official site before booking travel.

Seasons and weather

Vancouver has the mildest winters of any big Canadian city, but they are wet: from October to April, grey skies and steady rain are the norm, and the mountains above the city collect deep snow. Snow downtown is rare, and when it does fall the hills and bridges snarl quickly. Spring starts early, with cherry blossoms lining streets from March into April. Summers are dry, sunny and comfortably warm, though wildfire smoke can drift in during some seasons. Pack a proper waterproof shell and shoes that can handle puddles for most of the year, and layers for cool summer evenings on the water.

Safety and practicalities

Downtown Vancouver is generally safe for visitors, but the Downtown Eastside, centred on East Hastings Street between Gastown and Chinatown, has a concentration of homelessness, public drug use and mental illness; it is a community in a public-health crisis rather than a sight, so be aware, be respectful and don't take photos of people. Car break-ins and bike theft are common, so never leave anything visible in a parked car and lock bikes securely. The seawall separates cyclists from walkers — keep to your lane. SkyTrain stations have elevators, and the seawall is flat and step-free. The region is earthquake country, so it is worth reading the emergency information in your building or hotel.

Local tips

  • Rent a bike and ride the full Stanley Park seawall counter-clockwise, as required.
  • Take the SeaBus for a cheap harbour cruise.
  • Pack a rain shell — the weather turns quickly outside summer.
  • Prices exclude GST and British Columbia's PST, so expect a higher total at the till.
  • Cross to the North Shore on a clear day for the reverse view of the downtown skyline from the North Vancouver waterfront by the SeaBus terminal.
