In 30 seconds

  • Best for: literary history, Georgian squares, free national museums and a pub culture built on conversation and music
  • Get a TFI Leap Card on day one: it beats cash fares, and buses don't give change
  • Book the Book of Kells and Kilmainham Gaol before you travel; both sell out
  • Pack a hooded waterproof in every season

Downtown Dublin is the city centre on both banks of the River Liffey, roughly the area held between the Royal Canal to the north and the Grand Canal to the south. The south side holds Trinity College, Grafton Street, St Stephen's Green, the Georgian squares and Temple Bar. The north side has O'Connell Street, the main shopping streets around Henry Street, Parnell Square and the old market district of Smithfield. To the east, where the river widens toward the port, the Docklands have grown into a second centre of glass offices.

What makes it work is scale. Most of what a visitor wants is within a half-hour walk of the Ha'penny Bridge, the national museums and gallery are free, and the pubs remain genuinely social places rather than just bars. What makes it harder is that Dublin has no metro, buses get stuck in traffic, and accommodation and rents are expensive by any European standard. Come for the walking, the talk and the history, and budget accordingly.

The districts

Grafton Street and St Stephen's GreenThe pedestrianised spine of the south side, running from Trinity College to the city's best-loved park, with buskers, flagship shops and side streets full of cafés and pubs.
Temple BarA cobbled quarter between Dame Street and the river: crowded pubs and live music at night, and by day small galleries, the Irish Film Institute and a weekend food market in Meeting House Square.
Georgian DublinMerrion Square, Fitzwilliam Square and the streets around them: brick terraces with fanlit doors, Leinster House (the seat of the Oireachtas, Ireland's parliament), the National Gallery and the national museums. Much of it is now offices.
O'Connell Street and the north inner cityThe city's widest street, with the GPO and the Spire, leading up to Parnell Square. Henry Street is the north side's busiest shopping street, and Moore Street keeps its fruit and vegetable traders.
The Liberties and Christ ChurchThe medieval city west of Dublin Castle: two cathedrals, brewing and distilling heritage, antique dealers on Francis Street and a neighbourhood changing fast as new offices and apartments arrive.
DocklandsGlass offices on both quays where the Liffey widens: the financial services centre on the north bank and Grand Canal Dock, nicknamed "Silicon Docks", on the south, linked by the harp-shaped Samuel Beckett Bridge.

Landmarks worth your time

  • Trinity College and the Book of Kells. The university's cobbled squares are free to walk through. The illuminated gospel book, made around 800 AD, and the Old Library's Long Room need a timed ticket, and the library is partway through a long conservation project.
  • Dublin Castle and the Chester Beatty. The castle's State Apartments are open for tours when not in official use. The Chester Beatty in the castle gardens is a free museum of manuscripts, prints and rare books from across Asia, the Middle East and Europe.
  • Christ Church and St Patrick's cathedrals. Two medieval cathedrals a few minutes apart; Jonathan Swift was dean of St Patrick's and is buried there. Both charge admission for sightseeing.
  • National Museum of Ireland – Archaeology. Free, on Kildare Street: prehistoric gold, the Ardagh Chalice and Tara Brooch, and startlingly preserved Iron Age bog bodies.
  • National Gallery of Ireland. Free, on Merrion Square, with Irish and European art across several centuries, including Caravaggio's The Taking of Christ and a strong Jack B. Yeats collection.
  • Ha'penny Bridge and the quays. The cast-iron footbridge of 1816 is named after the toll once charged to cross it. Walk the quays either way for the city's best long views.
  • The GPO. The General Post Office on O'Connell Street was the rebels' headquarters during the 1916 Easter Rising. It is still a working post office, with an exhibition on the Rising inside.
  • Kilmainham Gaol. A short bus ride west, the former prison where leaders of the Rising were executed. Entry is by guided tour only, and tickets go well in advance.

A perfect day downtown

Morning. Start at Trinity College as it opens, with a pre-booked slot for the Book of Kells, then walk up Grafton Street before the shopping crowds arrive and loop through St Stephen's Green. Head east along the Georgian streets to Merrion Square for the coloured doors and the Oscar Wilde statue, and give an hour to either the National Gallery or the archaeology museum on Kildare Street. Both are free, so you can leave when your attention runs out.

Afternoon. Eat lunch in the lanes around George's Street, then walk to Dublin Castle and the Chester Beatty and on to Christ Church. Cross the river by the Ha'penny Bridge, follow the quays to O'Connell Street and the GPO, and continue up to Parnell Square for the Garden of Remembrance and the free Hugh Lane Gallery, which houses Francis Bacon's reconstructed studio. If you booked ahead, the tenement museum at 14 Henrietta Street is a few minutes away.

Evening. Many restaurants offer early-evening set menus that are much cheaper than ordering later. Afterwards, find a pub with a traditional music session. Temple Bar has the most, but sessions also run in pubs on the north side and around the Liberties, usually with fewer tourists. For theatre, the Abbey on Abbey Street is Ireland's national theatre.

Eat and drink

Dublin's cooking leans on Irish produce: grass-fed beef and lamb, butter and cheese, and seafood from Dublin Bay and the west coast. Try oysters and chowder, brown soda bread, a full Irish breakfast with black and white pudding, and coddle, a slow-cooked Dublin dish of sausages, bacon and potatoes. The pint of stout is a ritual; order at the bar, and if your group is buying rounds, take your turn.

For browsing and cheap eating, start with the Victorian George's Street Arcade, the Saturday food market in Temple Bar's Meeting House Square, and Capel Street on the north side, pedestrianised and lined with independent restaurants from across Asia and beyond. Moore Street's stalls sell fruit and veg alongside newer international grocers. Fish and chips from a chipper, pub lunches of soup and a sandwich, and early-evening set menus are the classic ways to eat well without paying Dublin's dinner prices. Most pubs close before midnight on weeknights and a little later at weekends.

Getting around

Public transport runs under the Transport for Ireland (TFI) banner. The Luas light rail has two lines: the Red line runs east–west along the north quays, linking Connolly and Heuston stations with the Docklands, and the Green line runs north–south through O'Connell Street, past Trinity College and on to St Stephen's Green and the southern suburbs. The DART follows the coast from Howth and Malahide in the north to Greystones in the south, with central stops at Connolly, Tara Street and Pearse. Dublin Bus and other operators cover everything else, and the network is being redrawn under the BusConnects programme, so route numbers in older guides may be out of date. With a Leap Card, the TFI 90-minute fare lets you transfer between bus, Luas and DART for a single fare.

There is no train from the airport; buses and express coaches run to the centre, and taxis wait outside arrivals. Intercity trains leave from Heuston for Cork, Galway and Limerick, and from Connolly for Belfast and Sligo. The centre is flat, so walking is usually quickest. The city's dublinbikes stations cover the centre and protected cycle lanes are expanding, though traffic can be tight. Driving is not worth it: congestion is heavy, central parking is expensive and limited, and several streets are reserved for buses. Traffic keeps to the left.

Where to stay

First-timers should aim for the south side, between Trinity College, St Stephen's Green and Dublin Castle, where you can walk to nearly everything. Temple Bar puts you in the middle of the action but stays loud late, especially at weekends. The Georgian streets around Merrion Square and Baggot Street are quieter and suit business travellers. The north side, around O'Connell Street, Parnell Square and Smithfield, is often better value and handy for Connolly station, the central bus station and the airport coaches. The Docklands suit conference-goers and anyone working with the tech firms, and leafy Ballsbridge, beyond the Grand Canal, is close to the RDS and the Lansdowne Road stadium. Book early for rugby internationals, big concerts and St Patrick's week.

Living downtown

The centre's housing ranges from Georgian townhouses divided into flats, with high ceilings and draughty sash windows, to Victorian red-brick terraces just beyond the canals and modern apartment blocks along the quays, in the Docklands and around Smithfield. Purpose-built rental and student blocks have added supply, but demand still outstrips it: viewings are crowded, good flats go within days, and you should expect to pay a significant premium for a central address.

Landlords must register tenancies with the Residential Tenancies Board (RTB), which also handles disputes. Deposits and advance rent are each capped at one month's rent, and you will usually be asked for references and proof of income. Rent increases are restricted under Rent Pressure Zone rules, which have been reformed recently, so check the RTB's current guidance before you sign. After six months, tenants gain security of tenure and can only be asked to leave on specified grounds. The centre suits students, young professionals and anyone happy without a car; families tend to look to suburbs on the DART or Luas lines.

Doing business downtown

EU membership, an English-speaking workforce and Ireland's corporate tax regime have made Dublin the European headquarters city for many international technology, pharmaceutical and financial firms. The Georgian core of Dublin 2 is the traditional office district for law firms, accountants, government departments and embassies. The Docklands hold the financial services centre on the north quays, home to funds, banks, insurers and aircraft-leasing firms, and Grand Canal Dock on the south side, where the big tech companies cluster.

Grafton Street and Henry Street are the two prime shopping streets, while Temple Bar, George's Street and Camden Street carry the hospitality trade. Companies are registered with the Companies Registration Office and taxes handled by Revenue; IDA Ireland supports overseas investors, and the Local Enterprise Office helps small firms. Signature events

Mid-MarchSt Patrick's Festival — Several days of music, talks and street events, culminating in the parade through the city centre on St Patrick's Day itself.
Mid-JuneBloomsday — Readings, walks and Edwardian costume tracing the route of James Joyce's Ulysses, which is set on 16 June 1904.
September–OctoberDublin Fringe and Dublin Theatre Festival — Back-to-back festivals of new, experimental and international theatre in venues across the city.
Late OctoberDublin Marathon — A mass-participation marathon through the city's streets and parks, starting and finishing in the centre.

Dates move from year to year. Check the organiser's official site before booking travel.

Seasons and weather

Dublin has a mild, damp maritime climate: rarely very hot, rarely freezing, and changeable within the hour. Summer brings warm rather than hot days and extraordinarily long evenings, with daylight lasting until around 10pm at midsummer. Spring and autumn are fresh and showery, with bright spells that make the parks glow. Winter is grey, windy and dark by late afternoon, but snow and hard frost are uncommon. Whatever the month, pack layers, a hooded waterproof jacket (wind makes umbrellas a struggle) and shoes that can cope with wet pavements.

Safety and practicalities

Dublin is generally safe, and the main risks are petty ones. Pickpockets and bag thieves work the crowds in Temple Bar, Grafton Street and O'Connell Street, and on busy trams. Parts of O'Connell Street and the north quays can feel rowdy late at night, so stick to busier streets and keep your phone out of sight. The Luas runs until around half past midnight, some bus routes run through the night, and licensed taxis can be hailed or booked by app, though ranks get long when the pubs close on Friday and Saturday. Luas trams are step-free, but Georgian buildings and older pubs often have steps at the door. Plugs are the three-pin type used in the UK, and EU roaming rules apply to EU phone plans.

Local tips

  • Buy a Leap Card (the visitor version is sold at the airport) for capped fares and the 90-minute transfer.
  • The national museums, the National Gallery, the Chester Beatty and the Hugh Lane are all free.
  • Order at the bar in pubs; table service is the exception.
  • Look right before you cross: traffic drives on the left.
  • For a cheap sea view, take the DART to Howth or Dalkey and walk the coast.
  • Ask about early-evening set menus when you book dinner.
