In 30 seconds

  • Best for: art, architecture and long walks along the Seine
  • Book major museum tickets online and early
  • Rent control applies to many Paris apartments
  • Say "bonjour" when you walk into any shop or café

In 2020, Paris merged its first four arrondissements into a single district called Paris Centre. It covers the Louvre, Les Halles, the Marais and the two islands in the Seine — the oldest parts of the city. It also takes in the covered passages and the old textile quarter of the Sentier around the Bourse, the former stock exchange.

This is a downtown that has spent the last decade reducing car traffic. Riverbanks once used by expressways are now promenades, and many streets near the Louvre are reserved for bikes, buses and pedestrians. The result is one of the most walkable city centres in the world, dense with monuments, museums, markets and cafés. The trade-offs are crowds at the big sights, tiny and expensive apartments, and prices that reward planning ahead.

The districts

Le MaraisMedieval streets, mansions, galleries and boutiques. Many shops open on Sundays, when the area is at its liveliest.
Les HallesThe commercial heart above a major rail hub, with a vast underground shopping complex beneath a garden and the church of Saint-Eustache alongside.
Île de la Cité and Île Saint-LouisThe islands holding Notre-Dame, Sainte-Chapelle and quiet residential quays.
Palais-Royal and the LouvreFormal gardens, covered arcades and the world's most-visited museum.
Sentier and the BourseThe old textile and press quarter of the 2nd arrondissement, now full of startups, with glass-roofed covered passages and the market street of Rue Montorgueil.
Beaubourg and Hôtel de VilleThe grand city hall on its riverside square and, behind it, the Centre Pompidou, closed for a multi-year renovation, with its piazza and the Stravinsky Fountain.

Landmarks worth your time

  • The Louvre. A former royal palace and one of the world's great museums. Closed on Tuesdays; book a timed entry.
  • Notre-Dame Cathedral. The Gothic cathedral, reopened in December 2024 after restoration. Entry is free, and reserving a time slot online can save queueing.
  • Sainte-Chapelle. A chapel with soaring stained-glass walls, best on a sunny morning.
  • Place des Vosges. The oldest planned square in Paris, in the Marais.
  • Tuileries Garden. Formal gardens running from the Louvre toward the Place de la Concorde.
  • Seine quays. Riverside promenades turned into public spaces, with summer events.
  • Musée Carnavalet. The museum of the history of Paris, in two Marais mansions, with a free permanent collection.
  • Musée de l'Orangerie. In a corner of the Tuileries, home to Monet's panoramic Water Lilies in two oval rooms.

A perfect day downtown

Morning. Start on the Île de la Cité: Notre-Dame as early as you can manage, then Sainte-Chapelle with a pre-booked slot while the morning light pours through the glass. Cross the footbridge to the Île Saint-Louis and walk its quiet quays, then follow the Right Bank into the Marais for the Place des Vosges and the free Musée Carnavalet.

Afternoon. Lunch on falafel from Rue des Rosiers or a set lunch in a bistro, then walk west past the Hôtel de Ville and Saint-Eustache to the Palais-Royal gardens and the arcades of Galerie Vivienne. Spend the late afternoon in the Louvre with a timed ticket, or choose the smaller Orangerie and a walk through the Tuileries.

Evening. Walk the Seine quays at sunset, when the stone glows and the bridges light up. Have an apéritif on a café terrace, then dinner around Rue Montorgueil or in the Haut-Marais. Many restaurants start dinner service at half past seven, so book ahead for Friday and Saturday.

Eat and drink

Paris Centre is a perfect place to graze. The Marais has bakeries, falafel counters, Jewish delis and natural-wine bars; Rue Montorgueil near Les Halles is a market street of fishmongers, cheese shops and pâtisseries. Classic cafés are for watching the street, not for speed. Many restaurants close between lunch and dinner, and some close in August.

Eat like a Parisian by making lunch your big meal: the formule, a set menu of two or three courses, is the best-value way to try a good bistro. The Marché des Enfants Rouges in the Haut-Marais, the oldest covered market in Paris, has food stalls for a casual lunch, and the streets around Rue Sainte-Anne near the Palais-Royal form a little Japanese quarter of noodle bars. Bakeries sell sandwiches and quiches for a cheap picnic in the Tuileries or on the quays. Coffee costs less at the counter than at a table, and you can ask for a free carafe of tap water with any meal.

Getting around

The Métro and RER converge at Châtelet–Les Halles. RER B runs to Charles de Gaulle, and Line 14 serves Orly. Vélib' bike share and expanding cycle lanes make biking practical. Line 1 runs east–west under Rue de Rivoli, Line 4 north–south across the islands, and the driverless Line 14 crosses the centre quickly toward Gare Saint-Lazare and Gare de Lyon. Buses are slower but scenic, and the Métro runs until around 1am on weeknights, later on Friday and Saturday nights.

Walking is often faster than the Métro for short trips: Notre-Dame to the Louvre is a pleasant stroll along the river. Cycle lanes now line Rue de Rivoli and the quays, but watch for pedestrians stepping into them. Driving is pointless for visitors. Much of the city is limited to 30 km/h, a limited-traffic zone restricts through traffic in Paris Centre, older cars need a Crit'Air emissions sticker, and parking is scarce. From either airport, taxis charge a fixed fare to central Paris, which makes them easy to budget for.

Where to stay

The Marais is the best all-round base: walkable, lively at night and full of places to eat, though rooms can be small and noise carries on weekend evenings. The streets around the Louvre and Palais-Royal suit a classic, elegant stay close to the big museums and the Tuileries. Les Halles is the most practical for transport, with direct RER trains to Charles de Gaulle, but the immediate area is busy and less charming. The Île Saint-Louis is quiet and romantic, with few options. The Sentier and the streets around Rue Montorgueil can be slightly better value and are excellent for food. The Left Bank's Latin Quarter and Saint-Germain are just across the river if you prefer that side.

Living downtown

Apartments are often in Haussmann-era buildings with small kitchens and lifts that hold two people. Rent control rules set reference rents in Paris. Expect to provide extensive documents for a lease. In the Marais and on the islands, older seventeenth- and eighteenth-century buildings mean beams, uneven floors and walk-up stairs, and former maids' rooms under the roofs are rented as tiny studios.

An unfurnished lease normally runs for three years and a furnished one for a year, renewable; a short "bail mobilité" covers stays of one to ten months for people in training or on assignment. Deposits are capped by law, at one month's rent for unfurnished homes and two for furnished ones. Landlords usually want payslips, a tax notice and a guarantor, and the free Visale guarantee scheme can help newcomers who lack one. Paris Centre suits people who prize walking and culture over space; families often head to the outer arrondissements or the inner suburbs for more room.

Doing business downtown

Luxury retail, fashion, tourism and professional services dominate the centre. The Marais is a hotspot for concept stores and pop-ups, and fashion weeks bring waves of buyers and press. French commercial leases typically run for long terms with renewal rights for the tenant, and taking over an existing lease (cession de bail) is a common way to secure a good storefront.

The city's main office district, known as the Quartier Central des Affaires, centres on the 8th and 9th arrondissements but reaches into the 1st and 2nd around the Bourse and Opéra, where banks, law firms and fund managers take floors in Haussmann buildings. The Sentier remains a base for startups and agencies, the Marais holds fashion showrooms and galleries, and La Défense, just outside the city limits, has the big modern towers. Company formalities now go through a single online portal run by INPI. Read more: Office and commercial space in downtown Paris.

Signature events

Late JuneFête de la Musique — Free music on streets across the city, held every year on the summer solstice.
Mid-JulyBastille Day — France's national day: parade, celebrations and fireworks across Paris.
July–AugustParis Plages — Seasonal beach-style spaces along the Seine.
Mid-SeptemberEuropean Heritage Days — Government buildings, ministries and private mansions usually closed to the public open their doors for a weekend.

Dates move from year to year. Check the organiser's official site before booking travel.

Seasons and weather

Spring is Paris at its most appealing, with chestnut trees in leaf and café terraces filling up, though April showers are real. Summer is warm and increasingly prone to heatwaves, and many older apartments and some hotels lack air conditioning; August is quieter as Parisians leave and some small businesses close. Autumn brings mild days, golden light along the Seine and the return of the cultural season. Winter is grey, damp and chilly, but rarely bitterly cold, and the museums are calmer. Pack layers, an umbrella and comfortable shoes that look smart enough for a nice restaurant.

Safety and practicalities

Paris Centre is generally safe, but pickpockets are skilled and busy around the Louvre, Notre-Dame, Châtelet–Les Halles and on Métro Line 1, so keep bags closed and in front of you. Ignore petition-signers and anyone offering string bracelets or "found" rings; they are distractions. Châtelet–Les Halles can feel chaotic late at night, so stay alert there. Most Métro stations have stairs rather than lifts, so travellers with mobility needs should rely on buses, the newer Line 14 and taxis. Shops and restaurants keep varied hours, and some small businesses close on Sundays and Mondays. Plugs are the two-pin European type.

Local tips

  • Visit the Louvre in the evening on late-opening days.
  • Walk the islands at dawn.
  • Learn a few French phrases; a 'bonjour' goes a long way.
  • Ask for "une carafe d'eau" for free tap water instead of buying bottled.
  • Order coffee at the counter if you want it fast and cheap.
  • Shop the Marais on a Sunday, when many boutiques open and nearby streets are calmer.
