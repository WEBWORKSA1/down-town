In 30 seconds

  • Best for: a frictionless global business base with excellent food
  • Eat at hawker centres — they are the city's culinary heart and remarkably affordable
  • Hot and humid all year; plan walks for early morning or after sunset
  • Covered walkways and underground links keep most of the core walkable, even in a downpour

Singapore's Downtown Core covers the Central Business District around Raffles Place, the newer towers of Marina Bay built on reclaimed land, and the colonial Civic District along the Singapore River. Chinatown's shophouses and temples sit just to the south-west, and the riverside quays curve inland toward Fort Canning Hill.

It is one of the most deliberately planned downtowns in the world: covered walkways for tropical downpours, generous parks, strict upkeep and a transit network that brings most of the island within an hour. The payoff is a business district that also works as an evening and weekend destination. The trade-off is cost — this is one of Asia's most expensive places to live, eat out at sit-down restaurants and drink.

Landmarks worth your time

  • Gardens by the Bay. Huge conservatories and the Supertree Grove, with a free light-and-sound show most evenings.
  • Marina Bay Sands SkyPark. An observation deck across the top of three hotel towers.
  • Merlion Park. The city's half-lion, half-fish symbol facing the bay.
  • National Gallery Singapore. Southeast Asian art inside the former Supreme Court and City Hall, with rooftop views over the Padang.
  • Lau Pa Sat. A Victorian cast-iron market hall whose side street becomes a satay strip at night.
  • Buddha Tooth Relic Temple. A large Tang-style temple and museum in Chinatown.
  • Esplanade – Theatres on the Bay. The spiky twin domes on the waterfront hold a concert hall and theatre, and free shows on the outdoor stage are a regular fixture.
  • Fort Canning Park. The green hill behind the Civic District, linked to early Malay kings and later a British military base, with heritage gardens and a wartime command bunker.

A perfect day downtown

Start before the heat with kaya toast, soft-boiled eggs and a strong kopi at a hawker centre or old-style coffee shop. Walk the Singapore River from Boat Quay, past the bronze sculptures that recall the river's trading days, to the Asian Civilisations Museum and the Civic District, then spend the late morning in the National Gallery.

Head to Chinatown for lunch at Maxwell Food Centre, then visit the Buddha Tooth Relic Temple and Sri Mariamman Temple, Singapore's oldest Hindu temple, a short walk apart on South Bridge Road. When the midday sun peaks, retreat into a museum or the air-conditioned underground links, then wander the shophouse rows of Duxton Hill and Club Street as the afternoon cools.

Cross to Marina Bay around sunset. Walk the promenade over the Helix Bridge, catch the free Supertree light show at Gardens by the Bay, then finish at Lau Pa Sat, where Boon Tat Street fills with satay grills after dark.

Eat and drink

Hawker centres are Singapore's great equaliser: office workers, families and food critics queue at the same stalls for chicken rice, laksa, char kway teow and satay. Within the CBD, Lau Pa Sat and the Maxwell and Amoy Street centres are lunchtime institutions. Tanjong Pagar and Keong Saik Road hold many of the city's most interesting restaurants and bars.

Learn the coffee-shop vocabulary: kopi comes with condensed milk, kopi-o is black with sugar, kopi-c uses evaporated milk, and "siew dai" means less sweet. Tables are shared, and a packet of tissues on a seat means someone has reserved it. Singapore's hawker culture was added to UNESCO's list of intangible cultural heritage in 2020, and some stalls have been recognised by international restaurant guides, so the cheapest meal in town can also be among the best. Alcohol is heavily taxed, so a round of drinks can cost more than dinner, and drinking in public places is banned late at night.

Getting around

The MRT network covers the island, and several lines intersect beneath the CBD and Marina Bay. Changi Airport is on the East–West Line with a change at Tanah Merah. Contactless bank cards work across trains and buses, and taxis and ride-hailing are widely used. Covered walkways link many towers to stations.

Driving makes little sense for visitors. Car ownership is deliberately expensive, Electronic Road Pricing gantries charge vehicles on the busiest roads at peak times, and CBD parking is costly. Walking is easier than the climate suggests: covered walkways, underground passages such as the link between City Hall and Esplanade stations, and air-conditioned malls let you cross much of the core out of the sun and rain. Shared bikes and cycling paths work well along the waterfront and the park connectors, though few people cycle through the busiest CBD streets. Trains stop running around midnight; after that, taxis and ride-hailing apps fill the gap.

Where to stay

Marina Bay suits business travellers and first-timers who want the skyline on the doorstep and an easy walk to the convention centres. The Civic District puts you between the museums, the river and several MRT lines. Chinatown and Tanjong Pagar have the most character, with restored shophouses converted into small hotels and hawker centres on the same street. Clarke Quay and Robertson Quay along the river are good for nightlife and for longer stays in serviced apartments, while Bugis and Kampong Glam tend to offer better value within walking distance of the core. Serious shoppers may prefer Orchard Road, a few MRT stops north.

Living downtown

Most foreign professionals rent private condominiums, which come with pools and security; public housing flats are largely for citizens and permanent residents. Rents near the CBD and Marina Bay are among Asia's highest, so many residents choose areas a few MRT stops away. Leases are commonly one or two years.

The security deposit is commonly one month's rent for each year of the lease, and tenants usually pay the stamp duty on the tenancy agreement. Expatriate tenants often negotiate a diplomatic clause, which allows them to end the lease early if they are transferred or leave the country, after a minimum period and with notice. Private homes cannot legally be let for stays shorter than three months, and estate agents must be registered with the Council for Estate Agencies. Downtown living suits professionals and couples who value a short walk to work; families often prefer quieter estates near the international schools further out.

Doing business downtown

Singapore is a global financial centre and a popular location for Asia-Pacific headquarters, with English as the business language, a competitive corporate tax regime and efficient company registration. Retail and food businesses face high rents but a dense, high-spending daytime population.

The office market has several cores. Raffles Place holds the established bank towers, Marina Bay the newest and largest floors, Shenton Way and Tanjong Pagar a mix of older blocks and redevelopments, and the Beach Road and Bugis fringe a central address at lower cost. The government also steers growth to regional hubs such as one-north and the Jurong Lake District. Companies register with the Accounting and Corporate Regulatory Authority, and foreign staff need work passes from the Ministry of Manpower. Orchard Road remains the main shopping belt, while downtown retail depends on station-linked malls, Chinatown shophouses and the food and bar streets of Telok Ayer and Amoy Street. Seasons and weather

Singapore sits just north of the equator, so it is hot and humid year-round, with daytime temperatures that rarely stray far from the low 30s Celsius and warm nights. The northeast monsoon brings the wettest weeks from December into early January, followed by a drier, breezier spell from February to April. From June to September the southwest monsoon brings early-morning squalls, and the inter-monsoon months around April–May and October–November are known for heavy afternoon thunderstorms. In some dry years, smoke haze from regional fires drifts over between roughly June and October. Pack light, breathable clothing, a compact umbrella and a light layer for the powerful air-conditioning in offices, malls and trains.

Safety and practicalities

Singapore has very low crime, and walking downtown at night is generally safe, but keep an eye on phones and bags in crowded spots such as Chinatown during festivals and the bay during big events. The bigger risks are environmental: heat exhaustion, and lightning, which is frequent here, so shelter during storms rather than staying out in open parks. Rules are strictly enforced and fines are real: no eating or drinking on the MRT, no littering, smoking only in designated areas, and e-cigarettes are illegal. Drug laws are among the harshest in the world. The MRT and buses are largely step-free, and newer buildings meet high accessibility standards. Tourist SIM cards and eSIM plans are easy to buy on arrival.

Local tips

  • Carry an umbrella — tropical showers arrive fast.
  • Order at hawker stalls by tapping a card or paying cash; tables are shared.
  • Respect strict local rules on littering, smoking areas and eating on public transport.
  • Use underground links and covered walkways between stations and towers to stay out of the sun and rain.
  • Bring a refillable bottle; tap water is safe to drink.
  • Book observation decks and conservatories ahead for weekends and school holidays.
