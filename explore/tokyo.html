In 30 seconds

  • Best for: safe, efficient city living and some of the world's best food at every price
  • Load a Suica or PASMO IC card (or add one to your phone) on day one
  • Cherry-blossom season (late March to early April) is the busiest and most beautiful time

Tokyo has no single downtown. Marunouchi and Otemachi, between Tokyo Station and the Imperial Palace moat, form the classic business district of bank headquarters and trading houses. Next door, Ginza is the polished shopping street of department stores and flagship boutiques. To the west, Shinjuku and Shibuya are the giant, neon-lit hubs where commuters, shoppers and nightlife collide.

What makes this multi-centre city work is rail. Trains arrive to the minute, stations are clean and safe at any hour, and an IC card in your phone pays for almost everything. That combination lets people live in quiet residential streets and reach any of the downtowns in half an hour. The flip side is scale: distances between the hubs are larger than they look on a map, and the busiest stations are mazes, so it pays to pick one or two areas per day rather than trying to see everything at once.

Landmarks worth your time

  • Imperial Palace East Gardens. Free gardens on the former castle grounds, a few minutes' walk from Tokyo Station; check closing days before you go.
  • Tokyo Station Marunouchi Building. The restored 1914 red-brick station façade, best seen from the plaza leading to the palace.
  • Ginza Chuo-dori. The main shopping avenue, which becomes a pedestrian street on weekend afternoons.
  • Tokyo Metropolitan Government Building. Free observation decks with views to Mount Fuji on clear winter days.
  • Shinjuku Gyoen. A large national garden mixing Japanese, English and French landscape styles.
  • Omoide Yokocho. A narrow lane of tiny grill bars beside Shinjuku Station.
  • Nihonbashi Bridge. A 1911 stone bridge with bronze lions and a kilometre-zero marker, currently overshadowed by an elevated expressway that a long-term project aims to move underground.
  • Meiji Jingu. A Shinto shrine in a deep, man-made forest beside Harajuku Station, a few minutes from Shibuya and a calm counterpoint to the crowds.

A perfect day downtown

Begin at Tokyo Station's Marunouchi side to see the restored façade, then walk the broad avenue to the palace moat and into the Imperial Palace East Gardens when they open. Afterwards, stroll through Marunouchi's tree-lined Naka-dori or head east to Nihonbashi to see the old bridge and the merchant district around it.

For lunch, choose a set meal in Ginza, where high-end kitchens offer lunch at a fraction of dinner prices, or graze through a department-store basement food hall. Spend the afternoon on Chuo-dori, which is pedestrianised on weekend afternoons, then rest in Hibiya Park. If you prefer markets, Tsukiji Outer Market is a short walk from Ginza and best visited before early afternoon.

At dusk, take the Marunouchi Line or JR to Shinjuku. Ride up the Tokyo Metropolitan Government Building for a free view of the lights, then cross to the east side for skewers in Omoide Yokocho or a nightcap in the tiny bars of Golden Gai. Keep an eye on the clock: the last trains leave around midnight.

Eat and drink

Tokyo rewards curiosity at every budget: standing soba counters, ramen shops with ticket machines, department-store basement food halls, izakaya pubs and some of the most acclaimed restaurants in the world. Convenience stores are a genuine option for breakfast. In Ginza and Marunouchi, set lunches at high-end restaurants cost a fraction of dinner.

The best-value eating clusters around stations and office districts. Set meals (teishoku) of grilled fish, rice, miso soup and pickles are a weekday staple; curry, tonkatsu and donburi rice bowls are quick and filling; and the under-the-tracks pubs of Yurakucho and Shimbashi fill with office workers after six. Many small places seat you at a counter, take orders by ticket machine or tablet, and expect you to leave promptly when you finish. Prices usually include tax, there is no tipping, and some evening izakaya add a small cover charge served as a starter dish. Seasonal menus matter: look for cherry-blossom sweets in spring, cold noodles in summer and chestnuts and matsutake in autumn.

Getting around

JR East's Yamanote loop line circles central Tokyo and connects every major hub, while Tokyo Metro and Toei subway lines cut through the middle. Haneda is reached by the Tokyo Monorail or Keikyu Line; Narita by the Narita Express or Keisei Skyliner. Avoid the morning rush with luggage, and note that most lines stop running around midnight.

An IC card covers JR, the subway, buses and many convenience-store purchases, and visitors can buy unlimited-ride subway passes for 24, 48 or 72 hours. Station signage and announcements are in English, and exits are numbered, so check your exit before you climb out. Walking between Marunouchi, Ginza and Nihonbashi is easy and often faster than changing trains. Taxis are clean and safe but expensive; their rear doors open and close automatically. Bike-share docks are spread across the central wards, but cycling on busy roads takes confidence. Driving is rarely worth it: parking is scarce and costly, and the rail network goes everywhere you need.

Where to stay

Marunouchi and the Tokyo Station area suit business travellers and anyone taking day trips by Shinkansen, with direct links to both airports. Ginza and Yurakucho put you among the shops and restaurants, and Nihonbashi offers a quieter, more traditional feel a short walk from Tokyo Station. Shinjuku is the most convenient base for nightlife and for the Narita Express, though its stations and streets are relentlessly busy. Shibuya suits younger travellers who want fashion and late nights. For better value, consider Ueno or Asakusa in the older east of the city, where the Keisei Skyliner from Narita arrives.

Living downtown

Apartments are compact but well designed, and many are close to a station — the key to everything here. Leasing often involves an upfront deposit, key money (a non-refundable gift to the landlord) and a guarantor company fee, so budget several months' rent to move in. Neighbourhoods one or two stops from the big hubs offer the best mix of quiet and convenience.

Listings describe layouts with codes such as 1K, 1LDK or 2LDK, which count rooms plus a living, dining and kitchen area, and sizes are given in square metres. Standard residential leases usually run two years, and many charge a renewal fee when you extend; fixed-term leases with no automatic renewal are increasingly common in newer buildings. Some landlords hesitate to rent to newcomers without a Japanese guarantor, which is why guarantor companies are standard, and furnished monthly apartments or share houses are a common first step. Once you move in, register your address at the ward office within 14 days. Central living suits professionals and couples who value short commutes; families often prefer the leafier residential wards to the west and south-west.

Doing business downtown

Marunouchi, Otemachi and Nihonbashi form the financial core, home to the stock exchange and major banks. Retail rents on Ginza's main streets are among the highest in the world, while Shibuya is the centre of the startup scene. Foreign founders usually work with a local administrative scrivener or accountant to register a company.

Beyond the core, Kasumigaseki holds the national ministries, and neighbouring Toranomon has grown into a cluster of new towers popular with foreign firms. Akasaka and Roppongi host media companies, embassies and international businesses, Shinagawa suits manufacturers with its Shinkansen station, and Nishi-Shinjuku's skyscrapers house insurers, technology firms and the metropolitan government. Retail is equally layered: Ginza and Omotesando for luxury flagships, Shinjuku and Shibuya for high-volume fashion and electronics, and station buildings everywhere for commuter trade. The Japan External Trade Organization (JETRO) offers support for companies setting up in Japan. For more on districts and leases, see Office and commercial space in downtown Tokyo.

Seasons and weather

Spring is mild and bright, peaking with cherry blossom in late March or early April, when parks and hotels fill up. The rainy season (tsuyu) usually runs from June into mid-July, with grey skies and frequent showers rather than constant downpours. High summer is hot and very humid, and heatstroke warnings are common; carry water, a hand towel and a folding umbrella for shade. Typhoons can bring storms from late summer into autumn. October and November are ideal, with clear skies and turning leaves. Winter is cool, dry and often sunny, with Mount Fuji visible on clear days; snow is occasional. Pack layers in spring and autumn, and a warm coat from December to February.

Safety and practicalities

Tokyo is one of the safest big cities in the world, and lost wallets are frequently handed in. Common-sense caution still applies in the nightlife districts of Kabukicho and Roppongi, where touts steer visitors into bars with inflated bills; agree prices before you sit down. Rush-hour trains are extremely crowded, and many lines run women-only cars in the morning peak. Japan is earthquake country: note the evacuation information in your accommodation, and consider the free English safety-alert app from Japan's national tourism organisation. Stations are well equipped with lifts and tactile paving, making Tokyo comparatively accessible. Connectivity is excellent via eSIM or rented pocket Wi-Fi, and convenience-store and post-office cash machines accept most foreign cards.

Local tips

  • Carry some cash; smaller restaurants and shrines may not take cards.
  • Stand on the left of escalators in Tokyo; railway operators now ask passengers not to walk on them at all.
  • Use department-store basement food halls for affordable, high-quality takeaway.
  • Public rubbish bins are rare, so carry a small bag for your litter.
  • Keep phone calls off the train and keep your voice low; quiet is the norm.
  • Send big suitcases ahead with a luggage-forwarding service rather than dragging them through rush hour.
