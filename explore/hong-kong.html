  • October to December has the clearest skies; summer brings heat, rain and typhoon warnings
  • The cheapest great experiences are the Star Ferry, the trams and a dim sum breakfast

Central is the business heart of Hong Kong Island, a narrow strip of reclaimed land where some of Asia's most famous towers face Victoria Harbour, with the steep green slopes of Victoria Peak rising behind them. Admiralty and Wan Chai extend the core east, Sheung Wan runs west, and Tsim Sha Tsui faces it all from the Kowloon side of the water.

Moving around is half the fun. Double-decker trams rattle along the north shore, the Star Ferry crosses to Kowloon in minutes, a funicular climbs to the Peak, and a covered escalator system carries commuters up the hillside to the Mid-Levels. Elevated walkways link offices, malls and stations so you can cross much of Central without touching the street. The density that makes it so efficient also makes it expensive and, in summer, relentlessly humid.

Landmarks worth your time

  • Victoria Peak. The classic view over the harbour, reached by the historic Peak Tram or by bus.
  • Star Ferry. A short, inexpensive crossing of Victoria Harbour that has run for more than a century.
  • Central–Mid-Levels Escalator. A long outdoor covered escalator system that runs downhill in the morning rush and uphill the rest of the day.
  • Tai Kwun. The former Central Police Station compound, restored as an arts and heritage complex.
  • Man Mo Temple. A 19th-century temple in Sheung Wan, filled with hanging incense coils.
  • Symphony of Lights. A nightly light-and-music show across the harbour skyline.
  • Hong Kong Park. A landscaped park in Admiralty with a walk-through aviary, a conservatory and the Flagstaff House Museum of Tea Ware, one of the oldest colonial buildings in the city.
  • M+. A museum of visual culture in the West Kowloon Cultural District, with a harbourside park and roof garden facing the Island skyline.

A perfect day downtown

Have dim sum early, when tea houses fill with older regulars and the steamed dishes come out freshest. Then ride the Peak Tram up Victoria Peak and walk the level Lugard Road loop, a shaded path around the summit with some of the best harbour views in the city.

Come down and ride the Central–Mid-Levels Escalator up to SoHo, stopping at Tai Kwun and at PMQ, the former police married quarters now filled with design studios. Walk west along Hollywood Road's antique dealers to Man Mo Temple, then drop down into Sheung Wan's dried-seafood and herbal-medicine streets. Catch a tram east and sit on the upper deck for the view.

Take the Star Ferry to Tsim Sha Tsui at dusk, walk the harbourfront promenade for the Symphony of Lights, then return to Central for dinner in SoHo, or head north to the Temple Street Night Market in Yau Ma Tei for claypot rice and street snacks.

Eat and drink

Dim sum for breakfast or lunch, roast goose and char siu, wonton noodles and milk tea in a cha chaan teng café are the essentials. Central and SoHo also hold dozens of internationally acclaimed restaurants, while Sheung Wan's side streets have newer independent spots.

To eat well cheaply, look for cha chaan teng set breakfasts and lunches, the cooked-food centres that sit above many municipal wet markets, and the few licensed open-air dai pai dong stalls that survive on Central's lanes. The Graham Street market is one of the oldest street markets on the island. Try egg tarts, pineapple buns, egg waffles and silky milk tea, and at dim sum tap two fingers on the table to thank whoever refills your tea. Central's office lunch rush peaks around 1pm, so eat a little earlier or later if you want a seat without a queue. Sharing tables with strangers is normal in busy places.

Getting around

The MTR is fast and extensive, and the Airport Express runs from Hong Kong Station to the airport in roughly 25 minutes, with in-town check-in for many airlines. Trams are the cheapest way to travel along the island's north shore. Ferries link Central with Kowloon and the outlying islands.

Central and Admiralty are the main interchanges: the Island Line runs along the north shore, the Tsuen Wan Line crosses to Kowloon, the South Island Line heads to Aberdeen, and the East Rail Line now crosses the harbour to Admiralty. Green minibuses run fixed routes up the hills where trains cannot. Taxis are plentiful and metered, and it helps to have your destination written in Chinese. Walking is often fastest in the core thanks to the elevated walkways, but the streets above Queen's Road climb steeply. Driving and parking are slow and expensive, and cycling on Hong Kong Island's streets is impractical; save bikes for the cycle tracks of the New Territories.

Where to stay

Central and Admiralty suit business travellers who want to walk to meetings and catch the Airport Express. Sheung Wan is a little quieter and better value, with galleries and cafés on its side streets. Wan Chai and Causeway Bay are livelier, handy for the convention centre and the shops, and well served by trams. Across the harbour, Tsim Sha Tsui offers the famous skyline view and the waterfront museums, often for less than the same standard of room in Central. For stays of a month or more, serviced apartments in the Mid-Levels and Sheung Wan are the usual choice.

Living downtown

Apartments are small and among the most expensive in the world, with the Mid-Levels and the western districts popular with professionals. Many buildings have clubhouses with gyms and pools to compensate for limited space. Leases usually run two years, with a break clause after one.

Expect to pay a deposit, commonly two months' rent, plus the first month in advance, and an agent's commission that is typically split between landlord and tenant. Stamp duty on the lease is usually shared by both sides too. Check whether the rent includes management fees and government rates, and ask for the saleable area — the floor space you can actually use — because advertised gross figures include a share of lifts, lobbies and corridors. Downtown suits professionals happy to trade space for a short commute; families often look to the south side of the island or to quieter districts further out.

Doing business downtown

Hong Kong is one of the world's leading financial centres, home to a major stock exchange in Central and a simple, low-rate tax system. Central's office and retail rents are very high; Sheung Wan and Wan Chai offer smaller, more affordable spaces for independents.

Grade A towers in Central and Admiralty house banks, law firms, insurers and the regional offices of mainland Chinese and international companies. Quarry Bay on the island's east side and Kowloon East, a former industrial area the government is promoting as a second business district, offer larger floors for less. Companies incorporate through the Companies Registry and take out business registration with the Inland Revenue Department. Retail in Central and Causeway Bay ranks among the most expensive street frontage in the world, while Tsim Sha Tsui depends heavily on visitors, so sales there swing with tourism. Dates move from year to year. Check the organiser's official site before booking travel.

Seasons and weather

Spring (March to May) is warm and increasingly humid, with fog and drizzle that can hide the Peak for days. Summer (June to September) is hot, sticky and wet, and it is the heart of typhoon season. The Hong Kong Observatory's tropical cyclone signals run from No. 1 (standby) to No. 8 and above, when most offices and schools close and many ferries stop; rainstorm warnings are colour-coded amber, red and black. Autumn, from October to early December, is the best time to visit: dry, sunny and clear. Winter (December to February) is cool and sometimes grey, and because many homes and older buildings lack heating, a warm layer is useful. In summer, pack light clothes and an umbrella, plus a cardigan for fiercely air-conditioned malls and offices.

Safety and practicalities

Hong Kong is one of the safer big cities in the world, and walking around Central late at night is normal. Pickpockets do work crowded markets, ferry piers and packed MTR carriages, so keep phones in front pockets. Lan Kwai Fong gets very crowded on weekend nights and holidays, and police sometimes manage crowd flows on its steep streets. Most MTR lines run until around 1am; after that, overnight buses and taxis take over. Heat and humidity are the main health risk, especially on the hiking trails above the city, which are steeper than they look, so carry water. The MTR is largely step-free, but stairs, slopes and the trams make older districts hard work with a wheelchair or pram. Mobile coverage is excellent, including underground.

Local tips

  • Take the Star Ferry after dark for the skyline at its best.
  • Ride the trams' upper deck for a cheap city tour.
  • Go up the Peak on a clear weekday morning to avoid queues.
  • Keep an Octopus card or phone wallet handy; it works on almost everything that moves.
  • In summer, check the Observatory's warnings and have an indoor plan for typhoon days.
  • Use in-town check-in at Hong Kong Station to drop your bags hours before your flight.
